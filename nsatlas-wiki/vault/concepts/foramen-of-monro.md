# Foramen of Monro

The foramen of Monro — also called the **interventricular foramen** — is the paired Y-shaped aperture that connects each lateral ventricle with the roof of the third ventricle. Named for the Scottish anatomist Alexander Monro secundus (1783), it is one of the most surgically significant landmarks in intraventricular neurosurgery: colloid cyst surgery, endoscopic third ventriculostomy planning, septostomy for isolated ventricle, and transcallosal/transcortical corridors all converge on it.

## Anatomic Boundaries

| Border | Structure |
|--------|-----------|
| Anterior | Column of fornix |
| Posterior | Anterior pole of thalamus (superior thalamic tubercle) and thalamostriate vein |
| Medial | Septum pellucidum (roof of third ventricle) |
| Lateral | Body of caudate nucleus / thalamus junction |
| Floor | Roof of third ventricle (chorioid plexus of third ventricle and velum interpositum) |

Each foramen is roughly ovoid, measuring 4–6 mm in normal adults and enlarging dramatically in hydrocephalus. The two foramina converge anteriorly — their anterior margins are formed by the columns of the fornix that descend into the mammillary bodies.

## Structures Passing Through

- **Choroid plexus** — continuous between lateral and third ventricle via the foramen; the glomus of the lateral ventricle plexus attenuates at the foramen.
- **Anterior septal vein** — runs along the septum pellucidum and joins the thalamostriate vein at the **venous angle** just behind the foramen, forming the internal cerebral vein.
- **Thalamostriate vein** — runs in the stria terminalis along the thalamus-caudate sulcus.
- **Internal cerebral vein** — formed at the posterior margin of the foramen; runs in the velum interpositum.

The **venous angle** (junction of anterior septal and thalamostriate veins) is the angiographic landmark indicating the position of the foramen and is used for ventriculography and stereotactic targeting.

## Surgical Relevance

### Transcallosal Approach

- Direct midline corridor through an anterior callosotomy into the body of the lateral ventricle.
- The **choroid plexus leads the surgeon to the foramen** — reliable internal GPS.
- Enlargement for wider access uses the **transchoroidal** or **subchoroidal** variant (incising the taenia choroidea) without splitting the fornix or septum pellucidum.

### Endoscopic Approach

- Kocher point entry — frontal horn → body → foramen → third ventricle.
- Standard corridor for **endoscopic third ventriculostomy**, biopsy of intraventricular tumors, colloid cyst resection, and septostomy.

### Transcortical Approach

- Middle frontal gyrus entry into the frontal horn → posteriorly to the foramen.
- Simpler but risks white-matter injury; less used in modern practice compared to transcallosal.

## Common Pathologies Centered on the Foramen

- **Colloid cyst of the third ventricle** — arises in the roof of the third ventricle just below the foramen; classic cause of intermittent obstructive hydrocephalus, position-dependent headache, and sudden death.
- **Subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA)** — in tuberous sclerosis; arises near the foramen and obstructs CSF flow; mTOR inhibitors (everolimus) and resection are mainstays.
- **Central neurocytoma** — attached to the septum pellucidum, straddling the foramen.
- **Aqueductal stenosis** — downstream obstruction; foramen dilates, leading to triventricular hydrocephalus.
- **Isolated lateral ventricle** — unilateral foramen obstruction produces monoventricular hydrocephalus; treated with septostomy or VP shunt.

## Endoscopic Landmarks at the Foramen

On entering the lateral ventricle with a rigid endoscope from Kocher point, the surgeon identifies:

1. **Choroid plexus** — leads anteriorly to the foramen.
2. **Septal vein** — along the medial wall.
3. **Thalamostriate vein** — along the lateral wall.
4. **Foramen** — beneath the converging veins.
5. **Third-ventricle floor** (through the foramen) — mammillary bodies, infundibular recess, tuber cinereum — target for ETV fenestration between mammillary bodies and infundibulum.

## Pitfalls

- **Fornix injury** — produces memory deficits (especially bilateral); the superior and medial margins of the foramen must be respected.
- **Thalamostriate vein injury** — sacrifice causes venous infarction of thalamus and basal ganglia; never cut to gain exposure.
- **Basilar artery injury during ETV** — perforate in the midline between mammillary bodies; do not aim posteriorly.
- **Colloid cyst sudden death** — emergency if diagnosed in symptomatic patient; urgent surgery warranted.
