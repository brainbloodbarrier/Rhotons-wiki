# Patient Positioning

*Principles of Cranial Surgery — Patient Positioning*
*DOI: 10.18791/nsatlas.v2.08*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol

Patient positioning is integral to operative planning — it prevents pressure-related injuries, expands the operative corridor through **gravity retraction**, and minimizes surgeon fatigue. Small openings to deep fields demand ergonomic positioning; small openings to deep targets are unforgiving of poor setup.

## The Three Requirements of a Good Position

A good operative position should:

1. Allow the **least obstructed intradural trajectory** to the target while minimizing risk to normal neurovascular structures
2. Keep the patient as close to **physiologic body position** as possible — avoiding pressure, stretch, and peripheral nerve injury
3. Enable the surgeon to maintain **ergonomic body posture** for relaxed microsurgical maneuvers

## Core Principles

- **Torso is positioned before the head** — once the skull clamp is locked, only Trendelenburg/reverse-Trendelenburg and tilts remain
- **Ideally the lesion is the highest point** — but for deep-seated lesions, **gravity retraction** of the overlying brain takes priority
- **Skull perimeter of craniotomy parallel to the floor** when possible
- **Skull clamp for all microsurgical procedures**; horseshoe/doughnut only for superficial tumor resection and hematoma evacuation
- **Never use rigid protocols** — decision-making is multifactorial (lesion location, body habitus, surgeon ergonomics, airway)

## Cohen-Gadol's Two-Position Algorithm

~90% of cases use **two positions**:

| Lesion location | Position |
|---|---|
| Nose → parietal eminence | **Supine** |
| Parietal eminence → occipitocervical junction | **Modified park-bench (lateral oblique)** |

Remaining ~10%:

- **Sitting** — pineal region in morbidly obese
- **Prone** — emergency posterior fossa (faster to set up than park-bench)

## Supine Position

Used for: endoscopic transnasal, pterional, frontal, temporal, interhemispheric, anterior parietal, and anterior/middle skull base osteotomies.

### Setup

- **"Back up, reflexed, leg down"** — back raised, body reflexed, knees flexed — prevents downward slippage, avoids knee-nerve tension
- Foam cushions, gel pads, pillows, padded armrests

### Head turn algorithm (pterional)

| Target | Head turn |
|---|---|
| Midline (AComA aneurysm, tuberculum sellae) | **30°** |
| Off-midline (MCA aneurysm, lateral sphenoid wing) | **45°** |
| Malar eminence | **highest point on head** |

**Rule:** The closer to midline and more anterior the lesion, the less the head is turned. Avoid excessive extension — complicates anterior skull base trajectory.

### Semi-lateral modification

For non-obese patients: **bulky shoulder roll** under ipsilateral shoulder rotates head aggressively without neck torsion — accesses posterior parietal region without compromising venous drainage.

### Awake craniotomy

- Supine with most **physiologic neck posture** possible
- Patient confirms comfortable swallowing **before** finalization
- See [[skull-clamp-placement]] (80-lb ceiling for agitation resistance)

## Modified Park-Bench (Lateral Oblique)

**Preferred for all posterior fossa, posterior parietal, occipital, posterior skull base, craniocervical junction.**

### Why park-bench over prone

- Patient is **physiologic**
- Surgeon can **sit** during microsurgical portion
- Gravity retracts dependent hemisphere for interhemispheric corridors

### Setup

- **Armored endotracheal tube** to avoid kinking with neck flexion
- **Thyromental distance** ≥ 2 fingers between chin and chest
- **Axillary roll** required; alternative: down-side arm extended over edge in sling
- **Two pillows** support upper arm
- **Head and thorax elevated ~15°** for venous return
- **Upper shoulder retracted caudally** with adhesive tape, allowed to fall forward — **essential** for suboccipital access in short necks
- **Peroneal nerve** at fibular head — not compressed by tape
- **Lumbar drain** placement readily possible — see [[lumbar-drain]]

### Head rotation for retromastoid ([[retromastoid-craniotomy]])

- **~20° vertex toward floor**, adjusted intraop by table tilt
- Facilitates "going around" the cerebellum to CPA
- Medial (brainstem) tumor extension → less head turn
- For paramedian/retromastoid in short necks: tilt vertex toward floor to **open shoulder-neck angle**

### Pitfalls

- **Obese patients** at risk of migration/displacement — strap aggressively, pad meticulously
- **Learning moment** (Cohen-Gadol): in an obese patient, the **shoulder was not mobilized** effectively — the shoulder obstructed the supracerebellar transtentorial trajectory to a petrous apex meningioma

## Sitting Position

### Advantages

- Dry, clear operative field (gravity clears blood)
- Gravity-assisted cerebellar retraction
- Atraumatic bimanual dissection around facial nerve (acoustic neuroma)
- Less suctioning around brainstem/CNs — intermittent irrigation suffices
- Useful in **morbidly obese** with compromised venous drainage in prone/park-bench

### Drawbacks

- **Venous air embolism** — potentially life-threatening
- Rare: subdural hematoma, brachial plexus injury, cervical quadriplegia, sciatic injury
- Surgeon hand fatigue, unsteady arm positions
- Long setup times
- Requires **transesophageal echocardiography + transthoracic Doppler** monitoring
- Head-of-table serves as armrest

Cohen-Gadol uses sitting position only in highly selected cases (e.g., pineal region in morbid obesity).

## Prone Position

- Superficial posterior lesions
- Emergency posterior fossa (faster than park-bench)
- **Flexed torso and lower extremities** — avoid stretch injury
- **Venous return compromised** by increased intra-abdominal pressure
- Large gel pads protect chest
- Female breasts/nipples mobilized medially; male genitalia hang freely

## Pressure-Point Protection

Avoid prolonged pressure on:

- **Radial nerve** in spiral groove of humerus
- **Ulnar nerve** at epicondylar groove
- **Peroneal nerve** at fibular head (check tape placement!)

All bony prominences padded; arms and legs in neutral / slightly flexed positions.

## Avoid Semisitting

Cohen-Gadol **avoids semisitting** positions with acutely flexed neck — increases air embolism risk and forces uncomfortable surgeon arm posture. For posterior parietal craniotomies, he prefers **park-bench** over semisitting-supine.

## Pediatric

- **<3 years** — no pins; cerebellar headrest
- **<10 years** — pediatric pins
- **≥10 years** — adult pins
- See [[skull-clamp-placement]]

## Surgeon Ergonomics

Under-recognized factor in outcome:

- **Armrest** during supracerebellar craniotomy in park-bench position — prevents hand tremor
- Awkward arm posture → loss of composure and patience in critical late parts of long cases (skull base, AVM)
- Assistants and techs must see the microscope video monitor

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- Least-obstructed trajectory + physiologic body + ergonomic surgeon posture = correct position
- The **modified park-bench** is versatile, seated-friendly, and obviates most prone cases
- **Exploit gravity retraction** — the "quiet revolution" of retractorless surgery depends on it
- **Surgeon comfort matters** — long operations amplify small positioning errors into tremor
- **Never use rigid positioning protocols** — decide from lesion + body + ergonomics each time
