# Scalp Incisions

*Principles of Cranial Surgery — Scalp Incisions*
*DOI: 10.18791/nsatlas.v2.10*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol

A clear understanding of wound healing, scalp vascular anatomy, atraumatic tissue handling, closure, and reconstruction techniques is necessary for planning scalp incisions. The first incision is the most important factor in minimizing wound complications — poor planning in the first operation constrains every reoperation that follows.

## Wound Healing

Wound healing proceeds through three overlapping phases:

1. **Inflammatory** — polymorphonuclear leukocytes migrate first, followed by macrophages and mononuclear leukocytes
2. **Proliferative** — fibroblasts migrate for ~4-5 days and deposit disorganized collagen; epidermal basal cells replenish the protective layer within 48 hours
3. **Maturation** — collagen remodels over months

### Tensile strength recovery

| Time after closure | Tensile strength |
|---|---|
| 3 weeks | ~20% |
| 6 weeks | 60-70% |
| 1 year | 80% |

Radiation inhibits fibroblast migration and collagen deposition, so irradiated wounds heal slowly and dehisce easily.

### Technical implications

- Cut skin **perpendicular** to its surface; avoid oblique incisions and scything of adjacent dermis
- During closure, the suture needle enters **perpendicular** to skin surface; sutures at equal distance and depth from each edge
- "Unequal bites" invert edges, disrupt epithelialization, and produce cosmetically poor scars
- Gentle tension approximates edges without strangulating tissue — excess tension causes ischemia

## Planning the Incision

### Surface landmarks for underlying cortex

- A line from the **lateral canthus** to the point 3/4 of the way posterior along the nasion-to-inion arc approximates the **Sylvian fissure**
- A **45° line from the external auditory meatus (EAM)** marks the central sulcus
- The **motor cortex** sits 4-5.5 cm behind the coronal suture; its superior aspect lies nearly directly above the EAM near the midline
- The central sulcus is 2-3 cm posterior to the midpoint of the nasion-inion arc
- The **angular gyrus** is just above the pinna (with significant individual variability)

### Scalp neurovascular anatomy

Vasculature is derived from both internal and external carotid systems:

- **Supraorbital** and **supratrochlear** arteries (ICA via ophthalmic)
- **Superficial temporal artery** (ECA) — frontal and parietal branches
- **Posterior auricular** and **occipital** arteries (ECA)

Sensory innervation: supraorbital, supratrochlear, zygomaticotemporal, auriculotemporal, lesser occipital, greater occipital, and third occipital nerves.

**Key principle:** The vascular supply becomes more tenuous toward the midline. Therefore the **base of the flap should sit at the periphery**, not at the vertex. The vascular network is robust enough that narrow pedicles usually survive.

### The four requirements of a good incision

A well-planned incision should:

1. Provide ample vascular support — **width of the flap wider than its height**
2. Mobilize a flap that minimally interferes with the intradural operative trajectory
3. Anticipate **salvage opportunities** for wound breakdown or infection
4. Appear cosmetically acceptable

### Linear vs curvilinear vs U-shaped

- **Linear incisions** parallel to major feeding arteries (STA, occipital) transect the fewest branches and offer the most salvage options. They provide the most flexibility for extending the incision in reoperation — **the initial linear incision is the safest bet** for tumors likely to recur
- **Curvilinear incisions** (e.g., retromastoid, pterional) preserve vascular pedicles while giving curved exposure
- **U-shaped incisions** are slower, more time-consuming, and leave narrow pedicles — avoid unless required

## Hair Removal

There is no Class I evidence supporting complete head shaving. Cohen-Gadol **clips** a strip of hair around the incision rather than shaving.

| Approach | Incision |
|---|---|
| [[pterional-craniotomy\|Pterional]] | Curvilinear, begins 1 cm anterior to tragus; preserves STA main trunk and frontal branch |
| [[bifrontal-craniotomy\|Bifrontal]] | Bicoronal behind hairline |
| [[supraorbital-craniotomy\|Supraorbital keyhole]] | Eyebrow incision |
| [[retromastoid-craniotomy\|Retromastoid]] | Curvilinear behind ear, preserves occipital artery; wider base for large CPA tumors |
| Midline suboccipital | Vertical midline |
| Paramedian / hockeystick | Vertical with lateral curve toward mastoid — for transcondylar, jugular foramen, supracerebellar corridors |
| Combined subtemporal + retromastoid | Extended curved incision, junction at risk due to poor pedicle |

## Reoperation Considerations

- Malignant tumors undergo radiation + chemotherapy — wound is at risk of dehiscence and infection
- Benign tumors may recur, requiring reoperation along or just beyond the previous cavity edge
- **Linear incisions** give the most flexibility to extend anteriorly, posteriorly, or as a T-extension
- Previous **perpendicular incisions** create ischemic junctions — use only the horizontally-oriented limb for reoperation to maximize vascular support
- For recurrent posterior tumors, the linear incision can be extended posteriorly as a **U-shaped flap with a broad base**

## Closure

Cohen-Gadol's preferred closure:

- **Galea**: Vicryl 3-0 multifilament braided — absorbed by hydrolysis in 50-70 days
- **Skin**: clear Monocryl 3-0 synthetic absorbable, simple continuous (running) — half-life 7-14 days, no staple removal, ideal for pediatric cases
- **Infected/traumatic wounds**: monofilament sutures for both galea and skin (avoid multifilament)

## Special Situations

- **Embolized scalp vessels** (meningiomas, superficial AVFs) — compromised vascular support; involve plastic surgery
- **Prior radiation** — plan vascularized pedicles; consider alternative incisions
- **Absent STA** — avoid reverse U-shaped incision for subtemporal craniotomy; use linear alternative that preserves supraorbital/supratrochlear and posterior auricular/occipital supply
- **Incision over hardware** — avoid placing the incision directly over osteotomies and plates; risk of breakdown over bone flap and foreign body

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- Linear incisions heal more effectively and provide the most flexibility for reoperation
- Base of flap wider than height; spare major scalp arteries
- Gentle suture tension — over-tight sutures strangulate the wound edge and cause ischemic necrosis
- The vascular supply is more tenuous toward the midline — keep the flap base lateral
- **DANGER:** Perpendicular incisions from prior surgeries create an avascular junction; a third operation should re-use only one limb
- Harvey Cushing's meticulous Halstedian wound handling is the reason neurosurgery survived its infancy — skip it at your peril
