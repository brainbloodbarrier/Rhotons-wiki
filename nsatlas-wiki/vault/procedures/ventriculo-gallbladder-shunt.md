# Ventriculo-Gallbladder Shunt

*CSF Diversion Procedures — Ventriculo-Gallbladder Shunt*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol. Contributor: Daniel Fulkerson, MD.

## History and Rationale

Ventriculo-biliary diversion was first described by Smith, Moretz, and Pritchard in 1958 as an alternative to VP and VA shunting. The gallbladder is biologically attractive as a distal terminus for three reasons:

- **Bile is sterile** in a normal gallbladder, limiting ascending infection risk when the biliary tree is intact.
- **Intestinal absorption** of CSF electrolytes is efficient once bile and CSF are released into the duodenum via cholecystokinin-driven emptying.
- **Biliary lytic activity** may blunt the fibrotic scarring and protein precipitation that obstruct peritoneal and atrial distal catheters.

Despite this rationale, clinical experience is limited and complication rates remain high. VGB shunting is therefore a **salvage procedure** for patients with complex hydrocephalus who have failed standard distal sites (see [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]], [[ventriculo-atrial-shunt]], [[ventriculo-pleural-shunt]]). Typical candidates include premature neonates with prior necrotizing enterocolitis, patients with congenital diaphragmatic hernia or spina bifida, and patients with tuberculous adhesive peritonitis precluding peritoneal absorption.

## Indications

- **Last-resort** distal terminus when peritoneum, atrium, and pleura are exhausted or contraindicated
- Specifically useful in patients with:
  - Severe peritoneal adhesions / pseudocysts / malabsorption
  - Right-heart contraindications to VA shunt
  - Pleural insufficiency or recurrent hydrothorax with VPl
- Not applicable in:
  - Prior cholecystectomy
  - Active cholelithiasis / chronic cholecystitis
  - Biliary dyskinesia

## Preoperative Planning

- **Right upper quadrant ultrasound / CT** — confirm gallbladder presence, exclude stones and wall thickening
- General surgery consult — joint procedure typical
- Hepatic / pancreatic workup — hepatic or pancreatic disease disqualifies; cholestatic jaundice is common in infants on long-term TPN and warrants extended gallbladder-function evaluation
- Cover infection risk discussion — bile does not inactivate CSF antibiotic prophylaxis as peritoneum does

### Valve Selection (physics-driven)

Unlike VP and VA shunts, where distal resistance is assumed to be zero, the biliary tree adds an **intrinsic pressure of approximately 10–20 cm H₂O** to the distal side of the valve. This must be compensated for to prevent functional under-drainage:

- Use a **low-pressure** fixed-pressure valve, or
- Use a **programmable valve** set to a low opening pressure
- A **one-way flow valve** (anti-siphon / anti-reflux) is mandatory to prevent biliary reflux up the catheter

### Antibiotic Prophylaxis

- Follow the [Hydrocephalus Clinical Research Network (HCRN) protocol](https://doi.org/10.3171/2015.8.PEDS15253) for standard shunt infection prevention
- Coverage for Gram-negative organisms (gentamicin or a third-generation cephalosporin) is frequently added because of theoretical enteric exposure
- **However**, the organism most commonly cultured from infected VGB shunts is **Staphylococcus epidermidis** — the same profile as VP shunt infection, indicating a skin-flora etiology rather than enteric contamination

## Surgical Technique

### Positioning

- Supine, 15° left tilt to shift bowel away from right upper quadrant
- Shoulder bump aligns mastoid–clavicle for tunneling
- Cranial and abdominal fields draped as a continuous column

### Proximal Catheter

Same as VP — Kocher or Frazier point (see [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]], [[external-ventricular-drain-placement]]).

### Distal Catheter Assembly (before gallbladder insertion)

Before the distal segment enters the abdomen, the catheter is modified so it can be anchored to the gallbladder wall without lumen collapse:

1. Attach a **metallic straight connector** to the distal end of the standard catheter
2. Connect a short **2-cm section of catheter** onto the downstream side of the connector
3. Secure with **nonabsorbable suture** at both ends of the connector

The metallic connector is critical — it keeps the lumen patent when the purse-string sutures are tightened around it. The 2-cm distal stub is what will sit inside the gallbladder lumen.

### Gallbladder Access — Open Mini-Cholecystotomy

1. Right subcostal or upper midline mini-laparotomy by general surgeon
2. Identify the gallbladder **dome** (fundus) through the peritoneum
3. Place **two concentric purse-string sutures** (nonabsorbable 3.0) on the dome, one inside the other
4. Aspirate bile sample for culture
5. Make a small puncture at the center of the inner purse-string, just large enough for the 2-cm distal segment
6. Insert the 2-cm catheter segment into the gallbladder lumen
7. Tighten the **inner purse-string** around the metallic connector — lumen stays patent due to the connector
8. Tighten the **outer purse-string** to invert the gallbladder wall over the first — creates a watertight, imbricated seal
9. Coil excess catheter loosely over the liver

### Laparoscopic Variant

- 3–4 port standard cholecystectomy setup
- Grasp fundus, secure with endoloop after catheter insertion
- Preferred by general surgeons comfortable with advanced laparoscopy

## Tunneling, Assembly, Flush

- Head → abdomen tunneling (above clavicle at neck)
- Valve + Ringer's flush
- Intrathecal vancomycin 10 mg + gentamicin 4 mg via valve tap
- See [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]] for shared assembly protocol

## Postoperative Considerations

- Immediate abdominal imaging (ultrasound or CT) to confirm catheter intra-gallbladder
- NPO overnight, advance diet as tolerated
- Monitor for:
  - **Cholecystitis** — RUQ pain, fever, leukocytosis → urgent shunt externalization + cholecystectomy
  - **Biliary leak** — bilious drainage, peritonitis
  - **Catheter migration** out of gallbladder — reflux of bile into catheter / obstruction
  - **Shunt obstruction** from biliary sludge — protein precipitation on catheter tip
  - **Pulmonary edema** — salvage-candidate patients carry heavy comorbidity; watch closely

## Complications (outcomes data)

- **Overall failure rate is higher than VP and VA shunts** — exact figure poorly characterized due to small series
- **Infection / removal rate: 36%** in the largest published series (9 of 25 shunts eventually removed — West et al, *J Pediatr Surg* 1987)
- Most common infecting organism: **Staphylococcus epidermidis** (identical to VP shunt profile) — one documented enteric fistula case
- **Gallbladder atony / cholestasis** — secondary to surgical stress, sphincter-of-Oddi spasm, or perioperative narcotics. Treat with intravenous cholecystokinin
- **Bile reflux meningitis / ventriculitis** — reported when anti-reflux valve fails or fractures
- **Distal tubing fracture** — causes bile peritonitis via reflux into peritoneal cavity
- **Gallstone disease developing on the shunt** — catastrophic; requires conversion

## Revision and Removal

The distal catheter is physically anchored to the gallbladder wall by the purse-string sutures. **Never apply traction** — it will tear the gallbladder.

Technique:

1. General surgery exposes the distal catheter at the gallbladder wall
2. Tie off the catheter **just outside** the gallbladder wall with nonabsorbable suture
3. Cut the catheter proximal to the tie
4. Remove the proximal segment — a short remnant is left embedded in the purse-string complex
5. If the shunt is failing due to cholelithiasis or cholecystitis, convert to cholecystectomy and choose an alternate distal terminus

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- Rule out gallstone disease first — stones on a shunted gallbladder means disaster
- Insist on general-surgery partnership for fundus access and layered closure
- Watertight purse-string + serosal imbrication — bile leakage causes peritonitis
- Counsel: any RUQ pain → immediate evaluation; this is not a tolerable symptom
- If obstruction recurs from sludge, consider conversion back to a higher-flow site
- Long-term data limited; reserve for salvage
