# Spinal Cord AVM Surgery

*Spinal Cord Surgery — Spinal Cord AVM*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol

## Classification (Spetzler-Detwiler)

| Type | Description | Treatability |
|---|---|---|
| I | Dural AVF | See [[spinal-av-fistula-disconnection]] |
| II | **Glomus intramedullary AVM** — compact nidus | Surgical (sometimes staged embo) |
| III | **Juvenile / metameric** — diffuse, large, cord + paraspinal | Poor; palliative / partial embo |
| IV | **Perimedullary AVF** — pial surface fistula | Surgical; sub-classified IVa/b/c by size |
| V | Conus AVM | Multimodal (cauda equina involved) |

## Epidemiology and Natural History

- **Age at presentation** typically 15–40 y (third decade peak); no gender predilection. Spinal dural AV fistulas affect older patients — age is a reliable discriminator.
- **Location**: thoracic cord in > 50%, cervical ~ 30%, conus the remainder.
- **Congenital origin** favored (young age, associated vascular anomalies, uniform cord distribution).
- **Associated aneurysms** in up to 48% (feeding-artery or intranidal) — doubles hemorrhage risk.
- **Other CNS vascular anomalies** co-occur in 13–37%.
- **Annual hemorrhage risk ≈ 4%** overall; **≈ 10%** after a prior bleed.
- **Partial surgical treatment still carries ≈ 3% annual bleed risk** — subtotal treatment is not benign but is better tolerated in the cord than in brain AVMs.

## Presentation

Distinctly bimodal — acute catastrophe vs slow decline:

- **Acute deterioration (35–86%)**: subarachnoid or intramedullary hemorrhage → ictal back pain ("*coup de poignard of Michon*"), meningismus, suboccipital pain, sudden motor loss, LOC. **Children: 84% acute onset, 59% sudden motor impairment** — hemorrhage dominates pediatric presentations.
- **Chronic progressive myelopathy** from venous hypertension, vascular steal, or mass effect from an aneurysm: paresis/paralysis 65–75%, paresthesias 40–60%, pain 10–51%, bladder/bowel 15–42%. **Unlike spinal dural AVF, symptoms do NOT vary with exercise or posture.**
- **Conus AVMs** present with radicular symptoms (~60%) and higher sphincter dysfunction.

Pathophysiologic mechanisms (all four in play): venous hypertension, steal, hemorrhage (SAH or intraparenchymal), mass effect.

Rare but high-value physical signs: spinal bruit and cutaneous angioma.

## Preoperative Evaluation

- **MRI first** — focal cord enlargement, serpiginous flow voids on T1/T2, subacute blood as high T1 signal, cord edema, globular flow voids suggesting aneurysms/varices. **Presence of a nidus distinguishes AVM from perimedullary AVF.**
- **MRA / CTA** can localize the fistulous connection but **do not supplant digital subtraction spinal angiography** for pre-op planning.
- **DSA spinal angiography** — defines all feeders, the nidus, the draining venous system, associated aneurysms, and venous stenoses. Mandatory before any operative plan.
- Functional baseline: ASIA, **Aminoff-Logue disability scale** (primary scoring system for spinal vascular malformations).
- **Treatment triage axes:** angioarchitecture, extent of cord involvement, local surgical + endovascular expertise, risk of natural history vs risk of intervention.
- Surgery after hemorrhage is **delayed** to permit clot lysis/resorption before cord manipulation.

### Features predicting higher morbidity regardless of modality

- Large size
- Thoracolumbar location
- **Ventral position** within the cord
- Complex blood supply, multiple anterior-spinal-artery feeders
- Prenidal aneurysms
- Large venous varices

## Treatment Strategy by Type

### Type II (Glomus Intramedullary)

- Pre-op embolization of easily accessed feeders to reduce intraoperative bleeding
- Surgical resection: posterior midline laminectomy at level
- Midline myelotomy (see [[operative-spinal-cord-anatomy]])
- Identify nidus; circumferential dissection in gliotic plane
- Devascularize systematically — feeders first, draining vein last
- Complete resection curative; subtotal = continued risk

### Type III (Juvenile / Metameric)

- Usually **inoperable** — diffuse, no plane
- Palliative embolization of high-risk feeders
- Symptom control, not cure
- Counsel families honestly

### Type IV (Perimedullary AVF)

- **IVa** (small pial feeder): microsurgical clipping/disconnection at the surface
- **IVb** (medium): embolization ± surgical disconnection
- **IVc** (giant, multiple feeders): combined approach, often staged
- Unlike Type I, the shunt is **intradural at the cord surface**

### Type V (Conus)

- Multimodal; cauda equina involvement limits aggressive resection
- Preserve root function

## Positioning and Exposure

- Prone, neutral head in Mayfield for cervical levels
- Laminectomy at level + one above/below
- Level marked intraop with fluoro + ICG confirmation

## Microsurgical Resection Principles

- **IONM required**: MEPs, SSEPs, D-wave
- Intraoperative **ICG angiography** multiple times — map the shunt, confirm devascularization
- Open pia sharply along planned corridor
- **Feeder-first** strategy — occlude arterial supply before approaching draining vein
- **Never sacrifice draining vein first** — catastrophic engorgement and hemorrhage
- Bipolar at lowest effective setting; irrigation continuous
- Temporary clips for test-occlusion before permanent sacrifice

## Intraoperative Hemorrhage Management

- Large-bore suctions available
- Cottonoid tamponade; packing; systemic BP support
- Have blood products available before skin incision

## Closure and Postoperative Care

- Watertight dural closure
- Flat bedrest 24 h
- Postoperative angiography at 1–3 months to confirm obliteration
- Neurologic baseline usually worsens transiently then recovers toward preop
- MRI annually

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- **Angio-define the lesion** before any skin incision
- **Pre-op embo** reduces intraoperative morbidity for Type II / IVc
- **Feeders first, vein last** — non-negotiable
- ICG angiography throughout — real-time shunt map
- Type III is rarely operable — set expectations
- Acute hemorrhagic presentation = higher re-bleed risk → treat
- IONM mandatory; stop on sustained MEP / D-wave drop
- Adamkiewicz at thoracolumbar junction — never sacrifice feeding radiculomedullary artery
- Surgical candidates are preferentially **dorsal** and **partly extramedullary (exophytic)** lesions — ventral and deep intramedullary AVMs favor endovascular-first or conservative tracks.
- *En passage* arteries supplying both the cord and the AVM are the defining technical hazard — isolate them at the nidus edge, never at the parent vessel.

## Outcomes and Obliteration Rates

- Complete obliteration: **≈ 80% microsurgical**, **≈ 35% endovascular alone**.
- Long-term morbidity similar between modalities (≈ 10%).
- **Short-term neurologic status** (3 y follow-up): 60–100% improve or maintain preoperative baseline.
- Complete obliteration and even partial embolization are associated with a significant reduction in re-hemorrhage rate — **any reduction in shunt flow matters**.
- Early intervention before disabling deficits develop correlates with better recovery — do not wait for plateau.

## Alternative Modalities

- **Endovascular (Onyx era):** liquid-embolizate injection at the nidus, preserving anterior spinal artery pedicles. Particulate agents are mainly pre-op adjuncts (high recanalization). Endovascular is limited when feeders share a pedicle with the anterior spinal artery or when vessels are small/tortuous.
- **Stereotactic radiosurgery:** anecdotal; not routinely recommended for spinal AVM.
- **Conservative observation:** appropriate when treatment risks exceed natural-history risk (deep ventral, complex angioarchitecture, stable neurologic status).
