# Peri-Insular Hemispherotomy

*Epilepsy Surgery — Peri-Insular Hemispherotomy*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol

## Indications

Catastrophic unilateral hemispheric epilepsy with existing contralateral hemiparesis (resection accelerates already-declining hemisphere function without major new deficit):

- **Rasmussen's encephalitis**
- **Hemimegalencephaly**
- **Sturge-Weber syndrome** with refractory seizures
- **Perinatal infarct / porencephaly** involving one hemisphere
- **Infantile spasms** with lateralized structural abnormality
- Extensive cortical dysplasia confined to one hemisphere

Contraindications: bilateral independent foci, significant contralateral hemispheric function that would be lost, severe coagulopathy.

## Evolution

- **Dandy (1928)** — first hemispherectomy, originally for malignant gliomas; hemispherectomy for epilepsy first reported 1938.
- **Anatomic hemispherectomy** — up to **33% develop superficial cerebral siderosis** at median 8 years post-op (chronic granular ependymitis on the cavity-lining membrane bleeds into CSF → neurologic decline, hydrocephalus, death).
- **Rasmussen functional hemispherectomy** — frontal and occipital poles disconnected but not removed; reduced cavity, lower siderosis, but higher recurrent seizure / reoperation rate vs anatomic.
- **Villemure / Schramm peri-insular hemispherotomy** — minimal resection, maximal disconnection — the modern standard. Lowest blood loss, shortest ICU stay, lowest overall complications; residual hydrocephalus and residual seizure risk persist.

### Comparison of techniques

| Technique | Key complications |
|---|---|
| Anatomic hemispherectomy | High blood loss; superficial siderosis; high shunt-dependent hydrocephalus |
| Functional hemispherectomy | Lower blood loss; lower shunt rate; **higher recurrent-seizure reoperation rate** |
| Hemispherotomy | Lowest blood loss; shortest ICU stay; residual seizures; postoperative hemorrhage; delayed hydrocephalus |

### Common invariants across all hemispherotomy variants

Whatever the approach — peri-insular, modified peri-insular, or vertical parasagittal — four disconnections are non-negotiable:

1. Resection/disconnection of **medial temporal structures** (amygdala, hippocampus).
2. Interruption of **internal capsule and corona radiata** fibers.
3. **Transventricular corpus callosotomy** (genu to splenium).
4. Disruption of the **horizontal frontal fibers** (occipitofrontal and uncinate fasciculi).

Miss any one of the four → persistent seizures.

## Preoperative Considerations

- Detailed MRI — hemispheric extent, residual contralateral abnormalities. **Any contralateral imaging or EEG abnormality predicts significant postoperative functional decline** and is a relative contraindication.
- Video-EEG to confirm the epileptogenic hemisphere and rule out independent contralateral foci.
- Neuropsychology, motor function assessment; document pre-existing hemiparesis and hemianopsia.
- **Language lateralization matters** — most children lateralize by age 6. Surgery after age 6 on the dominant hemisphere carries materially higher permanent language-deficit risk because contralateral reorganization becomes less plastic.
- Referral threshold: ≥ 2 failed, appropriately trialed anticonvulsants. Early surgical evaluation helps avoid developmental regression/arrest from ongoing epileptic syndrome.
- Family consent for expected post-op hemiplegia and hemianopia (often already present).
- Pediatric-specific: **central venous access** for rapid volume repletion (limited blood-volume reserve), type and cross with blood in room, minimize operative time.
- Intraoperative MR-based image guidance — especially useful for genu callosotomy extent and medial boundary of frontal-fiber tractotomy.

## Positioning

- Supine, **large gel pad under ipsilateral shoulder** to avoid neck torsion while permitting ~70° contralateral head rotation.
- Pediatric skull clamp: **single pin behind the ipsilateral ear above the superior nuchal line**; double pin on the contralateral superior temporal line. Clamps are generally reserved for children ≥ 2 y (see [[skull-clamp-placement]]).
- Head slightly elevated.
- Skin mark: standard question-mark incision centered on the peri-insular region.

## Incision and Craniotomy

- Large **question-mark or T-shaped** incision
- Frontotemporoparietal craniotomy exposing:
  - Sylvian fissure
  - Frontal and parietal opercula
  - Temporal lobe
- See [[burr-holes-and-bone-flaps]], [[dural-opening-and-closure]]

## Disconnection Steps

### 1. Temporal Lobectomy

- Standard anterior temporal lobectomy: resect anterior 4–5 cm of temporal lobe including uncus and amygdala
- Preserves hippocampal tail (disconnected later)
- Provides working corridor to insula and temporal horn

### 2. Suprasylvian Window

- Open Sylvian fissure widely (see [[techniques-of-sylvian-fissure-split]])
- Enter lateral ventricle through superior temporal gyrus
- Open lateral ventricle into the body, atrium, and occipital horn

### 3. Circumferential Peri-Insular Disconnection

- **Suprasylvian** corridor: disconnect through corona radiata above insula, enter frontal horn
- **Infrasylvian** corridor: disconnect under the insula through temporal stem
- Leaves the insula in place but disconnected from the rest of the hemisphere

### 4. Corpus Callosum Section

- Through the lateral ventricle, section the **entire corpus callosum** from genu to splenium
- Identify falx and preserve contralateral white matter

### 5. Frontobasal Disconnection

- Cut through frontal white matter at the level of the sphenoid wing to disconnect orbitofrontal / subfrontal cortex

### 6. Mesial Temporal Disconnection

- Remove or disconnect hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus

## Operative Anatomy Landmarks

- **Choroidal fissure** is the critical landmark — *everything medial to it belongs to the diencephalon and must be protected.* Losing this landmark risks thalamic, hypothalamic, or basal-ganglia injury.
- **Pericallosal artery** skeletonization guides the callosotomy anteriorly (to the genu, stopping a few millimeters short of the foramen of Monro) and posteriorly (to the splenium, at the hippocampus–fornix transition; the posterior falx is a reliable stopping landmark).
- **Vein of Galen** appears medial to the splenium after splenial section — protect.
- **Sphenoid wing** outline guides the subcortical frontal disconnection plane; the horizontal frontal fibers include the **occipitofrontal fasciculus** and **uncinate fasciculus**, cut in continuity with the genu callosotomy.
- **MCA cortical branches crossing the planned corticectomies must be preserved** — sacrificing them causes distal cortical ischemia in tissue the surgeon intends to leave in place.
- In hemimegalencephaly with small ventricles, use **temporal lobectomy** to expand the corridor; in more normal-sized ventricles a middle-temporal-gyrus corticectomy (infrasylvian window) alone may suffice without formal lobectomy.
- After unroofing the atrium, the opercular supramarginal gyrus is removed to expose the ventricular trigone; the C-shaped corticotomy outlines the entire lateral-ventricular system and interrupts the internal capsule.

## Variants

- **Classic peri-insular hemispherotomy (Villemure):** supra- and infrasylvian windows, insula left in situ but disconnected.
- **Modified peri-insular (Schramm):** smaller craniotomy, access to the lateral ventricle via the temporal horn — corridor familiar to most epilepsy surgeons. Favors reduced operative time and blood loss.
- **Vertical parasagittal hemispherotomy:** interhemispheric approach, callosotomy through a parasagittal corticectomy; avoids sylvian dissection. Useful when lateral anatomy is distorted.

## Closure

- Hemostasis over disconnection planes — oozing from denuded white matter is common
- **Loose dural closure** with duraplasty if needed
- Bone flap replacement
- Subgaleal drain for 24–48 h

## Postoperative Considerations

- ICU monitoring; aseptic meningitis common
- **Fluid / electrolyte shifts** — SIADH, cerebral salt wasting
- Hyperthermia common (chemical meningitis)
- Hydrocephalus — late, 10–30%; VP shunt may be required (see [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]])
- Contralateral hemiparesis worsens transiently then stabilizes
- Hemianopia expected
- Seizure freedom 70–85% at 2 years
- Long-term rehab; speech may reorganize in children <10 y

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- Confirm pre-existing contralateral deficit — do not hemispherotomize a functional hemisphere
- **Disconnection > resection** — anatomic hemispherectomy is obsolete
- Complete callosal section is non-negotiable — incomplete disconnection = persistent seizures
- Watch for dilated ependymal veins on the floor of the ventricle — torn → hemorrhage
- Drain subgaleal fluid to prevent subgaleal hygroma
- Counsel parents: seizure freedom at the cost of accepting known deficits
- Hydrocephalus risk persists for years — neurosurgical follow-up
