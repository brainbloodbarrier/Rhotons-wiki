# Ventriculo-Pleural Shunt

*CSF Diversion Procedures — Ventriculo-Pleural Shunt*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol. Contributors: Daniel H. Fulkerson, MD, and Joel C. Boaz, MD.

## Background and Rationale

Ventriculo-pleural (VPL) shunts divert CSF into the **absorptive pleural space**. Compared with VP and VA shunts, VPL shunts carry **higher failure rates and higher complication rates**, but **infection rates are comparable to VP shunts** (Hoffman 1983; Piatt 1994; Craven 2017). The pleural space is generally considered a second-line distal terminus.

A **small, asymptomatic pleural effusion** is expected on postoperative chest radiography and does not require treatment. The absorptive capacity of the pleural space can be overwhelmed — if so, the effusion enlarges, becomes symptomatic, and can progress to **life-threatening tension hydrothorax**. Anti-siphon valve design substantially reduces this risk (Martinez-Lage 2000).

The pleura is **innervated**, so patients may feel the distal catheter as pleuritic pain. Other device-specific complications include **fibrothorax**, **empyema**, and **pneumothorax**.

## Indications

- **Salvage** distal site when peritoneum and atrium have failed or are contraindicated
- Peritoneal failure (multiple abdominal surgeries, severe adhesions, malabsorption, peritonitis)
- Atrial contraindications (endocarditis history, right-heart dysfunction, pediatric growth issues)
- Higher failure rate than VP / VA — not first-line
- Adult / older-child preference; **avoid in children <7 years** (small lung capacity)

## Preoperative Planning

- Chest imaging — exclude pleural effusion, prior pleurodesis, significant lung disease
- Pulmonary function assessment if borderline
- **Pediatric caution**: infants and younger children have a smaller chest cavity and less absorptive pleural surface than adults. Most surgeons **defer VPL shunting until at least age 7** because of prohibitively high failure and symptomatic-effusion rates (Hoffman 1983).
- **Valve selection is critical**: pleural space has **negative subatmospheric intrathoracic pressure** with each inspiratory cycle, imparting an active "sucking" force on the distal catheter. Standard differential-pressure valves without anti-siphon will **overdrain → slit ventricles, intracranial hypotension, and large pleural effusions**.
- A **one-way valve with an anti-siphon feature** is mandatory (Martinez-Lage 2000). Adjustable (programmable) vs fixed-resistance valves is a surgeon preference — many prefer programmable for VPL to tune against real-time effusion volume.
- Use **anti-siphon devices** or **gravitational valves** (e.g., Aesculap proSA, Miethke ShuntAssistant) — see [[csf-ventricular-shunt-reference]]

### Infection Prophylaxis

- Follow the [HCRN shunt-infection protocol](https://doi.org/10.3171/2015.8.PEDS15253)
- **Double-glove all operative personnel** for the full case — a specific recommendation emphasized for VPL because the thoracic field adds skin-flora exposure

## Operating Room Setup

- Supine, ipsilateral arm abducted, axilla exposed
- Shoulder bump aligns mastoid–clavicle
- Field continuous from cranium to mid-axillary line, 3rd–5th intercostal space (original NSAtlas range)
- Anesthesia aware of planned brief ipsilateral lung retraction
- **Fluoroscopy / C-arm**: positioned from the start. The distal catheter's free passage into the pleural cavity is confirmed fluoroscopically — a catheter that coils or remains near the entry point signals pleural scarring and predicts rapid failure

## Proximal Catheter

Same as VP shunt — frontal Kocher or occipito-parietal Frazier. See [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]] and [[external-ventricular-drain-placement]].

## Pleural Access

1. Mark 4th–5th intercostal space in mid-axillary line (female: below inframammary fold, avoiding breast tissue)
2. ~2–3 cm incision **parallel to the ribs** (NSAtlas original), between ribs 3–4 or 4–5, lateral to the nipple line
3. Dissect through subcutaneous fat and muscle with a fine hemostat until ribs are palpable
4. Enter pleura **over the superior border of a rib** — the neurovascular bundle hugs the **inferior** border
5. Place a **figure-8 suture in the intercostal muscle** at the entry site but leave it untied — it will be tightened around the catheter after placement to seal against pneumothorax
6. Anesthesiologist performs a **prolonged expiration** to partially deflate and relax the ipsilateral lung
7. With controlled force, **"pop"** through the intercostal muscles into the pleural space with a fine mosquito hemostat — resistance is substantial; never plunge, never angle toward parenchyma
8. Spread the hemostat slightly and immediately feed the **small-caliber** distal catheter 15–20 cm caudally; a smaller catheter reduces pleuritic pain
9. The assistant **aggressively irrigates** the field as the catheter goes in — any air bubbling indicates ongoing communication
10. Lungs re-expanded under irrigation, ideally until bubbling stops at the entry point
11. **Valsalva maneuver** performed to evacuate any residual intrapleural air
12. **Tighten the figure-8 suture** around the distal catheter — pulls the muscle edges closed to prevent inspiratory air entry
13. Irrigate wound with antibiotic fluid; close in layers

### Access Variants

Described equivalents (same principle, different tools):

- **Thoracoscopic** placement — direct visualization, preferred in patients with prior pleural surgery
- **Trocar** or **peel-away sheath** entry — minimizes dissection at the expense of less tactile feedback (Richardson & Handler 2013)

## Tunneling and Assembly

- Tunnel head → thoracic incision; keep tunneler **above the clavicle** at neck (pneumothorax, esophageal injury)
- Attach valve, flush with Ringer's lactate, inject intrathecal vancomycin 10 mg + gentamicin 4 mg
- Connect proximal catheter last; confirm distal flow

## Postoperative Considerations

- **Portable chest radiograph in the recovery room** — exclude large pneumothorax, confirm catheter intrathoracic
- 24 hours of postoperative antibiotics
- **Standing PA and lateral radiographs before discharge**
- Observation in a unit experienced with pulmonary evaluation — not a general neurosurgical ward
- Small self-limited apical pneumothorax common — observe only
- **Family education** before discharge — recognizing signs of respiratory distress in the long-term outpatient setting

### Complication Surveillance

- **Symptomatic pleural effusion / hydrothorax** — most common VPL complication. Can compromise ventilation.
  - **Tension hydrothorax is life-threatening** but characteristically **progressive, not sudden** — prolonged cough, dyspnea on exertion, chest pain, frank shortness of breath. Imaging promptly on any of these.
- **Pleuritic chest pain** — common due to pleural innervation. After pneumothorax and effusion are ruled out, treat with anti-inflammatories; consider smaller-caliber catheter at next revision
- **Overdrainage** (postural headache, subdural hygroma, slit ventricle) — adjust programmable valve or add gravitational unit
- **Catheter migration / retraction** — especially with growth in pediatric patients
- **Fibrothorax / empyema** — rare but shunt-ending; usually requires explant
- If hydrothorax recurs, tap for evaluation and **consider conversion** to VA or gallbladder — do not tap indefinitely

### Follow-Up

- Full cardiopulmonary examination at every visit
- Any pulmonary distress in a VPL patient = prompt, diligent evaluation; assume shunt-related until proven otherwise

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- **Never** use a basic differential-pressure valve alone — pleural negative pressure mandates anti-siphon
- Avoid in children <7 y (pleural capacity too small relative to CSF flux)
- Enter over the **superior border of the rib** — below is the intercostal bundle
- Expand lung with positive-pressure breath during closure to evacuate residual air
- Upright chest X-ray in PACU mandatory
- Counsel patient: new dyspnea = suspect hydrothorax → urgent evaluation
- If hydrothorax recurrent, convert to VA or gallbladder — do not just tap repeatedly
