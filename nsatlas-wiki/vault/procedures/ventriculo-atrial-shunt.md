# Ventriculo-Atrial Shunt

*CSF Diversion Procedures — Ventriculo-Atrial Shunt*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol. Contributors: Scott Breeden, BS; Peter J. Morone, MD, MSCI; Daniel Fulkerson, MD.

## Background and Drawbacks

VA shunting has specific disadvantages that set it apart from VP:

1. **Fixed distal length** — only a limited amount of catheter can sit in the atrium. Growing children require revisions to lengthen the distal catheter as they grow.
2. **Shunt nephritis** — rare but characteristic immune-complex glomerulonephritis; months-to-years latency; often requires shunt removal for renal recovery.
3. **Bacteremia exposure** — any transient bacteremia can seed the intravascular shunt hardware.
4. **Cardiopulmonary aggravation** — VA shunting can exacerbate pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale (Piatt 1989), and is relatively contraindicated in right-heart dysfunction.
5. **Thromboembolism** — intravascular distal catheter carries thrombus-propagation risk, including pulmonary embolism and SVC syndrome.

Despite these, **overall failure and infection rates are similar to VP** in experienced hands, making VA a reasonable second-line distal site.

## Indications

- Hydrocephalus when peritoneum is unavailable or has failed (prior multiple abdominal surgeries, peritonitis, severe adhesions, malabsorption)
- Second-line after VP shunt failure — physiologic distal site (CSF normally drains to venous system)
- Similar infection and revision rates to VP in experienced hands

See [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]] for shared proximal technique and [[csf-ventricular-shunt-reference]] for valve selection.

## Preoperative Considerations

- **Echocardiogram** to exclude right-heart dysfunction, tricuspid regurgitation, and patent foramen ovale
- **Preoperative Doppler ultrasound** of the venous system — assess patency of IJV and subclavian
  - Patients with prior central lines frequently have **venous stenosis or occlusion**
  - High-risk populations: **premature infants with post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus**, patients with **prior ECMO**, patients with multiple prior CVCs
- Note any existing central venous catheter — not a contraindication, but constrains the choice of venous access
- Fluoroscopy available intraoperatively — mandatory for tip verification
- Informed consent covers VA-specific complications: thrombosis, bacterial endocarditis, cardiac arrhythmia, shunt nephritis (immune-complex glomerulonephritis), pulmonary embolism, SVC syndrome
- In children: distal catheter must be re-lengthened with growth (fixed distal length, unlike VP)
- **HCRN shunt-infection protocol** — centers with volume should adopt standardized infection-prevention bundles ([Kestle 2016](https://doi.org/10.3171/2015.8.PEDS15253))

### Side and Proximal-Catheter Selection

- **Right side preferred** when no anatomic constraints — the right venous system aligns more directly with the atrium, no sharp angles
- **Parieto-occipital** placement preferred in adults and ambulatory children
- **Frontal** placement preferred for patients who remain recumbent for extended periods — avoids pressure necrosis over the valve, wound dehiscence, and skin breakdown (particularly in infants)
- In **premature infants**, avoid forcing the head persistently to one side — can induce head-shape abnormality and torticollis

### Valve Selection

- Use a **one-way valve**; choose size carefully in small pediatric patients (adult valves may tent or erode neck skin with motion)
- **Atrial pressure is not always zero** — cardiac abnormalities, pulmonary hypertension, or elevated central venous pressure all raise it and hinder shunt function; additional CSF volume into the atrium can worsen existing pulmonary hypertension
- **Laminar atrial flow** can impart a "wicking" negative pressure on the distal catheter → consider an **anti-siphon valve** to prevent overshunting
- Familiarity with multiple valve types is expected — there is no one-size-fits-all VA valve

## Positioning

- Supine, head turned opposite shunt side
- **Chest roll between the shoulder blades** so the shoulders fall laterally — opens subclavian access
- Neck extended slightly so **mastoid process aligns in the same plane as the clavicle** — facilitates distal catheter passage and IJV cannulation
- **Radiolucent soft doughnut headrest** — metallic head holders and clamps block fluoroscopy. For adults the table may need to be reversed to allow full C-arm range
- Prepped area includes: scalp, neck (both IJV and subclavian entry points), upper chest. Include contralateral subclavian entry site if venous anatomy is abnormal
- Include all components of any existing shunt in the prep if converting
- Fluoroscopy C-arm planned for lateral and AP cardiac views

## Proximal Catheter

Identical to VP shunt — frontal Kocher's point or occipito-parietal Frazier's point. See [[external-ventricular-drain-placement]] for Kocher's landmarks.

## Distal Catheter — Atrial Access

Consider having a **vascular or general surgeon assist** with venous access, especially in infants.

### Cutdown Approach (open)

- **Linear incision along the medial border of sternocleidomastoid**; dissect below platysma
- Continue along SCM's medial border until the **union of common facial vein with the IJV** is identified
- Catheter is placed under direct vision — **preferred in smaller children** where anatomy prohibits a percutaneous approach
- Isolate facial vein with vessel loops; proximal and distal ties
- Venotomy; introduce distal catheter; advance under fluoroscopic guidance
- Ligate facial vein around catheter (no retrograde flow risk)

### Percutaneous Modified Seldinger

Most commonly used approach in adults and larger children:

1. **Ultrasound guidance** mandatory — identify IJV, distinguish from common carotid (IJV is lateral/anterior, compressible, dilates with Valsalva)
2. **Landmark**: inferior one-third of sternocleidomastoid, or 2–3 fingerbreadths above the clavicle moving superiorly
3. Extend the neck and place the patient in **head-down (Trendelenburg)** position to dilate the IJV
4. Needle insertion at **~45° angle**; aspirate gently — **dark venous blood draws easily**
5. Pass **soft-tipped J-wire** through the needle — should advance without resistance
6. Remove needle; advance wire to atrium under **live fluoroscopy**
7. **Ectopic beats on the cardiac monitor indicate the wire has entered too deeply** — pull back. Never release the wire: atrial pressure can "suck" it into the heart, requiring emergent retrieval by a cardiologist or interventional radiologist
8. **Dilate** over the wire; place **7- or 9-French peel-away sheath**
9. Pre-flush the distal catheter; test sheath-to-catheter fit before insertion
10. Pass catheter through the sheath; peel; fluoroscopy confirms position

**Subclavian access** is an alternative (same landmarks as central venous line). On the **left side**, the subclavian is often **easier than the left IJV** because the left IJV-to-subclavian-to-SVC pathway creates **two right-angle turns** that can trap the wire.

### Catheter Sizing (pediatric)

- Standard distal catheter has ~**2.2 mm outer diameter**, fits a 7- or 9-French sheath
- **Premature infants** may need smaller-caliber distal catheters — commercially available **step-down connectors** attach a smaller-caliber distal catheter to a standard one

## Tip Position — Fluoroscopic Landmarks

- **Target**: cavoatrial junction / mid right atrium (per NSAtlas original: **T6–T8** or midatrium)
- Too deep (mid-atrium or ventricle): arrhythmia, tricuspid injury
- Too shallow (SVC): thrombosis risk, catheter migration
- Lateral view confirms catheter is in SVC/atrium, not aberrant vessel
- If the catheter is not well visualized by fluoroscopy, **a small bolus of contrast** through the catheter can improve delineation
- **Account for neck position** when finalizing depth: if the neck is turned sharply during placement, the catheter may migrate deeper when the neck straightens postoperatively — leave a safety margin

## Connection and Flush

- Connect valve between proximal and distal catheters in subgaleal pocket
- Flush with Ringer's lactate (no bacitracin → airlock risk)
- Intrathecal vancomycin 10 mg + gentamicin 4 mg via valve tap; occlude distal during injection
- See [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]] for shared assembly protocol

## Postoperative Considerations

- **Extubate and examine before leaving the operating room** — baseline neuro + cardiopulmonary exam
- Immediate postop **chest X-ray** to confirm tip at cavoatrial junction AND **rule out pneumothorax** (subclavian access specifically)
- Baseline ECG; monitor for new arrhythmia
- **Persistent arrhythmia** typically **resolves with a 1–2 cm pullback** of the distal catheter
- Uncomplicated patients discharge **postoperative day 1**
- Watch for:
  - **Shunt nephritis** — proteinuria, hematuria, hypocomplementemia; months-to-years after placement; indication for shunt removal (Hunt & Somers 2019)
  - **Bacterial endocarditis** — persistent bacteremia; tough to clear without shunt removal
  - **Atrial thrombosis / pulmonary embolism**
  - **Cor pulmonale** — lethal in severe cases (Piatt 1989); particular concern in high-volume shunts into a compromised pulmonary vascular bed
  - **Catheter tip withdrawal with growth** in children — annual chest X-ray surveillance

### Central Line / TPN Caveat

In patients with a VA shunt, use of additional central intravenous access or **total parenteral nutrition** has been associated with increased complication risk (White 2015). Plan around this: minimize additional intravascular hardware in VA-shunted patients when possible.

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- Do **not** leave tip in SVC — thrombosis; advance to cavoatrial junction (T6–T7)
- Do **not** push into right ventricle — arrhythmia and tricuspid injury
- Echo before surgery; ECG monitoring intraoperatively
- Facial vein ligation after cutdown avoids retrograde venous bleeding
- Children need catheter lengthening — monitor chest X-ray serially
- Consider VA over VPl when peritoneum fails but pleura contraindicated
- Prior endocarditis or known right-heart shunt: choose VP or [[ventriculo-gallbladder-shunt]] instead
