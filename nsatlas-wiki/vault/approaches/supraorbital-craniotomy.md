# Supraorbital Craniotomy

*Eyebrow Incision and Supraorbital Craniotomy*
*DOI: 10.18791/nsatlas.v2.ch04*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol / Acioly | **Pages:** 17 | **Figures:** 13

## General Considerations

The supraorbital craniotomy is a lateral skull base approach suitable for the parasellar, parachiasmatic, and intrasylvian space. It minimizes brain retraction — critical for deep lesions underneath the dominant hemisphere. When combined with removal of the orbital roof, intraorbital tumors become readily accessible.

With an **eyebrow incision**, this becomes a minimally invasive keyhole approach. Variations include: supraorbital, eyebrow supraorbital, frontolateral, supraciliary frontolateral keyhole, and lateral supraorbital.

Compared to the [[pterional-craniotomy|standard pterional]], the supraorbital subfrontal corridor affords a **longer operative working distance** but potentially less brain manipulation. Dynamic retraction via suction apparatus and dissectors provides flexible working angles for anterior circulation aneurysms and large anterior skull base tumors.

> Orbitofrontal and mediobasal cortices are often misleadingly considered "silent" — subfrontal injury leads to **disinhibition** (orbitofrontal) and **anterograde/retrograde amnesia** (mediobasal).

## Supraorbital vs Pterional vs Variants — Decision Table

| Approach | Working corridor | Vertical reach | Brain manipulation | Sphenoid-wing access | Cosmetic risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Supraorbital (eyebrow)** | Longer | Limited | Lowest | Poor | Eyebrow scar; frontalis palsy risk |
| **Lateral supraorbital (Hernesniemi)** | Moderate | Moderate | Low | Limited | Hairline scar, frontalis risk lower |
| **Minipterional (Figueiredo)** | Moderate | Moderate | Moderate | Good | Hidden hairline |
| **Standard pterional** | Shorter, wide | Best | Higher | Excellent | Hidden hairline |
| **Orbitozygomatic** | Best for inferior-superior | Excellent | Lowest at depth | Excellent | Orbitofacial asymmetry risk |

Rhoton dissections show **substantial overlap** among the anterolateral approaches — pick based on vertical reach needed, sphenoid-wing involvement, and cosmesis.

## Why the "Silent Frontal Lobe" Is Not Silent

Orbitofrontal and mediobasal cortices are frequently but **misleadingly** labeled as silent. Consequences of subfrontal injury:

- **Orbitofrontal damage** → **disinhibition**, impulse-control loss, socially inappropriate behavior.
- **Mediobasal temporal lobe / basal forebrain damage** → **anterograde + retrograde amnesia** (fornix, septal nuclei, hippocampal formation).

Keyhole approaches reduce but do not eliminate the risk; preserve venous drainage, avoid prolonged retraction, and drain CSF before any retraction begins.

## Frontalis Nerve — Why Two Incision Styles

- **Frontalis branches of the facial nerve** travel in the **superficial fascia over the fat pad** above the zygoma.
- **Horizontal transection of the fat pad** cuts these branches → **temporary frontalis palsy** that may become permanent if transected rather than retracted.
- **Subfascial dissection** reflecting the fat pad **posteriorly** preserves the nerve.
- The **supraorbital nerve** exits through the supraorbital notch and defines the **medial edge** of a safe eyebrow incision — stay lateral to it, dissect subperiosteally, and retract rather than cut.

## Frontal Sinus — Decision Principles

- **Pre-op imaging** (CT) delineates the lateral extent of the frontal sinus — often asymmetric, may extend laterally into the planned craniotomy window.
- If entered:
  - **Remove all mucosa** (pituitary rongeurs + monopolar).
  - Pack with **muscle, betadine-soaked bone wax, or betadine-soaked Gelfoam**.
  - Cover the defect with the pre-harvested **vascularized pericranial flap**.
  - **Do not place fixation plates/screws directly over the sinus** — screws invade the cavity and cause CSF leak.
- Prophylactic **lumbar drain for 1–2 days** post-op if the sinus was entered or if the skull base defect is large.

## Indications

- Large (<5cm) anterior skull base tumors: olfactory groove, planum sphenoidale, tuberculum sella [[meningioma|meningiomas]]
- Anteriorly and inferiorly pointing AComA aneurysms, common ophthalmic aneurysms
- Parasellar tumors including craniopharyngiomas
- Small mid-to-medial anterior sylvian fissure lesions (small MCA aneurysms, interpeduncular small tumors)

### NOT indicated for

- More posteriorly situated lesions with significant cranial extensions — limited vertical reach
- Large tumors or complex aneurysms — long working distance restricts working angles
- Tumors extending along sphenoid wing into middle fossa — use traditional [[pterional-craniotomy|pterional]]
- **Cosmetic concern:** slightly increased risk with eyebrow incision; patients with sparse eyebrows should consider pterional routes

## Preoperative Considerations

- Tumor embolization rarely used for meningiomas — craniotomy allows devascularization along anterior skull base
- Assess **frontal sinus** lateral extent on preoperative imaging — pack with muscle/bone wax if entered
- **Lumbar drain** for patients with lesional edema and expected increased intracranial tension
- Pericranial flap harvested during exposure for potential frontal sinus coverage

## Operative Anatomy

Key anatomical considerations:
- Anterior skull base: frontal sinus (asymmetric — review preop imaging), olfactory bulbs, cribriform plate in a "valley" relative to orbital roofs
- **Supraorbital nerve** defines medial edge of incision — must be protected
- **Frontalis nerve branches** in superficial fascia of fat pad — do NOT transect fat pad horizontally

## Steps of the Operation

### 1. Positioning
- Supine, head turned ~20 degrees (mainly midline lesions)
- Pins placed along superior temporal line, single pin above pinna
- Patient secured to table for potential "airplane" maneuvers

### 2. Incision — Two Styles
- **Eyebrow incision** (Cohen-Gadol preference): marked just above the eyebrow, cosmetically acceptable for normal/thick eyebrows
- **Bicoronal (Soutar) incision**: extends to contralateral pupil level, for those concerned about cosmesis

### 3. Soft Tissue Dissection
- Eyebrow: identify and protect the **supraorbital nerve** (medial edge landmark)
- Soft tissue over keyhole transected parallel to frontal process of zygoma, nerve on fat pad reflected posteriorly
- Expose supraorbital rim; pericranium elevated separately as vascularized graft
- *Fishhook retractors crucial for maximizing bony exposure*

### 4. Craniotomy
- **Single burr hole** under temporalis at keyhole — true MacCarty keyhole exposes frontal dura and orbit
- Inferior cut parallel to orbital roof guided by dissector "feeling" the roof through keyhole
- *The height of the craniotomy is the most limiting aspect* during intradural work
- Navigation to avoid frontal sinus, but bone removal > sinus preservation

### 5. Sphenoid Ridge and Orbital Roof
- Overhanging frontal bone drilled flush with orbital roof (side-cutting bit)
- Orbital rim may also be removed for expanded inferior-to-superior trajectory in larger tumors
- *There should be no frontal bone obstructing the view parallel to the orbital roof*

### 6. Dural Opening and Intradural Work
- U-shaped opening based on orbital rim
- Frontal lobes mobilized via gravity retraction + lumbar drain + optico-carotid cistern opening
- Anterior sylvian fissure split, optico-carotid arachnoid membranes released

### 7. Closure
- Watertight dural closure with running suture, especially if frontal sinus entered
- Pericranial flap for dural closure or frontal sinus coverage
- Cranioplasty to fill depressions; **avoid fixation plates over frontal sinus** (screws can cause CSF leak)
- Eyebrow incision closed carefully with absorbable sutures for cosmesis; no subgaleal drain

## Orbital Rim Osteotomy (Fronto-Orbital Craniotomy)

Modified form for parasellar lesions with superior extensions or superior intraorbital tumors. Bony cuts medial to supraorbital notch and above frontozygomatic suture — orbital roof can be fractured to expand corridor.

## Postoperative Considerations

- ICU 1-2 days, then ward transfer
- Lumbar drain 1-2 days if concern for CSF leak through frontal sinus
- **Temporary frontalis palsy** common — resolves in 4-6 weeks
- Supraorbital nerve numbness undesirable — minimize traction during early steps
- **DANGER:** Significant pneumocephalus on postop CT without visible leak → suspect large leak; **continuation of lumbar drainage will reverse-suction air through the nose** → worsening pneumocephalus → acute neurologic deterioration and infection. Requires repeat operation.

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- Head must be tilted back to maximize gravity retraction
- Supraorbital rim removal significantly expands access to anterior cranial base
- Exposure should be **minimally invasive AND minimally disruptive**
- Inferior edge of craniotomy must be flush with orbital roof
