# Interhemispheric Craniotomy

*Interhemispheric Dissection for Reaching the Ventricular System*
*DOI: 10.18791/nsatlas.v2.ch07*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol | **Pages:** 18 | **Figures:** 16

## Overview

The anterior or posterior interhemispheric craniotomy provides access to **deep midline parafalcine and paraventricular spaces** through the natural interhemispheric fissure. Working in this natural plane minimizes brain retraction and transgression, but the corridor is deep and narrow.

The craniotomy sits **around the coronal suture** for the frontal (anterior) interhemispheric route, or **behind the central lobule** for the superior parietal (posterior) route. This avoids the **sensorimotor cortices** and the dominant parasagittal veins, which are typically 3-4 cm posterior to the coronal suture.

**Non-negotiables:** avoid fixed retractors (use dynamic/gravity retraction in lateral position); drain CSF early (lumbar drain or ventriculostomy) to mobilize the hemisphere away from midline; preserve large parasagittal bridging veins — sacrifice risks venous infarction and hemiparesis. When lateral reach is inadequate, the contralateral transfalcine modification (see [[contralateral-interhemispheric-transfalcine-transprecuneus-approach|PITTA]]) expands the working angle.

## Indications

- **Parafalcine meningiomas** and medial hemispheric gliomas
- **Distal anterior cerebral artery aneurysms** (including pericallosal)
- Medial metastases and arteriovenous malformations
- **Anterior third ventricular lesions**: colloid cysts, hypothalamic hamartomas, craniopharyngiomas
- **Posterior third ventricular / pineal region masses** with superior extension — posterior interhemispheric transcallosal intervenous route (working around the internal cerebral veins)
- **Posterior thalamic tumors**

### Pericallosal aneurysms

- Trajectory depends on aneurysm location relative to the genu of the corpus callosum
- **Aneurysms below the genu** → low interhemispheric approach for proximal control

### NOT indicated for

- Lesions primarily affecting the **ipsilateral thalamus** or **caudate nucleus** (lateral trajectory is limited) — use [[contralateral-interhemispheric-transfalcine-transprecuneus-approach|contralateral interhemispheric]] or transcortical corridors instead

## Preoperative Considerations

- **Lumbar drain** for CSF drainage — basal cisterns/ventricles are not readily accessible during fissure dissection
- **MRV or CTA** if preoperative MRI raises concern for the SSS or bridging vein anatomy
- **Anterior third of SSS** — venous structures are considered relatively safe for ligation but risk of infarction persists; minimize manipulation
- If numerous large parasagittal veins drain into the SSS over the tumor, **switch to contralateral transfalcine** route rather than sacrifice them
- **Parafalcine meningiomas**: typically do not require SSS ligation; if they infiltrate the sinus, prepare for **air embolism** management
- **Mannitol 1 g/kg** at skin incision for large tumors
- **30-40 cc lumbar CSF** drained gradually during craniotomy

## Operative Anatomy

### Parasagittal Venous Anatomy

- Parasagittal bridging veins are **sparse anterior to the coronal suture** — an interhemispheric craniotomy mostly anterior to the suture avoids these veins
- A **large bridging vein just posterior to the coronal suture** drains the frontal lobes — preserve

### Midline Dissection Landmarks (cingulum → corpus callosum → fornices)

1. **Cingulum** — medial cortices, often adherent between hemispheres; can be mistaken for corpus callosum
2. **Corpus callosum** — glistening white, stands out against cingulate cortex; pericallosal arteries lie on its superior surface
3. **Fornices** — deep to the corpus callosum, C-shaped fiber bundles

The **callosomarginal artery** serves as a roadmap down to the pericallosal arteries. Deep diencephalic veins (internal cerebral, thalamostriate) must be preserved.

## Patient Position

- **Supine**: orients operator to midline structures; preferred for some operators
- **Lateral**: allows gravity retraction of the dependent (ipsilateral) hemisphere away from the falx
- **Pericallosal aneurysm** below genu: slightly flexed neck; location of lesion relative to genu determines degree of neck extension
- **Tumors**: position lesion as high as possible within the operative field
- **Mayfield** skull clamp

## Skin Incision

- **Curvilinear** or **horseshoe** incision behind the hairline
- Horseshoe: **one-third behind, two-thirds anterior** to the coronal suture; crosses the midline
- Linear incision location tailored to lesion location
- **U-shaped incision** for larger tumors
- Skull-clamp pin placement planned relative to incision

## Bone Work

- **Paramedian craniotomy** outlined (right-sided for right-handed operator; guided by lesion laterality)
- **Burr holes placed on the SSS**; part of the sinus width is exposed to allow gentle lateral rotation of the dural sinus after opening
- **Neuronavigation** guides planning of the craniotomy and callosotomy
- Use a **#3 Penfield dissector** to generously strip dura from the inner calvarium — especially over the sinus — before the final bone cut along the sinus, so the flap can be elevated quickly if the sinus is injured
- **Surgicel / Gelfoam** strips on standby for sinus hemostasis
- Lumbar CSF drainage allows the sinus and dura to fall away from the inner table, preventing unintentional sinus injury or durotomy

## Dural Opening

- **U-shaped dural flap** based on the SSS
- **Retraction sutures** through the superior falx rotate the SSS laterally and expand the interhemispheric reach
- **Preserve large bridging veins**; small ones may be sacrificed selectively
- Gravity retraction in the lateral position complements sinus rotation

## Intradural Work

### Interhemispheric Dissection

- Enter the subdural space along the falx
- Trace the **ipsilateral callosomarginal artery** along the midline to the cingulum — this artery defines midline dissection planes
- Adherent cingulate cortices are sharply split to avoid subpial injury
- **Meticulous, sharp microsurgical technique** — do not retract aggressively; augment CSF drainage if needed

### Corpus Callosum Identification

- **Glistening white** corpus callosum vs **reddish** cingulate gyrus
- Paired **pericallosal arteries** run along the superior surface of the corpus callosum
- Adherent arachnoid is split widely anteriorly and posteriorly
- Pericallosal arteries may adhere together or be displaced to one side

### Callosotomy (if needed)

- **1.5 cm callosotomy** exposes the foramen of Monro, choroid plexus, internal cerebral veins, and thalamostriate veins (colloid cyst, intraventricular tumor)
- **Neuronavigation** guides precise callosotomy location
- If septum pellucidum herniates into the field (biventricular obstruction at foramen of Monro), **fenestrate** to relieve herniation and clear view
- For **posterior third ventricular / pineal** lesions, a more posterior callosotomy is used

### Cross-court Option

- For lesions extending laterally, a **T-shaped falx incision** with falcine retention sutures enables a contralateral transfalcine trajectory — see [[contralateral-interhemispheric-transfalcine-transprecuneus-approach]]

## Closure

- **Watertight dural closure** (ventricle was likely entered)
- **Do NOT remove Surgicel/Gelfoam** from the superior sagittal sinus
- **Ventricular catheter** briefly postoperatively if the ventricle was entered — clears intraventricular debris

## Postoperative Considerations

- **ICU observation** for 1-2 days before ward transfer
- **Avoid hypertension** and narcotic overmedication
- **Ventriculostomy** typically removed on postoperative day 2

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- **Lateral position** allows gravity retraction but may disorient operators regarding normal ventricular anatomy — navigation helps
- **Craniotomy across the SSS** + **superior falx retraction sutures** expands the interhemispheric corridor meaningfully
- **Sharp microsurgical technique** + **CSF drainage** > aggressive retraction for avoiding cingulate subpial injury
- **Cingulate vs corpus callosum confusion** — remember the glistening white appearance of the callosum; pericallosal arteries are a reliable landmark
- **Disorientation along the sagittal axis** (too anterior or too posterior) is easy — navigate against cingulum → callosum → fornix landmarks
- When facing numerous contralateral parasagittal veins, **abandon this route** for a contralateral transfalcine or transcortical superior parietal lobule approach

## Complications

- **Venous infarction + hemiparesis** from sacrificed parasagittal bridging vein
- **Air embolism** (SSS involvement by tumor)
- **Subpial injury to cingulate cortices**
- **Pericallosal artery injury** from inattentive scissor tips
- **Hydrocephalus** / ventricular debris (mitigated by temporary EVD)
- **Fornix injury** during callosotomy if landmarks are miscalculated
