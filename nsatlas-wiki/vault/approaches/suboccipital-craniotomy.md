# Suboccipital Craniotomy

*Midline Suboccipital Approach.*
*DOI: 10.18791/nsatlas.v2.ch10*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol | **Pages:** 11 | **Figures:** 10

## Overview

The midline suboccipital and lateral suboccipital (retromastoid) craniotomies are the **workhorse approaches to the posterior fossa**. This chapter focuses on the **midline suboccipital** craniotomy — the preferred mode of exposure for the majority of vermian, posterior inferior pontine/medullary, foramen magnum, midline cerebellar, and pineal region pathologies.

Fourth-ventricular lesions are best approached through the **telovelar approach** performed through this craniotomy — see [[telovelar-approach]].

## Indications

1. **Developmental anomalies** — Chiari malformations
2. **Posterior fossa tumors** — metastases, meningiomas, ependymomas, astrocytomas, medulloblastomas
3. **Vascular lesions** — aneurysms, cavernous malformations, AVMs
4. **Pineal region tumors** via the supracerebellar corridor (see [[midline-supracerebellar-craniotomy]] and [[paramedian-supracerebellar-craniotomy]])

For lesions extending from posterior fossa into middle fossa: **supracerebellar transtentorial** or combined middle-and-posterior fossa craniotomy.

## Preoperative Considerations

- **Hydrocephalus management**: avoid preoperative shunting when possible (tumor resection may resolve obstruction and obviate permanent diversion)
- **Communicating hydrocephalus**: intraoperative **lumbar CSF drainage** affords brain relaxation — useful when lesion prohibits early cisterna magna / CPA access
- **Neurophysiologic monitoring** for lesions near brainstem/cranial nerves; **facial nucleus mapping** on the fourth ventricular floor to minimize morbidity
- **Imaging review** for displaced cerebrovascular structures; **pial invasion** of brainstem → plan radical subtotal with thin sheet over brainstem
- **Hemangioblastomas** (large, solid) / AVMs — aggressive preoperative **embolization** reduces intraoperative blood loss

## Operative Anatomy

### Suboccipital Bony Landmarks

- **Inion** (external occipital protuberance) ≈ torcula location
- **Superior nuchal line** ≈ transverse sinus location
- **Asterion** is **NOT a reliable landmark** for the transverse-sigmoid junction — a burr hole over the asterion usually lands on the transverse sinus and endangers it
- **Lambdoid suture**, **occipitomastoid suture**
- **External occipital crest**, **condylar fossa**, **digastric groove**
- **Occipital condyles**, **occipital artery groove**

### Craniocervical Junction

- **Dura is NOT covered by bone** between foramen magnum and C1
- **Vertebral artery** over the lateral C1 arch (**sulcus arteriosus**) is **not covered by bone**, may travel within suboccipital muscles
- **Monopolar electrocautery at low settings and blunt dissection** near the craniocervical junction
- **Brisk venous plexus bleeding** around the artery → warning sign that artery is near

### Cerebellar Surface and Fourth Ventricular Floor

- Suboccipital surface of cerebellum: **pyramid, uvula, tonsil, biventral lobule**, **cerebellomedullary fissure**, **vallecula**
- Fourth ventricular floor landmarks: **fastigium, superior medullary velum, facial colliculus, striae medullares, hypoglossal and vagal triangles, area postrema, obex, funiculus separans**
- Vascular anatomy: **SCA, AICA, PICA** with cranial and caudal PICA loops

## Patient Position

- **Park-bench** (Cohen-Gadol preference) — gravity keeps field clear, surgeon can sit during microsurgery
- **Prone**: allows two-surgeon co-observation; steep tentorial angle makes prone impractical for supracerebellar in obese patients
- **Sitting**: gravity retraction of cerebellum away from tentorium — advantageous for supracerebellar; venous air embolism risk and uncomfortable arm postures are drawbacks
- **Head turned 45° toward the floor**, skull clamp

### OR setup

- Surgeon and scrub tech across the table for efficient instrument transfer
- Anesthesiologist at the foot
- Keen's point prepared if intraoperative ventriculostomy anticipated

## Skin Incision

- **Midline incision from inion to C2 spinous process**
- Extended through deep layers to the suboccipital bone along **midline avascular fascial planes** — staying midline prevents postoperative neck pain
- Muscle attachments disconnected from superior nuchal line with monopolar; subperiosteal muscle elevation from occipital bone
- **Low cautery settings + blunt dissection** over the posterior C1 arch

## Bone Work

- **Two burr holes over the transverse sinuses**
- Penfield dissection of dura and sinus walls (including torcula) off the inner table
- **Craniotome with footplate** inscribes the bone flap over dura
- **Last cut over the sinuses**: **B1 bit, no footplate** to protect sinuses
- Alternative: complete craniotomy over dura, then **drill remaining sinus-overlying bone in layers**
- Alternative #2: **two paramedian craniotomies** flanking a midline septum, then disconnect the midline bone as second step
- **Kerrison rongeurs** extend opening laterally toward occipital condyles
- **C1 laminectomy** for foramen magnum / upper cervical lesions, or when wider dural opening / flexible inferior-to-superior trajectory is needed (e.g., telovelar route to 4th ventricle)

## Dural Opening

- **Y-shaped dural opening** with more inferior extension for lateral dural flap mobilization
- **Superior limb** extends to the inferior aspect of the transverse sinus
- For **midline lesions**, open along the midline — facilitates watertight closure
- **Arachnoid over cisterna magna** opened (arachnoid knife, microscissors) to drain CSF and relax the brain

## Intradural Work

- Depending on pathology:
  - **Vermis / 4th ventricle**: telovelar (preferred) or transvermian route
  - **Foramen magnum / craniocervical**: with C1 laminectomy
  - **Supracerebellar → pineal**: see [[midline-supracerebellar-craniotomy]]
- **CN floor mapping** if operating near facial nucleus or fourth ventricular floor

## Closure

- **Watertight dura** — 4-0 NuroloN running or interrupted
- **Avoid dural allograft**; use **pericranium** over occipital bone as dural graft
- **Valsalva maneuver** to check for CSF leak source
- **Bone replaced with cranial plates** if no cerebellar swelling; do not replace if swollen — leave off, no extra dural tension
- **Neck muscles gently approximated** (avoid tension/strangulation → muscle necrosis, uncontrolled postoperative pain)
- **Fascia watertight**

## Postoperative Considerations

- **ICU 1-2 days**, then ward
- **Prophylactic steroids** (aseptic meningitis)
- Ventriculostomy weaned off
- Watch for **posterior fossa tension** from cerebellar retraction edema — may require decompression; partial transverse sinus thrombosis + vermian vein sacrifice compound risk

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- **Asterion is not a reliable transverse-sigmoid junction landmark** — burr-hole risk
- **Vertebral artery at the C1 sulcus arteriosus**: blunt dissection, low-setting cautery only
- **Stay midline in the avascular fascial plane** to avoid postoperative neck pain
- **Air embolism** (sitting position): low threshold of suspicion
- **Last cut over sinuses with B1 bit** — protects transverse sinus and torcula
- **Pericranial autograft > allograft** for posterior fossa dural reconstruction
- **Do NOT tension the closure or replace the bone flap over a swollen brain**
- **Telovelar > transvermian** for fourth ventricular access — reduces cerebellar mutism risk

## Complications

- **Vertebral artery injury** at craniocervical junction
- **Venous air embolism** (sitting)
- **Transverse sinus laceration / thrombosis**
- **Cerebellar retraction edema** → posterior fossa tension
- **Cerebellar mutism** (pediatric; vermian injury)
- **CSF fistula**, **aseptic meningitis**, **pseudomeningocele**
- **Postcraniotomy headache** (mitigated by fascia/muscle approximation technique)
- **Lower cranial nerve deficits** (floor of 4th ventricle pathology)
