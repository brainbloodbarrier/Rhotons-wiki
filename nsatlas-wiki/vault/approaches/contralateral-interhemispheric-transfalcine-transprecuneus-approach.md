# Contralateral Interhemispheric Transfalcine Transprecuneus Approach

*PITTA — cross-court corridor to the atrium and medial trigone.*
*DOI: 10.18791/nsatlas.v2.ch16*

**Source:** NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol | **Pages:** 16 | **Figures:** 13

## Overview

The posterior interhemispheric craniotomy provides access to deep midline parafalcine and paraventricular spaces through the natural interhemispheric fissure. The corridor is deep and narrow; the highly functional overlying cortices and white-matter tracts (optic radiations lateral to the ventricle, postcentral gyrus laterally/superficially, thalamus anteroinferiorly) constrain surgical corridors to the atrium.

The **contralateral posterior interhemispheric transfalcine transprecuneus approach (PITTA)** sacrifices a small wedge of contralateral precuneus to gain a **cross-court trajectory** onto the medial atrial wall. This offers more flexible working angles along the lateral pole of the lesion while **minimizing ipsilateral brain manipulation**. It is a refinement of the ipsilateral interhemispheric route, in which the limited working angle along the lateral pole of an atrial lesion commonly forces subtotal resection.

## Indications

- Tumors and vascular lesions confined to the **medial wall of the atrium** and within the **trigone** of the lateral ventricle
- **Gliomas**, **meningiomas**, and **arteriovenous malformations** of the medial atrial wall / trigone
- Meningiomas benefit from **early devascularization** via direct exposure of choroidal feeders from the choroid plexus
- Medial atrial AVMs: proximal control over choroidal and posterior cerebral artery feeders early, with minimal normal-brain transgression

### NOT indicated for

- Tumors extending anteriorly into the **diencephalon** from the periatrial region
- Lesions predominantly involving the **lateral wall** of the atrium (use transcortical superior parietal lobule route instead)
- Patients with **numerous large parasagittal veins** draining into the contralateral SSS in the region — ligating them risks venous infarction; use a transcortical superior parietal lobule approach instead

## Preoperative Considerations

- **Visual field testing** — atrial lesions may affect vision; most present with nonspecific symptoms and signs
- **Lumbar drain** is the method of choice for intraoperative CSF drainage: **50-60 cc removed gradually in 10-15 cc aliquots** during craniotomy and after dural opening. Obviates fixed retractors on the unaffected hemisphere since cisterns are not accessed
- **Venous imaging**: evaluate the SSS and bridging veins over the superior parietal lobule on MR, with **MRV or CTA** if needed
- **Straight sinus**: falx incision may approach it — preoperative evaluation is essential
- **Neuronavigation** for incision and craniotomy planning, falx incision design, and precuneus corticotomy
- **Anticonvulsants** administered

## Operative Anatomy

### Parasagittal Venous Anatomy

The parasagittal veins are often sparse over the superior parietal lobule — favorable for this approach. However, **duplicated veins of Trolard** and large veins immediately anterior and posterior to the superior parietal lobule region may be present (especially when the vein of Labbé is absent). Ligating any large bridging vein risks significant venous infarction.

### Atrium / Trigone

The interhemispheric fissure gives access to the medial wall of the lateral ventricle through a precuneus corticotomy. The **choroid plexus** lies within the atrium; aggressive manipulation or coagulation risks ischemic lesions and dense visual field deficits.

## Patient Position

- **Park-bench position** with the **normal (unaffected) hemisphere dependent** — gravity retraction mobilizes the dependent contralateral hemisphere away from the midline
- Head rotated so the **midline / sagittal suture axis sits at 45° to the floor**
- No fixed retractors are required; gravity plus lumbar drainage expand the interhemispheric corridor

## Skin Incision

- **Linear incision just behind the parietal bossing**
- Designed with **neuronavigation** to avoid parasagittal bridging veins
- The interhemispheric operative corridor is situated **between two bridging veins** — the incision and flap must respect both

## Bone Work

- Craniotomy **exposes the full width of the superior sagittal sinus** on the relevant side
- Bone flap designed around preoperative venous mapping

## Dural Opening

- Dural opening is **adjusted to preserve parasagittal veins**
- The preserved veins are **untethered** (freed from their dural investments) to widen the working zone through the interhemispheric corridor
- **Two retention sutures** are placed into the superior aspect of the falx to facilitate gentle dural-sinus retraction and mobilization away from the midline — critical for expanding the interhemispheric corridor
- Telfa covers and protects the normal hemisphere

## Intradural Work

### Falx Incision ("T" Incision)

- A **"T"-shaped incision** in the falx cerebri exposes the contralateral medial hemisphere (precuneus)
- **Lumbar CSF drainage** is crucial here for mobilizing the unaffected hemisphere and minimizing retraction injury
- The **lower limb** of the T is extended to the level of the corpus callosum; the **inferior sagittal sinus** is coagulated and transected
- The **straight sinus** and other relevant deep draining veins are localized and protected using neuronavigation
- If the vertical falx incision approaches the straight sinus, complete it **obliquely from posterior to anterior**, so that reflecting the falcine flaps exposes more posterior precuneus generously

### Transfalcine Retraction and Corticotomy

- Additional **sutures on the falcine dural flaps** mobilize the contralateral hemisphere and increase the transfalcine working angle toward the ipsilateral atrium
- **Cottonoid patties** protect the contralateral hemisphere against the gentle retraction vector of the sutures
- **Fixed retractors are NOT used**
- A **transprecuneus corticotomy** is carried on the contralateral medial surface, entering the atrium of the (ipsilateral) lateral ventricle
- Intra-atrial tumors (meningiomas, AVMs, gliomas) are then resected. Choroidal feeders are identified and coagulated early

## Closure

- After hemostasis, a **ventricular catheter** is implanted to drain intraventricular debris and decrease hydrocephalus risk
- **Falcine dural flaps returned** to original position
- **Convexity dura** is closed

## Postoperative Considerations

- **ICU observation** for 1-2 days before ward transfer
- **Avoid hypertension** and overmedication with narcotics
- **Ventriculostomy** usually removed on postoperative day 2
- Continue **anticonvulsants**

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- **Park-bench + dependent normal hemisphere** allows gravity retraction to minimize injury to the unaffected hemisphere
- PITTA is **technically challenging** due to depth and narrow corridor — use selectively and judiciously
- The approach emphasizes **working angles over working space** for difficult-to-reach tumors
- **Meningiomas** can be devascularized early via direct exposure of choroidal feeders
- Interhemispheric transfalcine route provides **early proximal control** over choroidal and PCA feeders for medial atrial AVMs
- **Do NOT manipulate or coagulate the choroid plexus aggressively** — risk of ischemia and dense visual field deficits
- If the contralateral hemisphere has numerous large parasagittal veins in the region, **abandon PITTA** for a transcortical SPL route

## Complications

- **Venous infarction** from parasagittal vein ligation
- **Visual field deficit** from choroid plexus injury or optic radiation retraction
- **Straight sinus / inferior sagittal sinus injury** during falx incision
- **Hydrocephalus** from intraventricular debris (mitigated by post-resection EVD)
- **Seizures** — covered by anticonvulsant prophylaxis
