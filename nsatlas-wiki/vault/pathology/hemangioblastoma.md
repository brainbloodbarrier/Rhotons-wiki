# Hemangioblastoma

Hemangioblastoma is a histologically benign, highly vascular tumor that accounts for 1–2 % of intracranial tumors and 7–12 % of posterior fossa tumors in adults. It is the most common primary tumor of the adult cerebellar hemisphere. Despite its benign grade, it can produce devastating mass effect, life-threatening hemorrhage, and — in von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease — represents a lifelong burden of multifocal CNS involvement requiring repeated surgery and surveillance.

## Epidemiology

- 1–2 % of all intracranial tumors; up to 10 % in the adult posterior fossa.
- Peak age: 30–50 in sporadic disease; 20–40 in VHL.
- Sporadic forms outnumber VHL-associated by ~ 4 : 1 overall, though up to 30 % of cerebellar hemangioblastomas in young adults are VHL-associated.
- Spinal cord hemangioblastomas constitute ~ 3 % of intramedullary tumors, with a strong VHL association (> 50 %).
- Roughly 25 % of VHL patients harbor multiple CNS hemangioblastomas at diagnosis.

## Origin and Pathology

The cell of origin is debated; modern consensus favors a hemangioblast-like mesenchymal stromal cell with vascular and stromal differentiation. The tumor is composed of two principal elements:

- **Stromal cells** — large, vacuolated, lipid-rich; the genuinely neoplastic component carrying VHL biallelic inactivation.
- **Vascular component** — abundant capillary network with thin-walled vessels, sometimes showing hemangiomatous architecture.

Inactivation of the **VHL tumor-suppressor gene** (3p25-26) is the defining molecular event. Loss of pVHL function fails to ubiquitinate hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) under normoxia, producing constitutive HIF-1α / HIF-2α signaling, with downstream upregulation of VEGF, EPO, PDGF, and TGF-α — the basis of both the tumor's vascularity and the secondary erythrocytosis sometimes observed.

WHO 2021 classifies hemangioblastoma as **grade 1**. Anaplastic transformation is not described; recurrence is the principal concern, especially in VHL.

## von Hippel-Lindau Disease

VHL (autosomal dominant; chromosome 3p25-26) predisposes to a constellation of tumors:

- **CNS hemangioblastomas** (cerebellum, brainstem, spinal cord, retina): 60–80 %.
- **Retinal hemangioblastomas** (often the earliest manifestation): 25–60 %.
- **Renal cell carcinoma** (clear-cell): 25–60 %.
- **Pheochromocytoma**: 10–20 %.
- **Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors and cysts**.
- **Endolymphatic sac tumors** (papillary cystadenoma).
- **Epididymal cystadenoma** (men), broad-ligament cysts (women).

VHL clinical subtypes (Type 1, 2A, 2B, 2C) reflect mutation type and predict pheochromocytoma risk; genetic testing informs surveillance scheduling. Patients diagnosed with a hemangioblastoma in **young adults, multiple lesions, retinal involvement, or family history** require formal VHL evaluation.

## Anatomic Distribution

- **Cerebellum** (~ 50 %): hemispheric > vermian. Often a large cyst with a small mural nodule abutting the pial surface.
- **Spinal cord** (~ 25 %): intramedullary, often with associated syrinx; cervicothoracic predominance; pial surface attachment is the rule.
- **Brainstem** (~ 10 %): obex / floor of fourth ventricle / dorsal medulla, often pial. The most operatively challenging location.
- **Supratentorial** (< 5 %): rare; raises suspicion of metastatic clear-cell renal carcinoma in the differential.
- **Retina**: managed by ophthalmology with laser / cryotherapy / radiation; not surgical neurosurgical territory but always worth examining preoperatively in young patients.

## Clinical Presentation

### Cerebellar
- Headache, nausea, vomiting, gait ataxia, dysmetria.
- Obstructive [[hydrocephalus]] from fourth-ventricular compression — papilledema, depressed consciousness; emergent in some cases.
- Sudden deterioration from intratumoral hemorrhage is uncommon but described.

### Brainstem (medullary)
- Lower-cranial-nerve deficits, sensory level, gait disturbance.
- Sleep apnea, autonomic instability.

### Spinal cord
- Dermatomal sensory disturbance, paresthesias, gait ataxia, weakness.
- Syrinx-related symptoms predominate; the tumor itself may be small relative to the syrinx.
- Bowel / bladder dysfunction in advanced disease.

### Systemic clue: erythrocytosis
- 5–20 % of patients have secondary polycythemia from tumor EPO production. Resolves after resection — a useful tumor-marker analog and a screening trigger in VHL families.

## Imaging

### MRI
- **T1**: cyst is hypointense; nodule is iso- to hypointense to brain.
- **T1 + gadolinium**: nodule shows **intense, uniform, early enhancement**; cyst wall typically does not enhance (reactive gliosis, not tumor).
- **T2 / FLAIR**: cyst hyperintense; nodule has **prominent flow voids** from feeding and draining vessels — the "tumor-like AVM" appearance.
- **Susceptibility-weighted imaging**: useful for the spinal-cord variant where small lesions are easily missed.

The classic "**cyst with mural nodule**" pattern is diagnostic but not pathognomonic — pilocytic astrocytoma in younger patients shares the appearance, and metastatic clear-cell renal cell carcinoma can mimic it (especially in known VHL patients, where biopsy may be needed to distinguish).

### Catheter angiography
- Indicated for large lesions, multiple feeders, or planned **preoperative embolization** of cerebellar / spinal-cord tumors with deep-seated arterial supply.
- Demonstrates the tumor blush, defines feeding pedicles, and identifies veins to be preserved or sacrificed.

### CT
- Cyst is hypodense; nodule sometimes hyperdense from the high vascular component; calcifications uncommon.
- Bony scalloping may be seen with chronic spinal-cord tumors.

### Whole-CNS screening
- VHL patients (or any patient with multiple lesions, retinal disease, or family history) require gadolinium-enhanced MRI of brain **and** entire spine — small intramedullary lesions are easily missed without dedicated thin-slice imaging.

## Differential Diagnosis

- **Metastatic renal cell carcinoma** — same patient population in VHL; biopsy may be required.
- **Pilocytic astrocytoma** — cyst-with-mural-nodule, but typically younger patient and more intense T2 hyperintensity in the nodule.
- **Pyogenic abscess** — central restricted diffusion, ring-enhancing, distinguished from the solid enhancing nodule of hemangioblastoma; see [[brain-abscess]].
- **Cavernous malformation** of the spinal cord (intramedullary differential).
- **Solitary metastasis** (in elderly patients with cerebellar mass).
- **Astrocytoma** of brainstem or spinal cord (intramedullary differential).

## Management

### Surgical resection (cornerstone)

The principal treatment for symptomatic or growing hemangioblastomas. Operative principles mirror those of an [[arteriovenous-malformation]] resection:

1. **Identify and control feeding pedicles before disturbing the nodule.** Hemangioblastomas exsanguinate through their feeders; biopsying or coagulating the surface produces uncontrolled bleeding into a high-pressure tumor bed.
2. **Resect the nodule en bloc.** Dissect circumferentially in the gliotic plane between tumor and parenchyma. Bipolar shrinkage of the tumor is a tactic for small lesions but never the primary plan.
3. **Drain the cyst, leave the cyst wall.** The cyst wall is reactive glia; en-bloc nodule resection eliminates the source of fluid and the cyst regresses spontaneously.
4. **Preserve draining veins until the inflow is controlled** — premature venous sacrifice risks intraoperative hemorrhage and cerebellar swelling.

### Approach selection

- **Cerebellar hemispheric**: posterior fossa craniotomy with paramedian or lateral suboccipital approach; choose corridor by lesion location and pial surface presentation. The [[paramedian-supracerebellar-craniotomy]] or a lateral suboccipital trajectory works well for most superior cerebellar lesions; the [[suboccipital-craniotomy]] (median or lateral) for inferior cerebellar disease.
- **Cerebellar vermian**: midline suboccipital craniotomy.
- **Brainstem (dorsal medulla / obex)**: telovelar approach; preserve the floor of the fourth ventricle and lower-cranial-nerve nuclei.
- **Spinal-cord intramedullary**: midline myelotomy for dorsal lesions; lateral myelotomy or pial dissection for laterally based pial lesions; intraoperative neuromonitoring (SSEPs, MEPs, D-wave) is mandatory. See [[intramedullary-spinal-cord-tumor-resection]] for technique details.

### Preoperative embolization

Considered for:
- Large cerebellar tumors with deep arterial supply.
- Brainstem tumors with high vascularity.
- Spinal hemangioblastomas with ASA-fed nodules where staged devascularization simplifies surgery.

Embolization risks include stroke, posterior fossa edema, and tumor swelling — performed only with surgery to follow within 24–72 hours.

### Stereotactic radiosurgery

A reasonable alternative or adjunct in:
- Surgically inaccessible lesions.
- Multiple small asymptomatic lesions in VHL where serial surgery is impractical.
- Postoperative residual or recurrent disease.

Local control rates 70–90 % at 5 years; tumors with associated cysts respond less reliably as the cyst component is independent of the radiation effect.

### Cyst aspiration alone

Not a definitive treatment — cysts re-accumulate unless the nodule is removed or radiated. Reserved for emergency decompression as a temporizing maneuver before definitive surgery.

### Adjuncts and emerging therapy

- **Belzutifan** (HIF-2α inhibitor) — FDA-approved for VHL-associated CNS hemangioblastomas, RCC, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors. Demonstrated tumor shrinkage in VHL hemangioblastomas; an emerging option for surgically high-risk lesions or systemic disease control. Hypoxia and anemia are notable adverse effects.

### CSF diversion

Obstructive hydrocephalus from a posterior fossa hemangioblastoma may require an [[external-ventricular-drain-placement]] preoperatively; many resolve after tumor resection without permanent shunting.

## Complications

- **Intraoperative hemorrhage** — the dominant operative risk; controlled by feeder-first dissection.
- **Posterior fossa edema and swelling**, particularly after embolization or piecemeal resection.
- **Cerebellar deficit** — dysmetria, ataxia, dysarthria; usually transient.
- **Lower-cranial-nerve palsy** in brainstem / floor-of-fourth-ventricle lesions.
- **Spinal cord deficit** in intramedullary resections — myelotomy site, manipulation, and proximity to long tracts.
- **CSF leak / pseudomeningocele** in suboccipital exposures.
- **Hydrocephalus** post-resection — uncommon but warrants surveillance.

## Prognosis and Surveillance

- Sporadic disease: gross-total resection is generally curative; recurrence in well-resected lesions is rare.
- VHL: lifelong surveillance is the rule — annual to biennial MRI of brain and spine, plus systemic surveillance for renal, adrenal, pancreatic, retinal, and inner-ear lesions.
- Operate **symptomatic** lesions and **growing asymptomatic** lesions; static asymptomatic lesions are watched, as the cumulative surgical morbidity of multiple resections in VHL is real.
- Genetic counseling for VHL patients and at-risk family members; predictive testing of children before surveillance schedule kicks in.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Treat the hemangioblastoma like an [[arteriovenous-malformation]].** Feeders first, nodule en bloc, veins last — entering the nodule head-on is the classic catastrophic error.
2. **Drain the cyst, resect the nodule, leave the cyst wall.** Reactive gliosis is not tumor; pursuing it adds morbidity without oncologic gain.
3. **Embolize selectively, not reflexively.** A 2-cm cerebellar hemispheric lesion with a clean pial surface rarely benefits; a 4-cm tumor with deep PICA / SCA feeders does.
4. **In VHL, do not chase every lesion.** Asymptomatic, non-growing lesions can be watched; the surgical decision integrates symptom trajectory, growth rate, location, and the patient's cumulative operative history.
5. **Examine the retina before operating on a young adult.** A hemangioblastoma in a 28-year-old without family history should still trigger fundoscopy, genetic counseling, and whole-CNS screening before the index surgery.
6. **Bring the systemic team in early.** Renal cell surveillance, adrenal screening for pheochromocytoma, and audiology workup do not wait for the post-operative recovery — they are part of presenting with VHL.
7. **Belzutifan is now in the algorithm.** Discuss with neuro-oncology in any VHL patient where surgery is high-risk or where systemic disease control is needed; it is no longer a research-only option.
