# Radiation Necrosis

Cerebral radiation necrosis (RN) is the signature delayed complication of therapeutic brain radiation — an evolving focus of parenchymal injury that appears at or near a prior high-dose radiation volume, typically **6 months to 3 years** after treatment. Incidence is 5–25% for single-fraction [[stereotactic-biopsy|stereotactic]] radiosurgery and ~5% for conventional fractionated radiotherapy. The core clinical problem is not the histology itself — tissue injury is well-understood — but the fact that RN is MRI-indistinguishable from **recurrent tumor** in the very patient populations who most commonly undergo brain radiation (glioma, metastasis, AVM).

## Pathophysiology

Radiation delivers DNA damage, but delayed injury is chiefly vascular:

- **Endothelial cell depletion** → microvascular dropout, chronic hypoperfusion, infarction.
- **Secondary demyelination** and white-matter injury.
- **Fibrinoid necrosis** of small arterioles — pathognomonic histologic finding.
- Inflammatory cascade with VEGF upregulation → edema and progressive enlargement (the substrate for bevacizumab efficacy).

Risk factors: large irradiated volume (V12 >10 cm³ for SRS), high biologically-effective dose, concurrent chemotherapy (especially temozolomide), prior radiation, and pediatric age.

## Clinical presentation

- Asymptomatic imaging finding on routine surveillance — most common presentation.
- Focal deficit corresponding to lesion location.
- Progressive headache, nausea, or cognitive decline as peri-lesional edema accumulates.
- Seizures (more frequent with supratentorial RN).

## Diagnostic dilemma — RN vs tumor recurrence

Conventional contrast MRI shows an enhancing lesion with surrounding T2/FLAIR hyperintensity — a signal shared by tumor progression. Supportive imaging techniques:

| Modality | RN signature | Tumor recurrence signature |
|---|---|---|
| **MR perfusion (rCBV)** | Decreased / normal | Elevated |
| **MR spectroscopy** | ↓ NAA, ↓ choline, lipid/lactate peaks | ↑ Cho/NAA ratio |
| **DWI / ADC** | Variable; often facilitated diffusion | Restricted diffusion |
| **FDG-PET** | Hypometabolism | Hypermetabolism |
| **Amino-acid PET (MET/FET)** | Low uptake | High uptake |
| **Perfusion DSC**, **DCE** | Low K-trans | High K-trans |

Serial MRI (6–8 weeks) showing stability or regression favors RN; continued enlargement favors recurrence. Biopsy — often [[stereotactic-biopsy|stereotactic]] — is the definitive arbiter for ambiguous cases. Mixed lesions (RN + residual tumor) are common in practice.

## Treatment ladder

### Tier 1 — Observation
- Small, asymptomatic lesions with perfusion/PET favoring RN.
- Serial imaging at 6–8 week intervals; most RN stabilizes or regresses spontaneously.

### Tier 2 — Corticosteroids
- Dexamethasone 4–16 mg/day tapered to lowest effective dose.
- Addresses peri-lesional vasogenic edema rather than the lesion itself.
- Long-term use limited by cushingoid side effects; indication to escalate when steroid dependence emerges.

### Tier 3 — Bevacizumab
- Anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody; randomized trial data (Levin 2011) demonstrate lesion shrinkage and symptomatic improvement.
- Typical regimen 5–10 mg/kg IV every 2–3 weeks × 4 doses.
- Side effects: hypertension, proteinuria, delayed wound healing, rare thromboembolism; caution in patients requiring surgery within 4–6 weeks.

### Tier 4 — Hyperbaric oxygen
- 100% O₂ at 2–2.4 atm × 20–30 sessions; mechanism via enhanced angiogenesis and collagen deposition.
- Evidence is observational; used as salvage when bevacizumab is contraindicated or ineffective.

### Tier 5 — Laser-interstitial thermal therapy (LITT)
- MRI-guided thermal coagulation of the necrotic lesion; disrupts the inflammatory cascade and debulks mass effect.
- Minimally invasive alternative to open resection in deep or eloquent locations.

### Tier 6 — Surgical resection
- For mass effect, symptomatic edema refractory to medical therapy, and ambiguous histology.
- Standard craniotomy principles; see [[brain-tumors-general-principles]] and [[cranial-approaches-general-principles]].
- Hemostasis and brain protection are paramount in a post-radiation field; see [[intraoperative-hemostasis]] and [[brain-mobilization-and-protection]].
- [[decompressive-craniectomy]] is rarely indicated but considered for malignant edema.

## Post-treatment complications

Related surgical and peri-operative considerations:

- **Wound healing** in a radiated scalp — dehiscence risk elevated; see [[scalp-incisions]] and [[suturing-and-closure]].
- **Dural integrity** — post-radiation dura is friable; meticulous [[dural-opening-and-closure]].
- **Hemostasis** — friable neovasculature requires careful bipolar control.
- Steroid-related hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, and immunosuppression.

## Prognosis

- Most non-progressive RN stabilizes spontaneously in 6–12 months.
- Bevacizumab achieves radiographic response in 60–80% of treated lesions.
- Surgical resection is curative for isolated mass-effect RN but carries a 5–10% perioperative morbidity in a radiated field.
