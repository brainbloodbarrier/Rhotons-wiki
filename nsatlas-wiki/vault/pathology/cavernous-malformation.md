# Cavernous Malformation

The cerebral cavernous malformation (CCM) is the most common angiographically occult vascular lesion of the central nervous system and one of the most clinically heterogeneous: many are entirely incidental, others produce devastating brainstem hemorrhages from the first event. Modern management has matured around three observations: (1) hemorrhage rates are highly location- and history-dependent, (2) the developmental venous anomaly that often accompanies a sporadic cavernoma must never be sacrificed, and (3) brainstem surgery — even the right operation through the right safe-entry zone — is unforgiving of timing errors.

## Epidemiology

- MRI prevalence 0.4–0.9 % in unselected populations.
- Account for 5–13 % of all CNS vascular malformations.
- Many are **incidental** at MRI obtained for unrelated reasons.
- ~ 20 % are familial (autosomal dominant); 80 % sporadic.
- Mean age at presentation 30–40 years for sporadic; familial cases often present earlier.
- No clear sex predilection.

## Pathology and Pathogenesis

A cavernous malformation is composed of dilated, thin-walled vascular channels lined by a single layer of endothelium with little or no smooth muscle or elastic tissue. The defining histologic features:

- **No intervening brain parenchyma** between the vascular channels — the histologic distinction from a [[arteriovenous-malformation]] (AVM), which has interspersed neural tissue.
- **Hemosiderin-stained reactive parenchymal rim** outside the lesion — the imaging "halo" sign.
- **Chronic intermittent hemorrhage** at the microscopic level, with organized clot at multiple stages of evolution.
- **No high-flow shunting** — angiographically occult; the lesion is below the resolution of catheter angiography in most cases.

Sporadic cavernomas frequently coexist with a **developmental venous anomaly (DVA)**: the DVA is a normal variant of venous drainage that can never be safely sacrificed; sacrificing the DVA produces venous infarction in territory it serves, often catastrophic. CCM resection respects the DVA in every case.

### Genetics
- **CCM1 / KRIT1** (chromosome 7q): most common familial form; classic Hispanic-American kindreds.
- **CCM2 / MGC4607** (7p): less common.
- **CCM3 / PDCD10** (3q): more aggressive — earlier presentation, more lesions, higher hemorrhage rate; multiple meningiomas and skin lesions can coexist.
- Familial disease is characterized by **multiple lesions** (often dozens) and a higher new-lesion-formation rate; sporadic cases typically have a single lesion.
- Genetic testing is recommended when family history is positive, when multiple lesions are present, or when a single lesion presents at an unusually young age.

## Clinical Presentation

### Symptomatic patterns
- **Seizures** (40–50 %) — particularly common with supratentorial cortical lesions; the hemosiderin rim is highly epileptogenic.
- **Hemorrhage** (~ 30 %) — focal deficit by location, headache, seizure, sometimes coma in brainstem disease.
- **Focal deficits without overt hemorrhage** (10–20 %) — small infarcts or progressive growth from microbleeds.
- **Incidental** at MRI for an unrelated reason — increasingly common with widespread imaging.

### Location-specific features
- **Supratentorial cortical / subcortical**: seizures dominate; focal deficits depend on eloquence.
- **Deep supratentorial** (basal ganglia, thalamus): focal deficits, sometimes movement disorders.
- **Brainstem**: cranial nerve palsies, long-tract signs, ataxia, autonomic instability — even small lesions are potentially devastating.
- **Cerebellar**: ataxia, headache, hydrocephalus from posterior fossa mass effect.
- **Spinal cord (intramedullary)**: progressive myelopathy, sometimes acute deficits with hemorrhage; see [[spinal-cord-cavernous-malformation-resection]] for the surgical synthesis.

## Imaging

### MRI (modality of choice)
- **T1**: heterogeneous, often "popcorn" appearance from blood products at multiple ages.
- **T2**: heterogeneous central core surrounded by a **hypointense hemosiderin halo** — the diagnostic signature.
- **SWI / GRE / T2\***: most sensitive; **blooming** of the hemosiderin halo and detection of small additional lesions ("micro-cavernomas") that are invisible on T2 alone. SWI screening is mandatory in familial cases.
- **Post-contrast**: minimal or no enhancement of the lesion; the associated DVA enhances avidly and is best seen on delayed venous-phase post-contrast or contrast-enhanced SWI.
- **DWI**: typically not restricted; useful to differentiate from cystic / necrotic tumor and abscess (see [[brain-abscess]]).

### Other modalities
- **Catheter angiography**: occult — does not visualize the cavernoma itself but may identify an associated DVA.
- **CT**: often subtle hyperdensity from chronic blood; not the modality for diagnosis or surveillance.
- **Functional MRI / DTI**: critical for eloquent-area lesions to plan corridor and counsel risk.

### Whole-CNS screening
- All patients with multiple lesions, family history, or known CCM mutation should have **whole-brain SWI** to enumerate and follow lesions.
- Spinal MRI is reasonable in symptomatic familial cases but not universally screened.

## Natural History

The hemorrhage rate is location- and history-dependent — the single most clinically actionable observation in CCM management:

| Context | Annual hemorrhage rate per lesion |
|---|---|
| Supratentorial, no prior hemorrhage | ~ 0.4–0.6 % |
| Brainstem, no prior hemorrhage | ~ 1–2 % |
| Any location, after a hemorrhage | 4–6 % (clusters in first 2–5 years; "temporal clustering") |
| Brainstem, after a hemorrhage | 5–30 % depending on series |
| CCM3 mutation | Substantially higher across all locations |

**Temporal clustering** — recurrent bleeds tend to occur within 2–5 years of the index event and then plateau — has profound implications for the timing of brainstem resection.

## Management

### Conservative
- **Asymptomatic supratentorial cavernoma** in non-eloquent location, identified incidentally → observation with periodic MRI.
- Avoid anticoagulation if alternatives exist; modern data suggest the absolute risk of CCM hemorrhage on anticoagulation is small but non-zero, weighed against the indication for anticoagulation.
- Antiepileptic drug therapy for seizures; surgery considered when seizures are medically refractory.
- Family screening, genetic counseling, lifestyle counseling for high-risk lesion-specific behaviors.

### Surgical resection — indications

#### Supratentorial
- **Symptomatic seizure-onset cavernoma** with the lesion in or adjacent to the seizure focus, particularly when antiepileptic therapy fails.
- **Hemorrhagic cavernoma** producing focal deficit, when the lesion is reachable without crossing eloquent cortex.
- **Eloquent supratentorial location** (motor, language, vision) — surgery indicated for symptomatic disease only after careful corridor planning with fMRI and DTI.

#### Brainstem
- **The two-hemorrhage rule** (relative, modern): defer surgery for the first non-disabling brainstem bleed; consider resection after a second symptomatic hemorrhage, given temporal clustering.
- **Pial / ependymal-surface lesions** that abut a safe-entry zone are reasonable surgical candidates after a single bleed.
- **Deep brainstem lesions without surface presentation** — surgery is high-risk; many groups defer or refer for radiosurgery (acknowledging its limited evidence base).
- The decision is highly individualized; outcome predictors include lesion size, surface presentation, surgeon experience, and patient functional status before the bleed.

#### Spinal cord intramedullary
- Symptomatic lesions abutting the pial surface are reasonable surgical candidates with the techniques established for [[intramedullary-spinal-cord-tumor-resection]] and [[spinal-cord-cavernous-malformation-resection]].
- Deep intramedullary lesions in eloquent regions (cervical spinal cord, motor tracts) carry high deficit risk.

### Surgical principles
- **Identify and preserve the DVA** — the single most important sporadic-CCM operative principle. The DVA is a normal venous variant; sacrificing it produces venous infarction.
- **Approach through the most direct, least eloquent corridor** — sometimes a small distance through "silent" cortex outweighs a longer trajectory through critical territory.
- **Resect the hemosiderin-stained rim** for seizure cases — the rim is epileptogenic, and incomplete rim resection compromises seizure freedom.
- For the cavernoma itself, a **complete circumferential dissection** is the goal; incomplete resection leaves residual tissue and a high recurrence risk.
- **Brainstem safe-entry zones**: lateral mesencephalic sulcus, supratrigeminal zone, suprafacial / infrafacial triangles, supracollicular zone, peri-trigeminal, lateral medullary, dorsal midline pontomedullary — Spetzler's mapping is the standard reference.
- For brainstem and spinal-cord cases, **intraoperative neuromonitoring** (SSEPs, MEPs, BAEPs, cranial nerves) is mandatory.

### Stereotactic radiosurgery
- Has a defined but limited role; reserved for surgically inaccessible symptomatic lesions, particularly deep brainstem.
- Putative benefit accrues over years (decreased annual bleed rate); a real **radiation-induced bleed risk in the first 2–3 years** must be discussed.
- Evidence base is observational; randomized comparison with conservative management does not exist.
- Whole-cohort meta-analyses have not consistently demonstrated benefit; it is a niche tool, not first-line.

### Emerging therapy
- **Statins** — pre-clinical and small clinical evidence of reduced hemorrhage in familial CCM3 disease; not standard.
- **Propranolol** — early trials in symptomatic CCM with mixed results.
- **Targeted therapy** (Rho-kinase inhibitors) — in clinical trial.

## Complications and Pitfalls

- **DVA sacrifice** — the avoidable disaster; venous infarction can render an otherwise textbook cavernoma resection a catastrophe.
- **Incomplete resection** with residual tissue and high recurrence and re-bleed risk; particularly important for hemosiderin-rim resection in seizure cases.
- **Brainstem deficit from corridor selection** rather than the cavernoma itself — the cost of a chosen safe-entry zone.
- **New-onset seizures** after supratentorial resection (uncommon if hemosiderin rim is resected).
- **Hydrocephalus** in posterior fossa lesions — may require [[external-ventricular-drain-placement]] perioperatively.

## Special Populations

### Familial (CCM1 / 2 / 3)
- Multiple lesions, lifelong surveillance.
- New lesions develop at ~ 0.5–1 per year; growth and hemorrhage of pre-existing lesions are independent risks.
- CCM3 is the most aggressive; pediatric onset and high lesion burden.
- Genetic counseling is essential; cascade testing of first-degree relatives.

### Pediatric
- Brainstem lesions present a particular challenge; outcomes in experienced hands are reasonable but disability after a second hemorrhage is often severe.
- Long surveillance horizon; cumulative radiation exposure from radiosurgery is a concern.

### Pregnancy
- Hemorrhage during pregnancy is described; the absolute risk is small but real.
- Vaginal delivery is generally safe; cesarean delivery indicated for obstetric reasons or specific neurosurgical contexts (recent hemorrhage, brainstem location with neurologic instability).

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Spare the DVA, always.** The associated DVA is a normal variant — sacrificing it converts a routine cavernoma resection into a venous infarction. Identify on contrast-enhanced MRI / SWI before opening.
2. **Resect the hemosiderin rim for seizure cases.** Incomplete rim resection leaves the epileptogenic substrate and compromises seizure freedom.
3. **Wait for the second brainstem bleed.** The two-hemorrhage rule is not absolute, but for deep lesions without a safe-entry-zone surface presentation, the first bleed alone is rarely sufficient indication.
4. **Pick the corridor first.** Spetzler's safe-entry zones map the brainstem; choose the corridor before deciding the procedure, not after.
5. **Trust the SWI for familial screening.** Standard T2 misses small cavernomas in familial disease; SWI is mandatory and the basis for surveillance protocols.
6. **Counsel realistically about radiosurgery.** It is not a no-risk alternative; the early-year radiation-bleed risk and the absence of strong randomized data deserve clear explanation.
7. **Re-image early after hemorrhage.** Temporal clustering is real; serial MRIs in the first 2–3 years inform shared decision-making more than any single number.
8. **Spinal cavernomas follow the same principles.** Pial surface presentation, [[intramedullary-spinal-cord-tumor-resection]] technique, hemosiderin rim resection — but the spinal cord is even less forgiving than the brainstem.
