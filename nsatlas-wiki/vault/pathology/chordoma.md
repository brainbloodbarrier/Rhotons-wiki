# Chordoma

Chordoma is a rare, slow-growing, locally aggressive malignancy arising from primitive notochord remnants along the midline craniospinal axis. It accounts for ~ 1 % of all primary bone tumors and < 0.2 % of all CNS tumors but punches well above its frequency in surgical complexity: clival chordomas in particular sit at the convergence of brainstem, ICA, cavernous sinus, optic apparatus, and lower cranial nerves, and a "clean margin" resection is geometrically impossible. The modern paradigm pairs **maximal safe surgical cytoreduction** with **high-dose particle radiotherapy** (proton or carbon ion); chemotherapy plays a minor role and targeted molecular therapy is in active development.

## Epidemiology

- Incidence ~ 1 per million per year.
- Adult predominance (median age 50–60 y), but clival and cervical chordomas have a younger peak (3rd–4th decade) and rare pediatric presentations.
- Male predominance modest (1.5–2 : 1).
- Distribution by location:
  - **Sacrococcygeal**: ~ 35 % (adults).
  - **Clival / spheno-occipital**: ~ 35 % (children and young adults disproportionately).
  - **Mobile spine** (cervical > lumbar > thoracic): ~ 30 %.

## Pathology and Origin

The notochord is the embryonic midline structure that induces the development of the spinal column and skull base. Normally, notochord cells regress by the late embryonic period, leaving only the nucleus pulposus of intervertebral discs as a remnant. **Chordomas arise from notochord cells that fail to regress**, predominantly at the cranial (clival / spheno-occipital) and caudal (sacrococcygeal) termini where notochord material is most abundant.

### Histologic patterns
- **Classic chordoma** (most common): physaliphorous (vacuolated, "bubble-bearing") cells in a myxoid matrix; **T-brachyury** transcription factor expression is **pathognomonic** and the gold-standard immunohistochemical confirmation.
- **Chondroid chordoma**: hyaline-cartilage-like areas; better prognosis than classic; can be misdiagnosed as chondrosarcoma without brachyury staining.
- **Dedifferentiated chordoma**: rare; sarcomatoid component; aggressive; metastasizes more readily.
- **Poorly-differentiated** with SMARCB1 / INI1 loss: aggressive, more common in pediatric clival chordomas.

### Molecular features
- **TBXT (T-brachyury)** is the lineage-defining transcription factor and a therapeutic target (vaccination trials).
- **CDKN2A** loss, **PI3K-mTOR** activation, **EZH2** overexpression — emerging targets.
- **Familial chordoma**: rare germline TBXT duplication produces a hereditary predisposition.

## Anatomic Distribution and Clinical Presentation

### Clival / spheno-occipital (~ 35 %)
- **Headache** (most common — bifrontal, retro-orbital, occipital depending on extension).
- **Diplopia** from sixth-nerve palsy (Dorello canal compression as the sixth nerve enters the cavernous sinus) — often the first specific sign.
- **Other cranial nerve palsies** (III, IV, V) from cavernous sinus extension.
- **Visual decline** from optic apparatus compression in suprasellar extension.
- **Endocrinopathy** if pituitary stalk involved (rare).
- **Lower cranial nerve dysfunction** (IX-XII) if extending toward the foramen magnum / jugular foramen.
- **Brainstem compression** in advanced disease — gait disturbance, long-tract signs.
- **Pediatric clival chordomas** often poorly-differentiated and aggressive.

### Sacrococcygeal (~ 35 %)
- **Lower back / sacral pain** dominant complaint, often longstanding before diagnosis.
- **Saddle anesthesia, sphincter dysfunction** in advanced disease (cauda equina compromise — see [[hydrocephalus]] differential overlap is minimal).
- **Bowel / bladder dysfunction** late.
- **Palpable mass** on rectal exam in advanced cases.
- Median diagnostic delay 6–12 months — pain often misattributed to mechanical low-back-pain syndromes.

### Mobile spine (~ 30 %)
- Local pain, radiculopathy, myelopathy.
- Cervical chordoma can extend retropharyngeally — odynophagia, voice change.

## Imaging

### MRI (modality of choice for soft-tissue characterization)
- **T1**: iso- to hypointense relative to brain; areas of high T1 signal from intratumoral hemorrhage or proteinaceous content.
- **T2**: characteristically **hyperintense** ("very bright") — reflecting the myxoid matrix; helpful distinguishing from chondrosarcoma (also bright but typically calcified) and meningioma (less bright).
- **Post-gadolinium**: heterogeneous enhancement, often "honeycomb" pattern.
- Defines extension: cavernous sinus invasion, brainstem compression, ICA encasement, sphenoid sinus extension, prepontine cisterns involvement.

### CT
- **Bone destruction** is the cardinal CT feature — lytic, expansile, often with retained bony fragments giving a "moth-eaten" appearance.
- Calcifications within the soft-tissue mass in 30–50 % (more in chondroid variant).
- Defines bony anatomy for surgical planning and post-radiation surveillance.

### Whole-spine imaging
- Multifocal disease and skip lesions are uncommon but described — image the entire neuraxis at diagnosis in atypical or pediatric cases.

### Differential on imaging (clival)
- **Chondrosarcoma** of skull base — typically more lateral / petroclival, also T2-bright, similar enhancement; histology and brachyury distinguish.
- **Meningioma** of clival or petroclival region — dural-based, broad attachment, dural tail; usually less T2-bright.
- **Pituitary macroadenoma** — sellar-suprasellar epicenter; chordoma can extend into the sella but the bony destruction pattern differs.
- **Plasmacytoma / multiple myeloma** of clivus — diffuse marrow involvement on MRI.
- **Metastasis** — multifocal in most cases, primary sought.

## Management

### Surgery — cytoreduction is the priority

**Maximal safe resection** is the strongest modifiable predictor of progression-free survival. The goal is **gross total resection** when geometrically feasible (sacrococcygeal en bloc; selected mobile-spine en bloc) or **maximal intralesional debulking** when en bloc is impossible (clival, most spinal locations).

#### Clival chordoma
- **Endoscopic endonasal approach** (EEA) is the modern default for midline clival lesions: directly to the clivus through the sphenoid sinus, with extended posterior, lateral, and superior corridors as needed.
- **Transcranial approaches** (anterior petrosectomy, posterior petrosectomy / Kawase, far-lateral, extreme lateral) reserved for lateral, trans-tentorial, or large lesions extending beyond endonasal reach.
- Reconstruction of skull-base defect with vascularized nasoseptal flap is the standard for EEA cases.
- Goal of clival resection: gross total when feasible, otherwise maximal cytoreduction preserving brainstem, ICA, lower cranial nerves, and optic apparatus.

#### Sacrococcygeal chordoma
- **En bloc sacrectomy** with the goal of clear margins — the only reliable curative operation for chordoma.
- Combined posterior or anterior-posterior approach depending on extent.
- Sacrifice of S1-S3 nerve roots (and, in extensive cases, S4-S5) accepted for oncologic clearance, with predictable bowel / bladder / sexual function consequences.
- The decision to preserve vs sacrifice nerve roots is the principal pre-operative discussion with the patient.

#### Mobile spine chordoma
- **En bloc spondylectomy** (Tomita / Boriani technique) for clear-margin resection in selected cases.
- Reconstruction with cage and instrumentation; demanding, multi-stage operations.

### Radiotherapy
- **High-dose particle therapy** (proton, carbon ion) is the **adjuvant standard** for skull-base and spinal chordomas.
- Doses ≥ 70 Gy equivalent (78 Gy in some protocols) — far above conventional photon thresholds.
- Particle therapy allows the high doses required while sparing brainstem, optic apparatus, and other critical structures (Bragg peak deposition).
- Photon IMRT is used when particle therapy is unavailable but with reduced dose ceilings and inferior local control.
- Adjuvant timing: typically 4–8 weeks post-operative, after wound healing and reconstruction stabilization.

### Chemotherapy
- Conventional chemotherapy has very limited efficacy.
- Reserved for unresectable / metastatic disease as palliative therapy, often combined with targeted agents.

### Targeted therapy and clinical trials
- **EZH2 inhibitors** (tazemetostat) — early-phase trials.
- **mTOR inhibitors** (rapamycin, everolimus) — combined with cetuximab or sunitinib in some protocols.
- **Brachyury-targeted vaccination** — induces anti-tumor immunity against the lineage-defining transcription factor.
- **Immune checkpoint inhibitors** — early-phase data; PD-L1 expression in subset.
- Enrollment in clinical trial is reasonable at diagnosis given the limited established options.

## Prognosis

- **Median overall survival** ~ 6–7 years for skull-base; longer for sacrococcygeal in some series; shorter for dedifferentiated.
- **5-year survival** ~ 60–70 % overall, varying by location, extent of resection, dedifferentiation, and adjuvant therapy.
- **Local recurrence** is the dominant cause of death; metastasis (lung, bone, soft tissue) is late and less common at diagnosis.
- Prognostic factors: extent of resection (the dominant modifiable factor), tumor location (sacrococcygeal > clival > mobile spine in some series), dedifferentiation, age, response to particle therapy.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **En bloc when geometrically possible.** Sacrococcygeal and selected mobile-spine chordomas are the only locations where en bloc resection is achievable; commit to it and accept the functional cost when diagnosis is confirmed.
2. **Maximal intralesional debulking when en bloc is not.** Clival chordomas cannot achieve clear margins; the goal is the smallest possible residual to leave for high-dose particle therapy.
3. **Endoscopic endonasal first for midline clival.** Modern EEA with extended corridors handles most clival chordomas with lower morbidity than transcranial. Transcranial routes are for lateral or trans-tentorial extension.
4. **Reconstruct the skull base.** Vascularized nasoseptal flap for endonasal cases; multilayer closure for transcranial. CSF leak is the principal post-operative pitfall.
5. **Refer for particle therapy.** Photon IMRT is a fallback, not the standard. Network referral for proton or carbon ion is part of modern chordoma care.
6. **Counsel about local recurrence.** Even with maximal resection and particle radiation, local recurrence drives long-term outcome; lifelong surveillance with serial MRI is mandatory.
7. **Confirm with brachyury.** T-brachyury immunohistochemistry distinguishes chordoma from chondrosarcoma — a critical distinction since chondrosarcoma has different prognosis and radiation thresholds.
8. **Multidisciplinary team.** Skull-base neurosurgery, head-and-neck / ENT, radiation oncology with particle therapy, medical oncology, radiology, and increasingly molecular pathology — chordoma is a team sport from diagnosis.
