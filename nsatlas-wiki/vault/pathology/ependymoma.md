# Ependymoma

Ependymoma is a glial neoplasm derived from the ependymal lining of the ventricular system, the central canal of the spinal cord, and the filum terminale. It accounts for ~ 5 % of adult primary CNS tumors and 10 % of pediatric brain tumors, but its clinical importance outstrips its frequency: it is the third most common pediatric posterior fossa tumor, a classic intramedullary spinal cord tumor, and a tumor whose modern management is being rewritten by molecular classification.

## Epidemiology

- ~ 30 % of all primary spinal cord tumors in adults; the most common adult intramedullary tumor.
- 6–12 % of pediatric brain tumors; pediatric peak age 1–5 years, with a smaller adult peak in the fourth and fifth decades.
- Posterior fossa predominance in children (~ 60 %); supratentorial in 30 %; spinal in 10 %.
- In adults the distribution shifts: spinal > supratentorial > posterior fossa.
- NF2 association is strong for spinal ependymoma; consider germline testing in young adults with multiple lesions.

## Anatomic Distribution

### Posterior fossa
- **Fourth-ventricle floor / midline**: the classic pediatric site, often producing obstructive hydrocephalus.
- **Lateral recess** with extension into the [[cerebellopontine-angle]] — the tumor "tongue" wrapping around lower cranial nerves and the brainstem; the principal source of resection morbidity.
- **Foramen of Magendie / Luschka extension** into the upper cervical canal is described.

### Supratentorial
- Often adjacent to or within the lateral or third ventricle, but a substantial subset is purely **intra-parenchymal** without ventricular contact — particularly in younger adults; the supposed ependymal origin is not always anatomically obvious.

### Spinal
- **Intramedullary** in cervical and thoracic cord, often with associated syrinx ("tumor-cyst-tumor" pattern).
- **Filum terminale** as myxopapillary subtype.

## Molecular Classification (WHO 2021)

The 2015 Pajtler classification, codified into WHO 2021, defines nine molecular groups across three anatomic compartments. Five are clinically dominant:

| Subgroup | Compartment | Clinical features | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PFA** | Posterior fossa | Children < 5 y; H3K27me3 loss; chromosome 1q gain confers worse prognosis | Poor; long-term survival 50–60 % |
| **PFB** | Posterior fossa | Adolescents / young adults; chromosomal-instability profile | Good; > 80 % long-term |
| **ST-RELA** (now ZFTA-fusion) | Supratentorial | Children and young adults; ZFTA-RELA fusion | Intermediate to poor |
| **ST-YAP1** | Supratentorial | Young children; YAP1-MAMLD1 fusion | Generally favorable |
| **SP-MPE** | Spine (filum) | Myxopapillary; adults | Generally favorable but recurrence-prone |

NF2-related spinal ependymoma carries its own molecular signature and clinical course.

This classification is now the basis for risk stratification, adjuvant decision-making, and clinical-trial eligibility — pure histologic grading (WHO 2 vs 3) is no longer sufficient.

## Pathology

- Histologic hallmarks: **perivascular pseudorosettes** (radial arrangement of tumor cells around blood vessels with fibrillary processes) and **true ependymal rosettes** (cuboidal cells around a central lumen — pathognomonic when present, but seen in only a minority of cases).
- Grading: WHO 2 (classic ependymoma) vs 3 (anaplastic). The 2021 WHO classification now recognizes that **molecular subgroup outweighs grade** in prognosis for many compartments.
- **Subependymoma** (WHO 1) is a distinct entity, typically incidental, often supratentorial in older adults, with very different (almost benign) biology.
- **Myxopapillary** (WHO 2 in 2021): filum terminale predilection, mucin-rich stroma; slow-growing but local recurrence common.

## Clinical Presentation

### Posterior fossa
- Headache, vomiting, gait ataxia.
- Obstructive [[hydrocephalus]] with papilledema; emergent in neonates and young children.
- Lower-cranial-nerve deficits when tumor extends to the lateral recess — dysphagia, hoarseness, facial weakness.
- "Brainstem signs" — long-tract or autonomic — when tumor adheres to the floor of the fourth ventricle.

### Supratentorial
- Seizures (cortical involvement).
- Focal deficits by location.
- Symptoms of mass effect or hydrocephalus from ventricular extension.

### Spinal intramedullary
- Slowly progressive sensory and motor deficits; gait ataxia.
- Pain, often nocturnal and positional.
- Bowel / bladder dysfunction in advanced disease.
- Long-standing symptom course (years) is common.

### Myxopapillary (filum terminale)
- Lower back pain dominant; sciatica.
- Mimics lumbar disc disease and is a diagnostic trap if MRI is not requested in chronic radicular syndromes.

## Imaging

### MRI (modality of choice)
- **T1**: iso- to hypointense relative to brain.
- **T2**: hyperintense; cysts and necrosis common; calcifications are a hallmark of supratentorial ependymoma (50 %).
- **Post-gadolinium**: heterogeneous, often patchy enhancement.
- **Diffusion**: typically restricted in cellular regions; helps differentiate from low-grade tumors.
- **Posterior fossa**: characteristic "**plastic**" or "toothpaste" extension through the foramina of Luschka and Magendie — emerging from the fourth ventricle into the cisterns and upper cervical canal — distinguishes ependymoma from medulloblastoma (which usually stays within the fourth ventricle and does not pass through foramina).
- **Spinal**: intramedullary T2 hyperintense lesion, often centrally located, with associated rostral/caudal cysts and syrinx; **"cap sign"** — hemosiderin deposit at the tumor poles — is suggestive.

### CT
- Calcifications visible.
- Hydrocephalus quantification.

### Whole-CNS imaging
- Mandatory at diagnosis: gadolinium MRI of brain and entire spine to rule out leptomeningeal dissemination, especially in pediatric posterior-fossa cases. Drop metastases worsen prognosis substantially.

### CSF cytology
- Lumbar puncture for CSF cytology after the obstructive hydrocephalus is treated — looks for leptomeningeal seeding. Avoid pre-operative LP in symptomatic obstruction.

## Differential Diagnosis

### Posterior fossa
- **Medulloblastoma** — typically midline, restricts diffusion centrally, does not extend through foramina.
- **Pilocytic astrocytoma** — cyst-with-mural-nodule, lateral cerebellar hemisphere, younger children.
- **Atypical teratoid / rhabdoid tumor (ATRT)** — infants, very aggressive.
- **Choroid plexus papilloma / carcinoma** — purely intraventricular, intense uniform enhancement.
- **Brainstem glioma** — within brainstem proper.

### Supratentorial
- High-grade glioma, oligodendroglioma, embryonal tumors.

### Spinal
- **Astrocytoma** (eccentric, less well demarcated, no cap sign).
- **[[hemangioblastoma]]** (mural nodule with cyst, intense uniform enhancement, often pial).
- **Cavernous malformation** (popcorn appearance with hemosiderin rim, no enhancement).

## Management

### Surgical resection — the cornerstone

**Maximal safe resection** is the strongest modifiable prognostic factor across virtually every ependymoma subgroup. In modern multivariate analyses, the magnitude of this effect rivals or exceeds molecular subgroup and adjuvant radiation effects.

#### Posterior fossa
- Approach via [[suboccipital-craniotomy]] (midline for fourth-ventricle / midline tumors; lateral / suboccipital with C1 laminectomy for lateral recess extension).
- The **telovelar approach** opens the cerebellomedullary fissure to expose the floor of the fourth ventricle without splitting the inferior vermis — the contemporary preferred corridor for fourth-ventricle tumors.
- Adherence to the floor of the fourth ventricle is the principal limiting factor — leaving a thin tumor remnant on the floor in exchange for cranial-nerve preservation is sometimes the right call (a true second-look or planned-residual strategy is increasingly accepted in modern molecular-tailored care).
- Lateral-recess "tongues" wrapping around lower cranial nerves are dissected with extreme care; intraoperative neuromonitoring (CN VII–XII) is mandatory.
- Pre-operative or peri-operative [[external-ventricular-drain-placement]] for obstructive [[hydrocephalus]] is common; permanent shunting is required in 20–30 % post-resection.

#### Supratentorial
- Standard tumor-resection principles by location; intra-operative MRI and awake mapping where eloquent.

#### Spinal intramedullary
- Approach via posterior midline myelotomy at the level of greatest tumor diameter, with intraoperative neuromonitoring (SSEPs, MEPs, D-wave). The **D-wave** is the key prognostic signal: a > 50 % drop in D-wave amplitude predicts permanent motor deficit and may be the moment to halt resection.
- Most ependymomas have a discrete plane between tumor and cord; gross-total resection is feasible in 60–80 % of cases. See [[intramedullary-spinal-cord-tumor-resection]] for the technique synthesis.
- Myxopapillary at the filum terminale: en-bloc resection with the filum is ideal but capsule violation can disseminate the tumor — careful microsurgical technique is more demanding than the indolent biology would suggest.

### Adjuvant therapy

#### Radiation
- **Focal conformal radiotherapy** (54–59.4 Gy in standard fractionation) is the mainstay after subtotal resection and after gross-total resection in PFA, RELA-fusion, and anaplastic disease.
- **Proton therapy** is increasingly used in pediatric patients to spare brainstem and cochlear structures.
- **Craniospinal irradiation** is reserved for documented leptomeningeal dissemination — not routine.
- **Radiation deferral** in infants under 18–36 months (depending on protocol) is the standard, given long-term neurocognitive cost.

#### Chemotherapy
- Adult ependymoma has a limited proven role for chemotherapy.
- In infants and young children where radiation is deferred, multi-agent chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide, vincristine, etoposide, cisplatin, methotrexate) is used as a bridge to delayed radiation or as primary post-operative therapy.
- Targeted therapy informed by molecular subgrouping is in clinical trial — RELA-fusion-directed and EZH2-inhibition strategies are early-phase.

### Surveillance

Lifelong surveillance is the rule:
- Brain (and spine if myxopapillary or seeded) MRI every 3–6 months for the first 2 years, then less frequently.
- Recurrence is common and locoregional; salvage surgery and re-irradiation strategies are pursued aggressively in young patients.
- Late distant recurrences and second malignancies after radiation are recognized risks.

## Prognosis

| Subgroup | 5-year overall survival |
|---|---|
| **PFA** | 50–60 % (with 1q gain: < 40 %) |
| **PFB** | 80–90 % |
| **ST-RELA / ZFTA** | 50–70 % |
| **ST-YAP1** | > 90 % |
| **Spinal** | 80–90 % (myxopapillary local recurrence common but rarely fatal) |

The classic prognostic predictors — extent of resection, age (worse in infants), grade — remain operative within each molecular subgroup but are subordinated to the molecular signature when both are known.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Do not chase tumor through the floor of the fourth ventricle.** Trade an unresectable thin remnant for cranial-nerve and brainstem preservation; modern adjuvant therapy and molecular-tailored care make planned residuals acceptable in selected cases.
2. **Send molecular tissue early.** PFA vs PFB, RELA-fusion vs YAP1 — the molecular result changes adjuvant decisions and should guide post-operative discussion with neuro-oncology.
3. **Beware the lateral recess.** A tumor extending into the [[cerebellopontine-angle]] is more dangerous than its supratentorial bulk suggests; lower-cranial-nerve injury is the dominant morbidity.
4. **Plan for hydrocephalus.** EVD pre-operatively for symptomatic obstruction; expect 20–30 % to require permanent shunting after resection.
5. **In spinal ependymoma, trust the D-wave.** A > 50 % drop is the call to stop dissection; chasing the last bit of tumor against this signal converts a controlled resection into a permanent paraplegia.
6. **Image the entire neuraxis at diagnosis.** Drop metastases change everything and are often clinically silent at presentation.
7. **Counsel honestly about recurrence.** Even in favorable subgroups, late recurrence demands long-term follow-up; early discharge from surveillance is not appropriate.
