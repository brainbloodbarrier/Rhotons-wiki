# Chiari Malformation

Named for Hans Chiari's 1891 autopsy series, the Chiari malformations are a heterogeneous set of **posterior-fossa / craniovertebral-junction** anomalies united by caudal displacement of hindbrain structures. Chiari I is the dominant clinical entity in adult neurosurgery; the others are pediatric or post-mortem curiosities.

## Classification

| Type | Anatomy | Associations |
|---|---|---|
| **Chiari I** | Cerebellar tonsils ≥5 mm below foramen magnum | Syringomyelia (~50%), small posterior fossa, basilar invagination, retroflexed odontoid, Ehlers-Danlos |
| **Chiari 1.5** | Chiari I + brainstem / 4th-ventricle descent | Bridging phenotype |
| **Chiari II (Arnold-Chiari)** | Tonsils + vermis + medulla + 4th ventricle herniate; medullary kink | Myelomeningocele, hydrocephalus, tectal beaking, callosal dysgenesis |
| **Chiari III** | Encephalocele (high-cervical / occipital) containing cerebellum | Rare, severe |
| **Chiari IV** | Cerebellar hypoplasia; no herniation (probably a misnomer) | Often incompatible with life |

## Chiari I — pathophysiology

The small, crowded posterior fossa (often with a shallow clivus, short supraocciput, and flat basiocciput) forces the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum. The resulting obstruction of CSF egress:

1. Produces a **pressure differential** between intracranial and spinal subarachnoid spaces.
2. Pistons the tonsils with cardiac cycle — reduced CSF flow on phase-contrast MRI.
3. Transmits pulsatile pressure down the **central canal**, producing a **syrinx** in ~50% of symptomatic patients (Oldfield hypothesis).

## Presentation

- **Occipital / suboccipital headache** worsened by Valsalva, cough, exercise — the pathognomonic complaint.
- **Lower cranial nerve signs** — dysphonia, dysphagia, hoarseness, sleep apnea.
- **Cerebellar** — truncal ataxia, nystagmus (classically downbeat).
- **Syrinx-related** — cape-distribution dissociated sensory loss, hand atrophy, scoliosis in children, painless burns.
- **Upper motor neuron signs** from brainstem / upper cervical cord compression.

## Imaging

- **Sagittal T1/T2 MRI** — tonsillar position relative to McRae line (basion–opisthion); measure descent, pointedness, crowding.
- **Phase-contrast cine MRI** — quantifies CSF flow at the foramen magnum; absent or bidirectional flow supports treatment.
- **Whole-spine MRI** — screens for syringomyelia.
- **Dynamic flexion / extension** — assess craniocervical instability (pannus, basilar invagination).

## Surgical indications

Symptomatic Chiari I with imaging evidence of tonsillar ectopia and/or syrinx. Asymptomatic adults with incidental tonsillar descent are generally observed. Pediatric scoliosis or sleep apnea from Chiari I may prompt earlier intervention.

## Operative strategy — posterior fossa decompression

Tailored to CSF dynamics and anatomy. The principal variants:

- **Bony-only decompression** — suboccipital craniectomy + C1 laminectomy without opening dura. Lower morbidity, acceptable in pediatric patients without syrinx.
- **Decompression with duraplasty** — adds durotomy and expansile patch (pericranium, fascia lata, synthetic). Greater CSF-flow improvement.
- **Decompression + tonsillar reduction / shrinkage** — for deeply impacted tonsils or persistent foramen of Magendie obstruction.
- **Extradural dural-band / scoring technique** — popularized alternative to reduce pseudomeningocele risk.

Operative conduct:

- Positioning: prone (horseshoe or Mayfield), neck neutral or slightly flexed; avoid excess flexion (myelopathic risk). See [[patient-positioning]] and [[skull-clamp-placement]].
- [[Suboccipital-craniotomy]] or small craniectomy (3×3 cm typical); C1 laminectomy if tonsils extend below C1 ring.
- Y-shaped durotomy, arachnoid preservation when possible, expansile duraplasty.
- Watertight closure critical — CSF leak is the dominant complication.
- See [[dural-opening-and-closure]] and [[suturing-and-closure]] for closure principles; [[intraoperative-hemostasis]] for occipital sinus management.

## Syringomyelia management

Most syringes stabilize or shrink after adequate foramen magnum decompression. Persistent or progressive syrinx despite decompression may warrant syringo-subarachnoid shunting or ventricular diversion (see [[ventriculo-peritoneal-shunt]]).

## Complications and outcomes

- **CSF leak / pseudomeningocele** (5–15%) — reduced by meticulous duraplasty.
- **Aseptic meningitis** from blood products or suture material.
- **Cerebellar slump** after overly aggressive bony decompression.
- **Symptomatic relief**: headache 75–90%, syrinx regression 70–80%, cranial neuropathy variable.

## Chiari II special considerations

In myelomeningocele patients, symptomatic brainstem dysfunction in infancy is a neurosurgical emergency. Hydrocephalus management (often with [[endoscopic-third-ventriculostomy]] or ventricular shunt) frequently precedes or replaces direct decompression. Prenatal MMC closure reduces the rate of shunt dependence and hindbrain herniation.
