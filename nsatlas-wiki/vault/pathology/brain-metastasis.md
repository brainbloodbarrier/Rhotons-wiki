# Brain Metastasis

Intracranial metastases are the most frequent adult brain tumor. They exceed primary CNS tumors by an order of magnitude and are increasing in incidence as systemic cancer survival improves. Within neurosurgical practice, the central question is not *whether* to treat (nearly always yes) but *which* of three modalities — **microsurgical resection**, **stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)**, and **whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT)** — best serves a specific patient's burden of disease and projected survival.

## Epidemiology and biology

- ~200 000 newly diagnosed annually in the US; true incidence underestimated by autopsy data.
- Primary source hierarchy (adult): **lung (NSCLC > SCLC) ~40–50%**, **breast ~15–25%**, **melanoma ~5–15%**, **renal cell ~5–10%**, **colorectal ~5%**, others.
- Hematogenous spread predominates → lodgement at the **gray-white junction** where vessel caliber narrows abruptly.
- Distribution: 80% supratentorial (proportional to cerebral mass), 15% cerebellum, 5% brainstem — but melanoma, GI, and pelvic primaries over-represent the posterior fossa.

## Clinical presentation

- Focal neurologic deficit (location-dependent).
- Headache (50%) — often morning, worsening over weeks.
- Seizure (25%) — classic presentation for a cortical metastasis.
- Cognitive / personality change with frontal or multifocal disease.
- Acute presentation with hemorrhage into a metastasis — classically **melanoma, renal cell, choriocarcinoma, thyroid**, occasionally lung.

## Diagnosis

- **MRI with contrast** — standard; ring-enhancing or solidly-enhancing lesions at gray-white junction with disproportionate T2/FLAIR vasogenic edema.
- **Number, size, and location** drive treatment more than any single imaging feature.
- **Advanced MRI** (perfusion, spectroscopy) helps distinguish metastasis from primary brain tumor (see [[brain-tumors-general-principles]]) in atypical presentations.
- **Stereotactic biopsy** ([[stereotactic-biopsy]]) is indicated when primary is unknown and systemic workup is non-diagnostic, or when the differential includes lymphoma, infection, or [[radiation-necrosis|radiation necrosis]] in a prior irradiated field.

## Graded Prognostic Assessment (GPA)

The modern tool for modality selection. Stratifies patients by:

1. Age
2. Karnofsky performance status
3. Number of metastases (or cumulative volume)
4. Extracranial disease status
5. Disease-specific molecular markers (EGFR/ALK in NSCLC; ER/PR/HER2 in breast; BRAF in melanoma)

Survival ranges from <4 months (worst quartile) to >2 years (best quartile) — critical for treatment-intensity calibration.

## Treatment modalities

### Microsurgical resection
**Patchell 1990** established resection + WBRT as superior to WBRT alone for single metastasis with controlled systemic disease — the foundational RCT for modern metastasis management. Indications:

- Single (or single symptomatic) lesion with mass effect, edema, or neurologic deficit.
- Need for tissue diagnosis (primary unknown).
- Lesion >3 cm (poor SRS candidate).
- Posterior fossa lesion with impending obstructive [[hydrocephalus]].
- Hemorrhagic lesion with clot evacuation indication.

Approach planning follows standard [[cranial-approaches-general-principles]]; the craniotomy is tailored to lesion location (pterional, parasagittal, occipital, retromastoid — see [[pterional-craniotomy]], [[retromastoid-craniotomy]]). En-bloc resection when feasible reduces local recurrence vs piecemeal. Post-operative **cavity SRS** (rather than WBRT) has become standard in modern oligo-metastatic protocols.

### Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)
- Lesion ≤3 cm (ideal ≤2 cm for maximum dose tolerance).
- ≤10 cumulative lesions in current practice (previous cutoff was 4; extended with modern evidence).
- Residual or recurrent lesions after prior resection or WBRT.
- Dose: inverse to volume; 24 Gy for <2 cm, 18 Gy for 2–3 cm, 15 Gy for 3–4 cm (single fraction), or hypofractionated (3 × 9 Gy).
- Late complication: [[radiation-necrosis]] in ~10% at 1 year, escalating with volume and re-irradiation.

### Whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT)
- Historical default; now reserved for miliary disease (>10–15 lesions), leptomeningeal involvement, or palliative symptom control.
- Neurocognitive toxicity is the limiting factor; hippocampal-avoidance WBRT and memantine reduce but do not eliminate this.

### Systemic therapy
Modern CNS-penetrant targeted therapies (osimertinib for EGFR NSCLC, lapatinib/tucatinib for HER2 breast, BRAF/MEK inhibitors for melanoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors) achieve CNS response rates of 40–80% and increasingly substitute for upfront SRS in selected molecular subgroups.

## Operative considerations

- **Pre-operative steroids** (dexamethasone 10 mg load, then 4 mg q6h) for symptomatic vasogenic edema — do not delay for steroids if impending herniation.
- **Anticonvulsants** for cortical lesions or pre-existing seizures; prophylaxis is optional for non-seizing patients.
- **Venous thromboembolism** prophylaxis is critical — metastatic cancer is a procoagulant state.
- Intraoperative principles follow [[brain-tumors-general-principles]], with emphasis on meticulous [[intraoperative-hemostasis]] (hemorrhagic metastases), careful [[brain-mobilization-and-protection]], and preserved [[cranial-base-preoperative-considerations|cranial-base planning]] when the tumor abuts the skull base.
- Cavity biopsy / re-resection of recurrent lesions common given long survival in oligo-metastatic disease.

## Prognosis

- Median survival highly dependent on primary histology and GPA: 4 weeks (worst) to 3+ years (best).
- Local control after resection + cavity SRS: 80–90% at 1 year.
- WBRT-only historical median: 4–6 months — a figure that has essentially doubled with modern combined-modality approaches and systemic therapy.
