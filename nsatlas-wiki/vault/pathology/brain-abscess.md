# Brain Abscess

A brain abscess is a focal collection of pus within the brain parenchyma surrounded by a vascularized capsule of granulation tissue. Despite advances in antibiotics, imaging, and stereotactic aspiration, it remains a true neurosurgical emergency: outcome is dominated by the speed of source control, the appropriateness of empiric antibiotics, and the patient's neurologic status at the moment of diagnosis.

## Epidemiology

- Incidence ≈ 0.4–0.9 per 100 000 person-years; higher in low- and middle-income countries where chronic ear and sinus disease remain common.
- Bimodal distribution: children and adults > 40 years.
- Male predominance (2–3 : 1).
- Predisposing populations: cyanotic congenital heart disease (right-to-left shunt bypasses pulmonary capillary filter), chronic otomastoiditis, untreated sinusitis, dental sepsis, IV drug use, immune compromise (HIV, transplant, hematologic malignancy), penetrating cranial injury, and prior craniotomy.

## Pathogenesis and Stages

Brain parenchyma is normally protected by the blood-brain barrier and lacks lymphatics. Once organisms breach these defenses, the lesion evolves through four classical stages described by Britt and Enzmann (1981):

1. **Early cerebritis** (days 1–3): unencapsulated focal inflammation, edema, neutrophil infiltration; ill-defined hypodense lesion on imaging.
2. **Late cerebritis** (days 4–9): central necrosis appears, with macrophages and reticulin networks beginning capsule formation; ring enhancement starts to develop on imaging.
3. **Early capsule** (days 10–13): collagen capsule forms; ring enhancement becomes well defined; the cavity is now a "young abscess."
4. **Late capsule** (day 14+): mature, thick collagen capsule (thinner on the ventricular side), pus cavity, surrounding edema, and gliotic rim.

The thinner medial (ventricular-facing) capsule explains the propensity for **intraventricular rupture**, a catastrophic complication associated with mortality > 80 %.

## Routes of Infection

### Contiguous spread (~ 25–50 %)
- **Otomastoiditis / chronic otitis media** → temporal lobe and cerebellar abscesses (Citelli's pathway).
- **Sinusitis** (especially frontal and ethmoid) → frontal lobe abscesses; sphenoid sinus → temporal lobe.
- **Dental infection** → frontal lobe via emissary veins.

### Hematogenous (~ 25 %)
- Multiple, often at the **gray-white junction** in the middle cerebral artery distribution.
- Sources: endocarditis, lung abscess / bronchiectasis, pulmonary AVM (especially HHT), pelvic and abdominal sepsis, IV drug use.
- Cyanotic congenital heart disease shunts the bacterial load past the pulmonary capillary filter — a unique pediatric risk factor.

### Direct inoculation (~ 10 %)
- Penetrating cranial trauma, retained foreign bodies, or post-craniotomy infection. Often polymicrobial and including skin flora.

### Cryptogenic (15–30 %)
- No source identified despite full workup.

## Microbiology

| Host context | Likely organisms |
|---|---|
| Immunocompetent, contiguous source | *Streptococcus* (esp. *S. anginosus* / "milleri" group), oral anaerobes (*Peptostreptococcus*, *Fusobacterium*, *Bacteroides*), *S. aureus* |
| Post-traumatic / post-surgical | *S. aureus* (incl. MRSA), *S. epidermidis*, gram-negatives (*Pseudomonas*, *Klebsiella*) |
| Endocarditis | *Streptococcus*, *S. aureus*, *Enterococcus* |
| Immunocompromised (HIV, transplant) | *Toxoplasma gondii*, *Nocardia*, *Aspergillus*, *Cryptococcus*, *Listeria*, mycobacteria, JC virus / PML in differential |
| Neonate | Gram-negatives (*Citrobacter*, *Cronobacter*), group B strep |

Polymicrobial infection is common (≥ 30 %), particularly in contiguous and post-surgical cases.

## Clinical Presentation

The classic triad of **fever, headache, and focal deficit** appears in fewer than half of patients. Pure presentations are common and misleading:

- **Headache** (most consistent symptom, ~ 70 %) — often progressive, worse on lying flat, may be remarkably bland.
- **Fever** — present in only 50 %, especially in well-encapsulated lesions where systemic markers normalize.
- **Focal neurologic deficit** (~ 50 %) reflects abscess location: hemiparesis, aphasia, visual field cuts, ataxia.
- **Altered mental status** — ranges from mild lethargy to coma; a strong predictor of poor outcome.
- **Seizures** (25–30 %) — focal or generalized; cortical and frontal lesions are most epileptogenic.
- **Signs of raised intracranial pressure** — papilledema, vomiting, bradycardia; impending herniation in advanced cases.
- **Meningismus** — particularly with intraventricular rupture; otherwise often absent.

A high index of suspicion is essential in any patient with a known predisposition (cyanotic heart disease, recent dental work, chronic ear infection, immunocompromise) and new neurologic symptoms — even subtle ones.

## Imaging

### MRI (modality of choice)
- **T1**: hypointense center, hyperintense rim post-gadolinium (smooth, thin, slightly thicker on the cortical side).
- **T2 / FLAIR**: hyperintense center surrounded by hypointense capsule and broad vasogenic edema.
- **DWI**: **central restricted diffusion** (high DWI / low ADC) is the cardinal MRI sign — pus is highly cellular and viscous. This is the single most useful feature distinguishing an abscess from a **cystic / necrotic tumor**, which typically shows facilitated diffusion.
- **MR spectroscopy** can show amino acid peaks (0.9 ppm), acetate, and succinate from bacterial metabolism — supportive when imaging is ambiguous.
- **SWI** may demonstrate a peripheral hypointense rim from hemosiderin / paramagnetic free radicals.

### CT
- Faster and more available; appropriate for the unstable patient and initial triage.
- Hypodense center, smooth ring enhancement, surrounding edema.
- Lower sensitivity in early cerebritis and in deep / posterior fossa locations.

### Differential on imaging
- Glioblastoma — irregular, thicker, nodular ring; facilitated diffusion in the necrotic core.
- Brain metastasis — often multiple, gray-white junction; facilitated diffusion centrally.
- Demyelinating lesion (tumefactive MS) — incomplete "open" ring toward the cortex.
- Resolving hematoma — characteristic blood products on susceptibility-weighted imaging.
- Radiation necrosis in a previously treated field.
- Cysticercosis, tuberculoma, and other granulomatous lesions in endemic settings.

## Diagnostic Workup

- **Blood cultures** (× 2 sets) before antibiotics — positive in 10–30 %, more in hematogenous cases.
- **Inflammatory markers** (CRP, ESR, procalcitonin) — sensitive but not specific; usually elevated.
- **Source workup**: dedicated CT of paranasal sinuses, mastoids, and middle ear; transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography for endocarditis; chest imaging for pulmonary source; dental exam.
- **HIV testing** in any newly diagnosed brain abscess where immune status is unknown.
- **Lumbar puncture is generally contraindicated** in mass lesions — risk of herniation outweighs the diagnostic yield (which is low for abscess).
- **Definitive microbiologic diagnosis comes from the abscess cavity itself**: gram stain, aerobic / anaerobic / fungal / mycobacterial cultures, 16S rRNA PCR. Aspiration is therefore both diagnostic and therapeutic.

## Management

### Initial stabilization
- ICU monitoring for any patient with deficits, depressed consciousness, or signs of raised ICP.
- Airway protection in coma; head elevation; treat seizures and prophylax in high-risk locations.
- Anticonvulsants for cortical lesions, frontal abscesses, or those with seizures; continue 1–2 years post-treatment in those who seized.
- **Corticosteroids** are NOT routine — they may reduce edema briefly but impair antibiotic penetration and capsule formation. Reserve for impending herniation or significant mass effect with a planned surgical decompression to follow.

### Empiric antibiotic therapy
Started immediately after blood cultures and aspiration when feasible. A common immunocompetent regimen:

- **Ceftriaxone 2 g IV q12h** (or cefotaxime) — gram-negative and streptococcal coverage.
- **+ Metronidazole 500 mg IV q6–8h** — anaerobic coverage.
- **+ Vancomycin** — empiric staphylococcal coverage; mandatory after trauma, surgery, or in MRSA-endemic settings.

Adjust based on context:
- Penetrating trauma / post-surgical → vancomycin + cefepime or meropenem.
- Endocarditis → vancomycin + ceftriaxone or per blood culture.
- Immunocompromised → add coverage for *Nocardia* (TMP-SMX), fungi (voriconazole or amphotericin), and *Toxoplasma* if neuroimaging compatible (sulfadiazine + pyrimethamine).
- Tailor narrowly once cultures and sensitivities return.

Duration: **6–8 weeks of intravenous therapy is the modern minimum**, sometimes followed by oral step-down for 2–3 months in well-responding cases. Serial MRI guides duration.

### Surgical drainage

Indicated for nearly all abscesses ≥ 2.5 cm, lesions causing significant mass effect, lesions with diagnostic uncertainty, and any lesion failing empiric therapy.

#### Image-guided stereotactic aspiration (preferred default)
- Single burr hole, frame-based or frameless image-guidance, soft cannula or needle into the abscess center.
- Aspirate to dryness; send pus for full microbiology panel including 16S rRNA PCR.
- Repeat aspiration in 24–72 h if reaccumulation on imaging or persistent mass effect.
- Workflow overlap with the [[stereotactic-biopsy]] technique used for lesions of unclear diagnosis.

#### Open craniotomy with excision
Reserved for:
- Traumatic abscesses with foreign bodies / bone fragments / contaminated dura.
- Multiloculated or thick-walled lesions that fail percutaneous drainage.
- Posterior fossa abscesses with critical mass effect.
- Fungal abscesses where the capsule itself harbors organisms.
- Post-surgical abscesses requiring debridement of infected hardware.

Excision affords source control but carries higher morbidity and is rarely first line in the modern era.

#### CSF diversion
- An [[external-ventricular-drain-placement]] is appropriate for [[hydrocephalus]] from posterior fossa abscess or from intraventricular rupture.
- For deep lesions abutting the ventricular system, the EVD also allows intraventricular antibiotic therapy if rupture has occurred.
- Long-term shunting may be required after resolution if ependymitis / arachnoiditis impairs CSF dynamics.

### Adjunctive measures
- Source control: ENT consultation for sinus or mastoid disease; dental extraction; cardiac valve repair; pulmonary AVM embolization.
- Repeat imaging at 1–2 weeks, then every 2 weeks during therapy; failure of expected radiographic regression should prompt reassessment.
- Failure of medical therapy or rapid neurologic deterioration may warrant emergent [[decompressive-craniectomy]] in severe edema or impending herniation.

## Complications and Pitfalls

- **Intraventricular rupture** — sudden meningismus, hyperthermia, depressed consciousness; mortality > 80 %; treated with EVD, intraventricular antibiotics, and aggressive systemic therapy.
- **Herniation** from progressive mass effect.
- **Persistent or recurrent abscess** — antibiotic resistance, inadequate source control, retained foreign body, immune compromise.
- **Symptomatic epilepsy** in 30–50 % long-term — anticonvulsant duration informed by lesion location and seizure history.
- **Focal neurologic deficits** — partially reversible with timely treatment but commonly persist when present at diagnosis.
- **Hydrocephalus** — communicating or obstructive; may require permanent shunting.
- **Diagnostic delay or misdiagnosis** as tumor is the most common preventable contributor to poor outcome.

## Prognosis

- Modern mortality 5–15 %; pre-CT era was > 40 %.
- Coma at presentation, intraventricular rupture, immunocompromise, posterior fossa location, fungal etiology, and delayed diagnosis are the strongest predictors of poor outcome.
- Functional recovery in survivors correlates inversely with pre-treatment GCS and the presence of focal deficit at diagnosis.
- Neuropsychologic sequelae and epilepsy are common and warrant long-term follow-up regardless of apparent radiographic cure.

## Key Surgical Decision Points

1. **Aspirate, don't excise, by default.** Stereotactic aspiration combined with prolonged IV antibiotics is curative in the majority of pyogenic abscesses and dramatically less morbid than open excision.
2. **Get pus before antibiotics whenever the patient is stable enough to allow it.** Unguided empiric therapy without microbiologic confirmation is the leading cause of treatment failure.
3. **Treat the source.** Antibiotic cure without addressing chronic otitis, sinus disease, dental infection, or right-to-left shunt invites recurrence.
4. **Anticipate intraventricular rupture in deep periventricular abscesses.** Plan for an EVD pre-emptively if the medial capsule is thin and the lesion is large.
5. **Six to eight weeks of IV antibiotics is the minimum.** Premature step-down is the single most common cause of relapse.
6. **Watch for hydrocephalus and herniation, especially in the posterior fossa.** Have a low threshold to convert from medical to surgical management when neurologic status worsens.
