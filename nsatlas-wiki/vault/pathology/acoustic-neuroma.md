# Acoustic Neuroma (Vestibular Schwannoma)

The vestibular schwannoma — historically and clinically called the "acoustic neuroma" — is a benign Schwann-cell tumor of the vestibular division of CN VIII. It is the most common tumor of the **cerebellopontine angle (CPA)** in adults and accounts for ~8% of all intracranial tumors. Modern management rests on a triad of **microsurgical resection**, **stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)**, and **serial observation** — choice driven by tumor size, hearing status, age, and patient preference.

## Epidemiology

- Annual incidence ~1 per 100,000
- Peak age 40–60; female-to-male ratio ~1.5:1
- Sporadic (~95%) or NF2-associated (~5%) — bilateral tumors pathognomonic of NF2
- Rising detected incidence with widespread MRI use; many are now small or intracanalicular at diagnosis

## Origin and Location

- Arises from Schwann cells at the **Obersteiner-Redlich zone** (glial–Schwann transition) — typically at or just outside the porus acusticus
- Grows medially into the CPA cistern and laterally into the internal auditory canal (IAC)
- **Most** arise from the superior or inferior vestibular nerve; minority from cochlear nerve (poorer hearing preservation)

## Clinical Presentation

| Symptom | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| Asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss (**~95%**) | Direct compression or vascular compromise of the cochlear nerve |
| Tinnitus (ipsilateral) | Cochlear nerve involvement |
| Vestibular imbalance / vertigo | Central compensation for vestibular deafferentation |
| Facial numbness | CN V compression with larger tumors (usually >2.5 cm) |
| Facial weakness (rare pre-op) | CN VII compression — suggests large tumor or alternative diagnosis |
| Hydrocephalus / headache | 4th-ventricle compression from large tumors |
| Trigeminal neuralgia | Rare; CN V compression |

## Classification

### Koos (size-based)

| Grade | Size | Description |
|-------|------|-------------|
| I | Intracanalicular only | Within IAC |
| II | <20 mm | Extension into CPA, no brainstem contact |
| III | 21–30 mm | Brainstem contact, no displacement |
| IV | >30 mm | Brainstem displacement and 4th-ventricle effacement |

### House-Brackmann (facial nerve function grading)

Used for pre- and postoperative facial nerve assessment — I (normal) to VI (complete paralysis).

## Imaging

- **MRI with contrast and thin-cut IAC sequences** — T1 iso, T2 hypo/iso, avid enhancement; "ice-cream cone" with intracanalicular and cisternal components
- **High-resolution T2 (CISS / FIESTA)** — for CN VII/VIII identification and cochlear-nerve visualization
- **CT** — canal enlargement, useful for temporal-bone surgical planning
- **MRA / MRV** — AICA loop localization; posterior petrosal vein anatomy

## Management Options

### Observation

- Small, minimally symptomatic tumors (especially Koos I–II in elderly patients)
- ~50% show no growth over 3–5 years; 30–40% grow ≤2 mm/yr
- Serial MRI at 6, 12, 24 months, then annually

### Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS)

- Ideal for Koos I–III, small-to-moderate size (<2.5–3 cm)
- Marginal dose 12–13 Gy
- Tumor control ~95% at 5–10 years
- **Hearing preservation** ~50–60% at 5 years (cochlear dose–dependent)
- Facial nerve preservation >95% (House-Brackmann I–II)

### Microsurgical Resection

Indications: large tumors (Koos IV), cystic tumors, brainstem compression, failed SRS, younger patients preferring definitive therapy.

## Surgical Approaches

### Retrosigmoid (retromastoid) — see [[retromastoid-craniotomy]]

- **Most versatile** approach
- Tumors of any size; hearing-preservation attempt possible for small/medium tumors
- Posterior fossa corridor; IAC can be drilled for lateral extension
- Operative position: lateral (park-bench) or semi-sitting

### Translabyrinthine

- Sacrifices hearing — used when pre-operative audiogram shows non-serviceable hearing
- Shortest working distance; best CN VII anatomic exposure at the fundus of the IAC
- Performed with neuro-otology team

### Middle Fossa

- Intracanalicular and small extracanalicular tumors in patients with serviceable hearing
- CN VII runs superior in the IAC — at risk during dissection
- Temporal lobe retraction; suitable in younger patients

## Operative Principles (Retromastoid)

1. Lateral position with operative side up; mastoid-centered incision
2. Small retromastoid craniotomy exposing transverse-sigmoid junction
3. Dural opening; cisterna magna drainage for cerebellar relaxation
4. Cerebellum retracted medially; petrosal vein preserved when possible
5. CN VII/VIII identified medially at the brainstem; **facial nerve** stimulator used throughout
6. IAC posterior wall drilled; tumor capsule opened and debulked
7. Internal debulking then capsule dissection off cochlear and facial nerves
8. Dural and bone-defect closure with careful mastoid air-cell sealing (CSF leak prevention)

## Complications

| Complication | Rate | Comment |
|--------------|------|---------|
| Hearing loss | 30–70% | Depends on size, approach, preop hearing |
| Facial nerve deficit | 5–15% HB III–VI | Size-dependent; small tumors <5% |
| CSF leak / pseudomeningocele | 5–10% | Mastoid air cells |
| Headache (chronic) | 10–20% | Retrosigmoid > translab |
| Cerebellar injury | <2% | Retraction or venous sacrifice |
| Meningitis | 1–3% | CSF leak sequela |
| Death | <1% | Brainstem injury, hemorrhage |
