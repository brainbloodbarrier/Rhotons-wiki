# Principles of Intraventricular Surgery

*Source: NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol | DOI via nsatlas.v4*

Ventricular tumors are <1% of intracranial lesions and ~10% of CNS neoplasms — of which only 10% are strictly intraventricular. They are technically punishing: deep, often highly vascular, surrounded by eloquent diencephalic and brainstem structures, and usually large by the time they are found because slow growth tolerates silent expansion until hydrocephalus or compression declares itself.

## Clinical Presentation

Two mechanisms drive the clinical picture:

1. **Obstructive hydrocephalus** — acute (headache, nausea, vomiting, rarely sudden death with colloid cysts), chronic, or intermittent (posturally induced symptoms, classic for colloid cysts at the foramen of Monro).
2. **Compression of deep structures** — midbrain compression with motor/sensory deficits or **Parinaud's syndrome** (vertical gaze palsy, upbeat/downbeat nystagmus, impaired convergence) from pineal/tectal masses compressing the rostral interstitial nucleus of the MLF; cerebellar tract signs (ataxia) from fourth-ventricular tumors.

Common nonspecific findings: headache, cognitive dysfunction, personality change, motor weakness, visual disturbance, seizures. **Preoperative neuropsychological testing** is mandatory — patients often underestimate their own memory decline, and postoperative forniceal injury must be benchmarked.

## Evaluation

**MRI is the gold standard.** It defines tumor size, vascularity, invasion, peritumoral edema, and ventricular anatomy. Reconstructed axial, sagittal, and coronal planes are required for entry-point selection. A hallmark finding: most intraventricular lesions have a **CSF "cap"** around them.

Key imaging priorities:

- **Long axis of the mass** — the operative corridor must approach along it. As depth increases, **working angles dominate over breadth of exposure**.
- **Ventricular veins** — thalamostriate, septal, internal cerebral veins. Preoperative angiography or MRV is valuable in hypervascular tumors.
- **Overlying cortex function** — determines transcortical versus transcallosal routing.

### Tumor-specific imaging clues (selected)

- **Colloid cyst** — T1 hyperintense, T2 hypointense with hyperintense rim; foramen of Monro; rare calcification.
- **Craniopharyngioma** — heterogeneous cystic/solid; T1 hyperintense cystic components; calcification.
- **Low-grade astrocytoma** — T1 hypo / T2 hyper, minimal enhancement, ~15% calcified.
- **Ependymoma** — heterogeneous on T1/T2, cystic, ~50% calcified.
- **Central neurocytoma** — heterogeneous with cysts, calcifications, moderate enhancement.
- **Subependymoma** — lobulated, minimal enhancement.
- **Choroid plexus papilloma** — isointense T1/T2, homogeneous enhancement; MRI cannot reliably distinguish papilloma from carcinoma.

## Indications for Surgery

Most ventricular tumors are asymptomatic and discovered incidentally. Benign/slow-growing lesions (e.g., subependymoma) may be observed with serial imaging. Symptomatic tumors warrant **complete surgical resection** for diagnosis, mass-effect relief, and growth control — **except pineal region masses**, where CSF analysis and stereotactic/endoscopic biopsy precede resection to exclude non-operative germ cell tumors.

Emergent indications: **acute intratumoral hemorrhage** (immediate resection), **acute obstructive hydrocephalus** (ventriculostomy).

## Corridor Selection (in order of priority)

1. **Minimal transgression and retraction of normal/functional brain**
2. **Expanded working angles** for effective gross total resection
3. **Early exposure of vital structures and tumor blood supply**
4. **Technical difficulty of the route** — least important

**Technical complexity is the lowest priority.** Deep lesions invite innovation — [[contralateral-interhemispheric-transfalcine-transprecuneus-approach|contralateral interhemispheric approaches]] yield flexible angles to off-midline lesions under less ipsilateral retraction.

### Lateral ventricle — location-dependent approaches

| Location | Preferred approaches |
|---|---|
| Frontal horn | Anterior interhemispheric transcallosal; transfrontal transcortical (middle frontal gyrus) |
| Body | Anterior/posterior transcallosal; transfrontal or transparietal transcortical |
| Atrium / trigone | [[contralateral-interhemispheric-transfalcine-transprecuneus-approach]]; ipsilateral transcingulate/transprecuneus; transparietal transcortical/transsulcal |
| Temporal horn | Anterior temporal resection; transtemporal transcortical/transsulcal; transsylvian |
| Occipital horn | Posterior interhemispheric transcortical; occipital neocortical resection |

**Transcortical vs transcallosal** is controversial. Transcortical avoids parasagittal vein dissection and interhemispheric arachnoid work but disrupts frontal projection fibers and limits working angles. Reports of increased postoperative seizures after transcortical approaches have not been consistently confirmed. Cohen-Gadol favors transcallosal for flexibility when lesion geometry permits.

### Third ventricle and fourth ventricle

- Third-ventricular involvement mandates preoperative **endocrinologic** and **neuro-ophthalmologic** assessment.
- Caudal fourth-ventricular lesions demand **otolaryngologic evaluation of lower cranial nerve function**.
- The [[telovelar-approach]] provides a midline route to the fourth ventricle without splitting vermis.

## Intraoperative Conduct

### Positioning and exposure

- Supine or lateral with **sagittal suture parallel to the floor** so the falx holds the contralateral hemisphere and **gravity retraction** widens the interhemispheric corridor.
- Long cases — pad all pressure points generously.
- For midline lateral ventricular lesions, the **superior sagittal sinus is often unroofed**; combine with [[burr-holes-and-bone-flaps|burr holes straddling the sinus]] and save the near-sinus cut for last.
- **CSF drainage** — [[lumbar-drain]] or contralateral ventricular catheter decompresses the brain early (critical for interhemispheric dissection). Place on the side opposite the approach to avoid dislodgement.

### Transcortical incisions

- Route through **sulci and fissures**, not gyri, when possible.
- Prefer longer-but-safer pathways over shortest distance.
- Avoid eloquent cortex and underlying white matter tracts — navigation + [[operating-room-setup-and-workflow|intraoperative cortical mapping]] guide the choice.

### Electrophysiologic monitoring

- **Motor, SSEP, BAEP** routinely.
- Any decline triggers: reconsider further transgression, reposition retractor, briskly debulk an expanding intralesional hemorrhage, drain CSF.

### Navigation and brain shift

MRI navigation is critical for transcortical/transcallosal incision accuracy. **Intraoperative ultrasound** complements navigation after ventricular CSF drainage distorts registration.

### Bleeding and vascular control

- Control the tumor's vascular supply **early** — piecemeal resection in narrow corridors demands a dry field.
- Isolate the rest of the ventricular system with cotton patties to prevent blood tracking.
- Place a cotton patty at the **foramen of Monro** to block blood pooling in the lateral ventricles.
- **Indiscriminate retraction and impulsive coagulation of ventricular walls are the two most common causes of new postoperative deficits** — both occur as panic responses to torrential bleeding. Use patient irrigation and targeted bipolar, not aggressive wall coagulation.

### Venous preservation

> *"There are no clear and reliable principles to determine which veins should be preserved and which can be safely sacrificed."*

- One **septal vein** may be sacrificed if necessary.
- **Thalamostriate and internal cerebral veins are indispensable.** Unilateral thalamostriate sacrifice is neither safe nor necessary in Cohen-Gadol's practice.
- Preoperative angiography / MRV clarifies dominance.

### Resection completeness

Subtotal resection is preferable to radical resection when the tumor involves ventricular walls, **fornices**, thalamus, or basal ganglia. **Forniceal manipulation produces memory dysfunction even with minimal injury** — leave tumor behind if needed.

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- Plan the corridor **along the tumor's long axis**; working angle > aperture at depth
- **Contralateral interhemispheric** routes expand angles with less ipsilateral retraction
- **Contralateral ventricular drain** decompresses the brain without crowding the field
- Never reach for the bipolar reflexively at a bleeding wall — use irrigation and patience
- **Fornix, thalamostriate vein, internal cerebral vein** are sacrosanct
- Pineal region masses: **biopsy first, resect second** (rule out germ cell tumor)
