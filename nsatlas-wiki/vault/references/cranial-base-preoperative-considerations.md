# Cranial Base — Preoperative Considerations, Instrumentation, and Patient Positioning

*Source: NSAtlas, Cohen-Gadol*

Cushing operated a staged subtemporal + suboccipital resection of a petroclival cholesteatoma in 1928 — a reminder that skull base surgery was technically ambitious long before the modern microscope. What changed is how comprehensively the team prepares before the first cut. This chapter formalizes the **preoperative, instrument, and positioning decisions** that precede every skull base case. Pair with [[cranial-base-surgery-general-principles]] and [[cranial-base-surgery-lessons-learned]].

## Preoperative Considerations

Every aspect — from ASA stratification through discharge rehab — is planned upfront.

### Imaging hierarchy

- **MRI** — contrast-enhanced T1, T2, FIESTA/CISS for cranial nerves
- **CT thin-slice bone window** — temporal bone pneumatization, hyperostosis, vascular canals, tumor-related bony invasion; guides Simpson-I planning and custom implants
- **CTA / MRA** — arterial encasement and relationship to the corridor
- **MRV / CTV** — **the venous phase is not optional.** Sacrifice of a skull base vein often yields a disabling venous infarction
- **Catheter angiography** — selectively, for hypervascular tumors (glomus jugulare), preop embolization, or when **BTO** is contemplated before ICA sacrifice

### Corridor-altering venous anatomy

- **Vein of Labbé position** alters or prohibits the petrosal approach
- **Tentorial-crossing veins** prevent tentorial division during petrosal approaches
- **Patency of major sinuses** engulfed by tumor affects the planned extent of resection

### Clinical workup

- **Neuro-ophthalmology** — visual fields, acuity, RAPD for any parasellar/sellar case
- **Neurotology** — audiogram, vestibular testing for CPA and petrosal work
- **Endocrinology** — any sellar/hypothalamic touch
- **Anesthesia + IOM** — CN EMG, SSEP/MEP, BAERs matched to corridor
- **Rehabilitation plan** — pre-discharge coordination for CN-injury anticipation

See [[cranial-base-operative-corridor-selection]] for the anatomic decision tree that converts this workup into a corridor.

## Instrumentation

**Philosophy: a limited, curated set outperforms a large menu.** See also [[instrumentation]] from the Principles volume.

### Bipolar forceps

- **Irrigating bipolars** with variable length and tip width
- **Fine tips with minimal charring** — essential around cranial nerves and brainstem
- **Slightly increased opening tension** allows spring-spreading dissection of thin arachnoid — **not** to be used on thick arachnoid (pial injury, perforator avulsion)

### Dissectors

- **A small, well-selected set** over a large rarely-used one
- Width and sharpness matched to dissection scale
- **Angled dissectors** for working around corners — always **under direct vision**; blind/blunt dissection causes perforator injury and pial transgression
- **Cohen-Gadol's favored pieces**:
  - **Rhoton #6** — his most-used dissector, period
  - **Rosen knife** — tumor-pial interface tracking
  - **Ball-tipped probe** — pursues arachnoid planes across adherent gyri (Sylvian split) or fine vessels adherent to tumor capsule
  - **Bayoneted** dissectors for posterior fossa
  - **Customized angled sharp-tipped instruments** for mobilizing fibrous tumors off the brainstem (acoustics, meningiomas)

### Microscissors and blades

- **Curved microscissors** for sharp dissection under direct vision; multiple lengths/blade sizes
- **Never** sharp-dissect blind — the temptation rises with fatigue and must be refused
- **"Round knife"** for Sylvian fissure dissection and peeling CN off tumor capsule
- **Standard sharp blades** for adherent arachnoid planes

### Forceps — the two-handed grammar

- **Tumor forceps / suction tip** (one hand) mobilizes capsule, tensions the arachnoid
- **Fine forceps** (other hand) grab the **arachnoid envelope of the cranial nerve** and peel it off the capsule — this is the [[cranial-base-surgery-lessons-learned|arachnoid-as-handle]] principle. The nerve itself is never grasped.

### Ultrasonic aspirator

- **Aggressive intracapsular debulking** for firm tumors before capsule mobilization
- Narrow skull base corridors + adherent tumor = piecemeal resection is mandatory. Inadequate debulking forces excessive capsule manipulation → traction injury.

## The Arachnoid-as-Handle Principle (Detailed)

The two-handed grammar central to Cohen-Gadol's cranial-base technique:

1. **Dominant hand** holds **tumor forceps** or the **suction tip** — mobilizes the **tumor capsule** away from the brainstem and cranial nerves, applying tension across the intervening arachnoid envelope.
2. **Non-dominant hand** holds **fine forceps** — grasps the **arachnoid encapsulating the cranial nerves** and peels it off the tumor capsule.
3. **The nerve itself is never grasped.** The arachnoid is the "handle"; the nerve is the protected cargo.
4. **Sharp microscissors** transect the arachnoid under direct vision once the plane is developed.

This sequence protects CN V, VII, IX, X during meningioma and schwannoma capsule mobilization — the alternative (blunt spread against the nerve surface) causes ischemic and traction injury even when the nerve appears intact intraoperatively.

## Adherent Fibrous Tumors and the Customized Angled Dissector

Fibrous tumors (some acoustic neuromas, meningiomas) resist the standard peeling sequence:

- **Aggressive intracapsular debulking with ultrasonic aspirator** first — creates working space inside the tumor.
- **Customized angled, sharp-tipped dissector** mobilizes residual capsule off the brainstem in the deep narrow corridor.
- **Piecemeal resection** is mandatory in skull base narrow corridors — inadequate debulking forces excessive capsule traction → brainstem/CN injury.
- Persistent en-bloc attempts in adherent lesions are a failure mode — switch to piecemeal explicitly.

## Why Blind Sharp Dissection Grows Tempting Late in the Case

A specific pitfall warning: **sharp dissection without direct visualization is dangerous at all times, but temptation rises as the surgeon fatigues late in the operation**. Fatigue erodes patience; patience is the only thing preventing a blind snip. Countermeasures:

- **Seated posture + armrests** (see [[surgeons-philosophy-and-operating-position]]) delays fatigue.
- **Mouthswitch efficiency** keeps focus on the dissection, not on scope adjustment.
- **Assistant calls out the plane** when the primary surgeon's attention drifts.
- **Short scheduled pauses** (hemostasis check, irrigation, brief step-back) reset attention before the closing dissection.

## Patient Positioning

See [[patient-positioning]] for the underlying algorithm. Skull base additions:

### General principles

- **Cranial venous return is the first priority.** Nonphysiologic neck postures are minimized
- **All pressure points padded** — skull base operations run long
- **Seated surgeon during microsurgery** whenever supine/park-bench permits; gravity clears CSF and blood from the working field
- **Mouthpiece** maintains bimanual continuity — see [[cerebrovascular-microscope-technique]]

### Anterolateral skull base (pterional, orbitozygomatic, fronto-orbital)

- **Supine, head turned 20–30° away** from the lesion
- **Neck slightly extended** so **malar eminence is the highest point**
- Frontal lobes fall away from the anterior fossa floor
- Same position as [[pterional-craniotomy]]

### Midline suboccipital / retromastoid

- **Lateral or park-bench** — **not** prone
- Avoids neck rotation/torsion, preserves venous drainage, reduces postop neck pain
- Allows seated operating for microsurgery

### Anterior and posterior petrosectomy, transcondylar

- **Supine with shoulder roll + head turn** so **SSS is parallel to the floor** (supple-neck patient)
- **Lateral** position for stiff necks, obesity, or venous hypertension risk
- **Vertex tilted downward** so **zygoma is highest point** — temporal lobe falls away from middle fossa floor
- See [[anterior-petrosectomy]] and [[posterior-petrosectomy]] for corridor-specific detail

### Anterior fossa (supraorbital, bifrontal)

- **Supine, head and torso elevated 15–20°**
- **Skull clamp or horseshoe** (horseshoe acceptable when no microsurgery planned)
- **Neck slightly extended** so frontal lobes fall from the anterior fossa floor
- See [[supraorbital-craniotomy]] and [[bifrontal-craniotomy]]

### Cohen-Gadol's abandonment of prone

- Prone is **abandoned** for lateral oblique / park-bench in all posterior corridors
- More physiologic neck, better venous return, easier seated microsurgery

## Skull Base Osteotomy Inventory

Cohen-Gadol's working list of "skull base" approaches extends beyond the classic five:

| Osteotomy | Target |
|-----------|--------|
| Orbitozygomatic | Parasellar, giant ICA, basilar apex |
| Fronto-orbital | Anterior fossa, parasellar |
| Extended pterional | Most medial SW meningiomas (see [[medial-sphenoid-wing-meningioma]]) |
| Anterior petrosal (Kawase) | Upper petroclivus, Meckel's cave |
| Posterior petrosal | Petroclival with hearing sacrifice |
| Transcondylar / far lateral | Ventrolateral lower brainstem, CC junction |
| Midline suboccipital | Fourth ventricle, pineal, torcular |
| Retromastoid / [[retromastoid-craniotomy]] | CPA, CN V/VII/VIII |

The decision tree is formalized in [[cranial-base-operative-corridor-selection]].

## Pearls and Pitfalls

- **Veins first.** Check MRV before finalizing corridor; a sacrificed vein can outweigh any exposure gained
- **Curate instruments.** A few tools used expertly beat a tray of unused variety
- **Never sharp-dissect blind** — especially late in the case
- **Arachnoid-as-handle** for CN peeling; never grab the nerve
- **Seated operating + mouthpiece** when geometry allows
- **Prone is obsolete** for posterior fossa — park-bench / lateral oblique instead
- **Zygoma / malar eminence as the highest point** orients gravity retraction for the two most common skull base corridors
