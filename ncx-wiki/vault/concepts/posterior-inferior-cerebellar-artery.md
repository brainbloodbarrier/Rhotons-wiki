# Posterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery (PICA)

The posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) is typically the largest branch of the vertebral artery, with the most variable anatomy of any cerebellar artery. It supplies the inferior cerebellum (tonsil, inferior vermis, posterior-inferior hemisphere), the lateral medulla, and the choroid plexus of the fourth ventricle.

## Segmentation (Rhoton — 5 segments)

| Segment | Course | Key relations |
|---------|--------|---------------|
| **p1 — Anterior medullary** | VA origin → inferior olive | Anterior to medulla; hypoglossal rootlets |
| **p2 — Lateral medullary** | Around olive → glossopharyngeal/vagal rootlets | Runs between CN IX-XI rootlets |
| **p3 — Tonsillomedullary (caudal loop)** | Between medulla and cerebellar tonsil | Forms the caudal loop; perforators to lateral medulla arise here |
| **p4 — Telovelotonsillar (cranial loop)** | Between tonsil and tela choroidea | Forms cranial loop near fourth-ventricle roof |
| **p5 — Cortical** | Over inferior cerebellum | Divides into medial (vermian) and lateral (hemispheric) trunks |

The caudal and cranial loops have critical perforator supply to the lateral medulla. Sacrifice of PICA proximal to these perforators causes **lateral medullary syndrome** (Wallenberg).

## Origin and Variants

- **Standard**: from the intracranial V4 segment of the vertebral artery, ~15 mm proximal to the VA-basilar junction.
- **Extradural origin** (15–20 %): arises from extradural V3 or the C1-C2 region, entering the dura with the vertebral artery.
- **AICA-PICA common trunk**: shared origin from the basilar artery instead of VA.
- **Absent PICA** (~10 %): territory supplied by enlarged ipsilateral AICA.
- **Duplicated PICA**: two separate VA branches, typically one to hemisphere and one to vermis.

## Surgical Relevance

### PICA Aneurysms
Most common posterior-circulation aneurysm location. Typical sites:
- **VA-PICA junction** (most common): accessed via far-lateral / suboccipital approach.
- **Distal PICA (p3-p5)**: approached via midline suboccipital; often associated with AVMs.

### PICA AVMs
Lawton classifies cerebellar AVMs with PICA dominance as the "suboccipital" / "petrosal" / "tentorial" subtypes. PICA-fed AVMs often drain via inferior cerebellar veins to the transverse or sigmoid sinus.

### Lateral Medullary Infarct (Wallenberg)
PICA occlusion proximal to the lateral medullary perforators produces:
- Ipsilateral Horner, facial hemianesthesia (V), ataxia, bulbar weakness (IX/X — dysphagia, hoarseness)
- Contralateral body hemianesthesia (spinothalamic)
- Vertigo, nystagmus, nausea

### PICA Revascularization (Lawton)
When the PICA origin must be sacrificed (fusiform or blister aneurysm, dolichoectatic), options include:
- **OA–PICA bypass** (occipital artery to PICA, p3 tonsillomedullary segment) — see [[reimplantation-bypass]], [[in-situ-bypass]]
- **PICA–PICA in-situ bypass** (side-to-side across the midline caudal loop)
- **VA reimplantation** of PICA
