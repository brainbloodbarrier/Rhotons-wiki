# Pericallosal Artery

The pericallosal artery is the distal continuation of the [[anterior-cerebral-artery|ACA]] after the ACoA complex, running along the dorsal surface of the corpus callosum in the pericallosal cistern (part of the [[basal-cisterns]] system). It corresponds to segments A3–A5 of the ACA.

## Segments

| Segment | Course | Key branches |
|---------|--------|-------------|
| **A3 (precallosal)** | ACoA → genu of corpus callosum | Orbitofrontal, frontopolar |
| **A4 (supracallosal)** | Above body of corpus callosum | Callosomarginal, internal frontal (anterior/middle/posterior) |
| **A5 (postcallosal)** | Around splenium | Inferior parietal, superior parietal, precuneal |

The **callosomarginal artery** (largest A4 branch) runs in the cingulate sulcus and is often mistaken for the pericallosal trunk itself. The branching pattern varies — a dominant callosomarginal may replace A4 distal flow.

## Surgical Relevance

### Pericallosal Aneurysms
- Arise at the A3 bifurcation (pericallosal–callosomarginal junction), typically at the genu of the corpus callosum.
- Represent 4–9 % of intracranial aneurysms.
- Accessed via [[anterior-interhemispheric-approach]]; the [[interhemispheric-fissure]] corridor navigates between [[superior-sagittal-sinus]] bridging veins to the cingulate sulcus.
- High re-rupture risk due to firm adherence to the corpus callosum and limited proximal control.

### Pericallosal AVMs
- Deep medial frontal AVMs with pericallosal feeders; draining veins reach the [[superior-sagittal-sinus]] or internal cerebral veins.
- Surgical treatment described in Lawton's Seven AVMs as the pericallosal category.

### Bypass Donor/Recipient
- Distal A3 is an uncommon but described recipient for [[reimplantation-bypass]] and [[in-situ-bypass]] configurations (e.g., A3-A3 side-to-side) when proximal ACA sacrifice is required.

## Cortical Territory

Supplies the medial frontal and parietal cortex:
- Cingulate gyrus
- Supplementary motor area (pre-SMA / SMA proper)
- Paracentral lobule (medial M1/S1 — leg representation)
- Precuneus

Occlusion produces contralateral leg weakness, urinary incontinence (SMA/cingulate), and, in dominant-hemisphere lesions, transcortical motor aphasia.
