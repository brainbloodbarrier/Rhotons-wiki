# Parieto-Occipital Arteriovenous Malformation

The parietal and occipital lobes are treated as a unit because their vascular anatomy is interrelated and AVMs in both lobes are managed similarly. The parieto-occipital unit is unique in receiving supply from all three major cerebral arteries ([[middle-cerebral-artery]], [[anterior-cerebral-artery]], [[posterior-cerebral-artery]]), unlike the frontal and temporal lobes which receive from only two. The lateral parieto-occipital AVM is the second most common subtype overall (n = 52, 9%).

## Microsurgical Anatomy

### Brain Surfaces

The parieto-occipital lobe has three surfaces:

| Surface | Key Structures | Eloquence |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral | Postcentral gyrus, SPL, IPL (supramarginal + angular gyri), SOG, IOG | Somatosensory strip, visual cortex, arcuate fasciculus (speech conduction) |
| Medial | Posterior [[paracentral-lobule]], precuneus, cuneus, lingula, calcarine fissure | Visual cortex (calcarine banks), somatosensory leg |
| Basal | Lower lingula, posterior fusiform gyrus, IOG | Visual pathway (optic radiations) |

### Arterial Supply

**MCA** -- distal M4 cortical arteries from the [[sylvian-fissure]]: central, anterior parietal, posterior parietal, [[angular-artery]], and temporo-occipital arteries. These supply the lateral surface.

**ACA** -- terminal A5 branches: superior and inferior parietal arteries from the [[pericallosal-artery]], supplying the medial parietal surface (precuneus).

**PCA** -- the dominant supply, including calcarine artery (P4 segment through the calcarine fissure to the visual cortex), parieto-occipital artery (in the parieto-occipital fissure, extending to convexity), splenial artery (anastomosing with [[pericallosal-artery]] as the "limbic loop"), and posterior temporal arteries to the basal surface.

### Venous Drainage

Ascending veins predominate to the [[superior-sagittal-sinus]]: postcentral, anterior/posterior parietal, and occipital veins, with the vein of Trolard as the largest. A 4-5 cm zone along the distal SSS is free of large bridging veins -- exploited for [[posterior-interhemispheric-approach]]. Medial drainage goes deep to the [[vein-of-galen]] via posterior pericallosal vein and anterior calcarine vein. Basal veins course to tentorial and [[transverse-sinus]].

## Four Parieto-Occipital AVM Subtypes

### 1. Lateral Parieto-Occipital AVM

The second most common subtype overall and half of all parieto-occipital AVMs. Convexity AVM with cortical base and conical extension toward the [[lateral-ventricle]]. Fed mainly by MCA cortical arteries with minimal ACA/PCA contribution. Simple convexity craniotomy without crossing venous sinuses. Patient positioned laterally; "nose down" for occipital lesions. Parietal AVMs are eloquent when in the postcentral gyrus; occipital AVMs when near the visual cortex. The angular/supramarginal gyrus location involves speech conduction tracts in the arcuate fasciculus connecting [[wernicke-area]] and [[broca-area]]. Deep extension may reach the atrium with [[lateral-posterior-choroidal-artery]] supply, connecting to [[ventricular-avm]] anatomy.

### 2. Medial Parieto-Occipital AVM

Based in the posterior [[interhemispheric-fissure]] without lateral extension. Fed by PCA branches (calcarine artery, parieto-occipital artery) and ACA branches, but not MCA. Exposed through a torcular craniotomy and [[posterior-interhemispheric-approach]] with gravity retraction. The 4-5 cm bridging-vein-free zone along the distal SSS is exploited for this approach. PCA feeders may be transit arteries requiring skeletonization to preserve occipital perfusion. The perpendicular approach demands nidal mobilization medially into the fissure.

### 3. Paramedian Parieto-Occipital AVM

Occupies both medial and lateral surfaces at the medial corner. The only cerebral AVM subtype fed by all three cerebral arteries (MCA + ACA + PCA), making it the most robustly supplied. Requires midline vertical positioning ("nose up" for parietal, "nose down" for occipital). Parietal variants (75%) use biparietal craniotomy; occipital variants use torcular craniotomy. Crossing the SSS opens both surfaces. Parallel attack achievable with aggressive table rotation.

### 4. Basal Occipital AVM

The least common parieto-occipital subtype (~10%). Located on the basal undersurface along the tentorium. Fed almost exclusively by PCA (PosTempA and calcarine artery). Exposed through torcular craniotomy crossing the [[transverse-sinus]] to access the supratentorial-infraoccipital plane. Visual pathways (optic radiations and visual cortex) are intimately associated, demanding tight dissection planes.

## Resection Principles

- **Gravity retraction** is the key technique for medial subtypes; the torcular craniotomy with proper positioning allows the occipital pole to sag away from the falx
- **Three arterial territories** in paramedian AVMs require interruption of separate fronts from MCA, ACA, and PCA
- **Visual preservation** -- the calcarine fissure houses the visual cortex and the optic radiations traverse occipital white matter lateral to the occipital horn
- **Transit arteries** -- PCA branches to the occipital pole must be preserved through skeletonization when they transit past the nidus
- **Deep planes** may connect to the atrium or occipital horn of the [[lateral-ventricle]], with choroidal artery supply paralleling [[ventricular-avm]] deep planes

Parieto-occipital AVMs differ from [[frontal-avm]] and [[temporal-avm]] because of their more robust multi-territory arterial supply and the surgical challenges in reaching the posterior brain. They require careful conceptualization of craniotomy, exposure, and positioning to optimize gravity retraction.
