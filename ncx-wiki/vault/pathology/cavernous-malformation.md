# Cavernous Malformation

Low-flow vascular lesions composed of thin, dilated sinusoidal channels lined by a single layer of leaky endothelium without intervening brain parenchyma. Also known as cavernomas, cavernous hemangiomas, and cavernous angiomas.

## Epidemiology

- **Prevalence:** 0.34–0.53% (mean 0.49%) — ~1 in 200 individuals
- **Distribution:** 80% supratentorial, 15% brainstem/basal ganglia, 5% spinal cord
- **Presentation peak:** 3rd–4th decade of life
- **Sex distribution:** Equal, though women may have higher symptomatic hemorrhage rates
- **Forms:** Sporadic (94%) vs familial (6%)
  - Sporadic: usually 1–2 lesions, no family history
  - Familial: multiple lesions, autosomal dominant, strong family history of seizures

## Pathophysiology

- Endothelium-lined sinusoidal cavities without tight junctions, smooth muscle, or adventitia
- "Mulberry" gross appearance from undulating thrombosis/recanalization
- Surrounded by gliotic border (not encapsulated)
- Hemosiderin deposition from recurrent microhemorrhage
- **Dynamic lesions** — exhibit growth, regression, and de novo formation
- Distinguished from capillary telangiectasias by absence of intervening brain tissue

### Genetics (Familial Forms)

Three causative genes (autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance):

| Gene | Locus | Frequency | Function |
|------|-------|-----------|----------|
| KRIT1 (CCM1) | 7q | 56% | Scaffold/effector protein |
| MGC4607 (CCM2) | 7p | 33% | Sequesters KRIT1 in nucleus |
| PDCD10 (CCM3) | 3q | 6% | Apoptosis during arterial morphogenesis |

All three form a single complex affecting downstream signaling and endothelial cell-cell junctions.

## Zabramski Classification

| Type | MRI Signal | Pathology | Hemorrhage Risk |
|------|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **IA** | T1/T2 hyperintense hemorrhage extending through lesion wall | Overt extralesional hemorrhage | Almost all symptomatic; up to **25%/year** (brainstem: 60%/year) |
| **IB** | T1/T2 hyperintense intralesional hemorrhage | Subacute intralesional hemorrhage | Brainstem: 5–10%/year; supratentorial asymptomatic: 0.5–1%/year |
| **II** | Reticulated "popcorn" mixed signal core + hypointense rim | Loculated hemorrhage/thrombosis of varying age | Symptomatic: 4–5%/year; asymptomatic: 0.5–1%/year |
| **III** | T2 hypointense with magnifying rim | Chronic resolved hemorrhage with hemosiderin | Rarely symptomatic; <0.5%/year |
| **IV** | Punctate on GRE only | Telangiectasias | Never symptomatic; very low risk |

## Natural History

### Hemorrhage Rates

| Scenario | Annual Hemorrhage Rate |
|----------|----------------------|
| Overall (retrospective) | 0.25–2.3% per patient/year |
| Symptomatic with prior hemorrhage | 4.5%/year |
| Incidental lesions | 0.6%/year |
| Seizure presentation | 0.4%/year |
| Deep-seated (brainstem/basal ganglia) | 10.6%/year |
| Superficial lesions | ~0%/year |
| Type IA overt extralesional hemorrhage | Up to 25.2%/year |
| Familial — overall | 2%/year (symptomatic: 1.1%/year) |

**Key factors predicting hemorrhage:** Location (deep > superficial), prior hemorrhage (rehemorrhage > first bleed), Zabramski type (IA > IB > II > III).

### Pregnancy

Symptomatic hemorrhage rate during pregnancy is ~3% — not significantly different from natural history. Pregnancy should NOT influence neurosurgical management; delivery method should be based on obstetrical considerations alone.

## Clinical Presentation

| Presentation | Frequency | Mechanism |
|--------------|-----------|-----------|
| **Seizures** | 38–70% | Hemosiderin → iron → epileptogenic (supratentorial) |
| **Hemorrhage** | 8–37% | Intralesional or extralesional bleeding |
| **Focal neurologic deficits** | 10–25% | Mass effect, hemorrhage near eloquent areas |
| **Incidental** | ~20% | Found on MRI for unrelated complaints |

### By Location

- **Supratentorial:** Seizures predominate (35–70% of symptomatic); refractory to medication in 40%
- **Brainstem:** Sudden focal deficits from hemorrhage; symptoms maximal at onset, gradual resolution; recurrence leads to progressive impairment
- **Spinal cord:** (1) Sudden motor/sensory loss from major hemorrhage, or (2) progressive myelopathy from repeated minor bleeds; pain is dominant symptom; recurrence up to 48%

## Diagnostic Imaging

| Modality | Findings |
|----------|----------|
| **MRI (gold standard)** | Reticulated "salt-and-pepper" on T2; hypointense hemosiderin rim; no mass effect (unless hemorrhage) |
| **GRE sequences** | Marked sensitivity to hemosiderin — reveals lesions invisible on T1/T2 (avg 5 lesions on T2 vs 20 on GRE in familial) |
| **T1 sequences** | Best anatomic detail for surgical planning; gadolinium reveals associated [[developmental-venous-anomaly]] (DVAs; present in ~1/3) |
| **CT** | Acute hemorrhage, focal calcifications; sensitivity ~50% |
| **Angiography** | Usually negative (low-flow); may show delayed venous pooling or associated DVA |

**Planning:** T2/GRE overestimate lesion size — use T1 sequences for surgical planning. Identify associated DVA (must be preserved during surgery).

## Treatment

### Indications (Barrow Neurological Institute Protocol)

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|----------|---------------|
| Asymptomatic Type IB, II, III | Conservative; annual MRI → 3–5 year intervals if stable |
| Type IA (overt extralesional hemorrhage) | Surgery for all accessible lesions (rehemorrhage up to 25%/year) |
| Medically refractory epilepsy | Surgery — earlier resection yields better seizure-free outcomes (98% with single seizure vs 69% with chronic epilepsy) |
| Brainstem with expansion/type change | Lower threshold for surgery; even intrinsic lesions without pial presentation can be safely approached |
| Spinal cord dorsal Types IB/II reaching pial surface | Consider surgery (higher risk of symptomatic hemorrhage) |
| Spinal cord ventral | Conservative in asymptomatic patients |

### Surgical Principles

- **Supratentorial:** Approach based on proximity to cortical surface; deep lesions via transsylvian, interhemispheric, or transcortical corridors guided by neuronavigation and intraoperative MRI
- **Brainstem:** Two-point method with safe entry zones; 300 cases at BNI with acceptable morbidity (Abla et al)
- **Spinal cord:** Dorsal midline myelotomy for posterior lesions; ventral lesions carry higher surgical risk
- **Associated DVA:** MUST be preserved — obliteration causes venous infarction
- **Hemosiderin rim:** Consider resection in epilepsy cases to improve seizure control; chronic hemosiderin = secondary epileptogenic focus

### Multiple/Familial Lesions

- Symptomatic hemorrhages and refractory seizures → surgical candidates
- Video EEG may be needed for seizure focus localization among multiple lesions
- Asymptomatic lesions → generally NOT resected (de novo formation continues at 0.2–0.4 new lesions/patient/year)
- Seizure control >80% with careful localization (Rocamora et al)
