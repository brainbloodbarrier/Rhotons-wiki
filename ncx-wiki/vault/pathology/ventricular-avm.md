# Ventricular Arteriovenous Malformation

Ventricular and periventricular AVMs are special: they reside in ventricular chambers "floating" in CSF, making circumdissection easier than any parenchymal AVM. They account for approximately 7% of brain AVMs -- second in rarity only to [[brainstem-avm]]. Despite the accessible ependymal plane, their deep location lengthens and limits surgical corridors, requiring intimate knowledge of choroidal fissure anatomy and the deep venous system.

## Microsurgical Anatomy

### Ventricular System

The [[lateral-ventricle]] is composed of five parts (frontal horn, body, atrium, occipital horn, temporal horn), but [[choroid-plexus]] is present only in the body, atrium, and temporal horn -- accounting for the absence of AVMs in the frontal and occipital horns.

Five C-shaped structures wrap around the thalamus in the ventricular wall: the ventricle itself, [[choroid-plexus]], [[fornix]], [[caudate-nucleus]], and [[corpus-callosum]]. The choroidal fissure between the [[fornix]] and [[thalamus]] is the critical surgical landmark for ventricular AVM access.

### Arterial Supply

Three choroidal arteries supply ventricular AVMs:

| Artery | Origin | Course | Territory |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Anterior-choroidal-artery]] (AChA) | Supraclinoid ICA | Cisternal then plexal segment through temporal horn choroidal fissure | Temporal horn plexus |
| Medial posterior choroidal (mPChA) | P2 PCA (crural segment) | Encircles midbrain, through velum interpositum, "3 sign" on angiography | Ventricular body, third ventricle roof |
| Lateral posterior choroidal (lPChA) | Distal P2 PCA (ambient segment) | Through atrial choroidal fissure | Atrium plexus |

The [[corpus-callosum]] is supplied by [[pericallosal-artery]] (short and long callosal branches) and splenial artery. The "limbic loop" connects ACA and PCA circulations around the callosum.

### Venous Drainage

Ventricular veins are the key surgical landmarks: septal veins (medial frontal horn to [[foramen-of-monro]]), caudate veins (lateral frontal horn to thalamostriate vein), thalamostriate vein (in striothalamic sulcus), and internal cerebral veins (paired veins in velum interpositum from [[foramen-of-monro]] to [[vein-of-galen]]). The "venous angle" on angiography marks the thalamostriate vein's hairpin turn at the foramen.

## Four Ventricular AVM Subtypes

### 1. Callosal AVM

The most common ventricular subtype (>50%). Located in the [[corpus-callosum]] from rostrum to splenium. Fed by [[pericallosal-artery]] (transit artery requiring skeletonization) via short and long callosal branches. Posterior callosal AVMs also receive splenial artery supply from PCA. Drains deep to septal, caudate, and thalamostriate veins, and to the internal cerebral vein.

Exposed via bifrontal craniotomy and [[anterior-interhemispheric-approach]] with gravity retraction -- the same corridor used for [[pericallosal-artery-aneurysm]]. Splenial AVMs are better approached through a torcular craniotomy and [[posterior-interhemispheric-approach]]. Transcallosal dissection has a parallel attack angle; the deep ependymal plane is the easiest of all AVMs because one needs only to enter the ventricle. Lateral extension into frontal white matter may recruit [[lenticulostriate-arteries]], connecting to [[deep-avm]] anatomy.

### 2. Ventricular Body AVM

Midline AVM at the complex intersection of [[thalamus]], [[caudate-nucleus]], [[fornix]], septum, velum interpositum, and [[choroid-plexus]]. Only 12% of ventricular AVMs. The mPChA runs through the center and is the major feeder. Exposed through bifrontal craniotomy, [[anterior-interhemispheric-approach]], and [[transcallosal-approach]] to the right of the [[falx-cerebri]] (protecting dominant hemisphere veins). Transchoroidal fissure dissection opens the velum interpositum. The [[fornix]] is intimately related and must be handled gently; even though the AVM is surrounded by CSF, forniceal manipulation causes debilitating memory and cognitive impairment.

### 3. Atrial AVM

Located in the atrium, posterior and lateral to the body (20% of ventricular AVMs). Fed primarily by lPChA through the atrial choroidal fissure. Drained through medial and lateral atrial veins to internal cerebral vein and [[basal-vein-of-rosenthal]]. Resected through a parietal craniotomy and superior parietal lobule approach (transcortical corridor 6-9 cm above the inion, 3-5 cm lateral to midline). Differs from thalamic [[deep-avm]] that protrudes into the atrium but is embedded in the [[thalamus]].

### 4. Temporal Horn AVM

Based on the [[choroid-plexus]] in the temporal horn. Differs from the medial [[temporal-avm]] that lies medial to the horn. Primary supply from the plexal segment of [[anterior-choroidal-artery]]. Resected through temporal craniotomy and transcortical approach through ITG, staying beneath the optic radiations in Meyer's loop. Dissection proceeds along the choroidal fissure from proximal to distal. Final stages reach the [[basal-vein-of-rosenthal]] in the ambient cistern and remaining lPChA inputs.

## Resection Principles

- **Ependymal dissection** is the easiest of all AVM types because the deep side is surrounded by CSF
- **Choroidal fissure** opened on the fornix side (tenia fornicis) to avoid the [[thalamus]]
- **Transit arteries** -- the [[pericallosal-artery]], [[anterior-choroidal-artery]], and mPChA are frequently transit arteries requiring distal-to-proximal skeletonization
- **Fornix protection** is paramount; even CSF-surrounded AVMs can damage memory circuits through forniceal manipulation
- **Interventricular bleeding** -- septal arteries and ventricular veins must be carefully divided; an intraventricular clot obstructing the [[foramen-of-monro]] must be evacuated

Ventricular AVMs are completely different from parenchymal AVMs ([[frontal-avm]], [[temporal-avm]], [[parieto-occipital-avm]]) and from [[deep-avm]] in the adjacent central core, despite geographic proximity.
