# Brainstem Arteriovenous Malformation

Brainstem AVMs are the rarest of the seven AVM locations (4% in Lawton's series, 25 of 600 patients). All are eloquent and drain deeply, raising Spetzler-Martin grades to at least III. However, 70% are pial or "epipial" -- sitting on the brainstem surface rather than within parenchyma -- making them fundamentally different from cerebral AVMs. This pial location enables a unique strategy of **occlusion in situ**: complete arterial disconnection without resection, leaving the devascularized nidus on the brainstem surface.

## Microsurgical Anatomy

### Three Neurovascular Complexes

Posterior fossa anatomy occurs in threes:

| Complex | Brainstem | Artery | Peduncle | Fissure | Surface | Cranial Nerves |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Upper | [[Midbrain]] | [[superior-cerebellar-artery]] (SCA) | Superior cerebellar | Cerebellomesencephalic | Tentorial | CN III, IV, V |
| Middle | [[Pons]] | [[anterior-inferior-cerebellar-artery]] (AICA) | Middle cerebellar | Cerebellopontine | Petrosal | CN VI, VII, VIII |
| Lower | [[Medulla-oblongata]] | [[posterior-inferior-cerebellar-artery]] (PICA) | Inferior cerebellar | Cerebellomedullary | Suboccipital | CN IX, X, XI, XII |

### Brainstem Surfaces and Landmarks

Surface landmarks define AVM localization: cerebral peduncles and interpeduncular fossa (anterior midbrain), tectum/quadrigeminal plate (posterior midbrain), basilar sulcus (anterior pons), middle cerebellar peduncle (lateral pons), pyramids and olives (medulla), and the fourth ventricular floor (posterior pons/medulla). The **rhomboid fossa** landmarks include the facial colliculus, sulcus limitans, hypoglossal and vagal triangles, and the stria medullaris.

### Arterial Supply

Brainstem AVMs are fed by the first three segments of each cerebellar artery: anterior surfaces by s1/a1/p1 segments, lateral surfaces by s2/a2/p2 segments, posterior surfaces by s3/a3/p3 segments. Perforating arteries from the [[basilar-artery]] can supply anterior pontine AVMs directly. The meningohypophyseal trunk may contribute feeders along CN V through Meckel's cave.

### Venous Drainage

Brainstem veins include longitudinal veins (median and lateral anterior pontomesencephalic/medullary veins, lateral mesencephalic vein) and transverse veins (pontomesencephalic sulcus vein, pontomedullary sulcus vein). Anterior veins course laterally to the superior and inferior petrosal veins. Posterior/ascending veins reach [[basal-vein-of-rosenthal]] and [[vein-of-galen]].

## Six Brainstem AVM Subtypes

### 1. Anterior Midbrain AVM

On the anterior [[midbrain]] surface on or between the [[cerebral-peduncle]]. Associated with CN III. Fed by P1 PCA perforators (PosThaP, PedP). Exposed via [[orbitozygomatic-approach]] and transsylvian approach, identical to [[basilar-bifurcation-aneurysm]] exposure. The carotid-oculomotor triangle provides access to the interpeduncular fossa.

### 2. Posterior Midbrain AVM

On the tectum/quadrigeminal plate below the pineal gland. Fed bilaterally by long circumflex perforators (P1/P2 PCA) and s3 SCA branches. Drains via tectal vein and vein of the cerebellomesencephalic fissure to [[vein-of-galen]]. Exposed through torcular craniotomy and [[supracerebellar-infratentorial-approach]]. The sitting position with gravity retraction opens a retractor-less panorama of the quadrigeminal cistern.

### 3. Anterior Pontine AVM

On the rectangular anterior pontine surface between the basilar sulcus and CN V root. Fed by s1 SCA, a1 AICA, and direct [[basilar-artery]] branches. Meningohypophyseal trunk feeders travel along CN V from Meckel's cave. Accessed via extended [[retrosigmoid-approach]] with limited mastoidectomy and sigmoid sinus skeletonization. Working windows above (supratrigeminal triangle) and below (infratrigeminal triangle) CN V provide limited but critical access. Most common brainstem AVM subtype by some accounts.

### 4. Lateral Pontine AVM

On the triangular surface between CN V and the cerebellopontine fissure. Variable pial invasion into the middle cerebellar peduncle. Supplied by AICA (a2 and a3 segments). The brachium pontis surprisingly tolerates surgical transgression, making these the **easiest brainstem AVMs to resect**. The "back door" technique circumdissects through non-eloquent cerebellum laterally, then rolls the AVM anteriorly. Most common brainstem subtype (27%) and best surgical outcomes.

### 5. Anterior Medullary AVM

Below the pontomedullary sulcus, between the anterolateral sulci. Fed bilaterally by distal vertebral artery branches near the anterior spinal artery origins. Resectable only when ruptured with hematoma creating a posterior route through the fourth ventricle. Rare (4% of brainstem AVMs).

### 6. Lateral Medullary AVM

Below the pontomedullary sulcus, lateral to the anterolateral sulcus, posterior to CN XII rootlets. Visible through a [[far-lateral-approach]] at the PICA p2 segment location. Small, pial, and unilateral. Fed by direct VA and proximal PICA branches (p1/p2). Accessing the vagoaccessory, suprahypoglossal, and infrahypoglossal triangles exposes the lateral medullary surface. Two-thirds separate cleanly for resection; the remainder are occluded in situ. The same [[far-lateral-approach]] is used for [[pica-aneurysm]] clipping.

## Occlusion In Situ

This unique brainstem strategy represents Lawton's conservative alternative to resection:

1. Circumferential pial dissection occludes all arterial input
2. Draining vein darkens, confirming complete disconnection
3. ICG videoangiography confirms absence of arteriovenous shunting
4. Test occlusion with forceps assesses completeness before permanent vein division
5. Devascularized nidus left on brainstem surface

This strategy avoids intraparenchymal dissection entirely. Ruptured AVMs with hematoma cavities may permit a more aggressive resection strategy.

## Resection Principles

- **Perforator preservation** is absolute; the loss of a single brainstem perforator can be devastating
- **Pial confinement** -- dissection remains in the pia without brainstem transgression for pial AVMs
- **Occlusion in situ** is preferred when visualization is limited and perforators are at risk
- **Hemorrhage rate** of brainstem AVMs is higher than supratentorial AVMs (up to 15%/year), justifying aggressive treatment
- **Incomplete occlusion** after occlusion in situ produces a smaller radiosurgery target with lower dose requirements

Brainstem AVMs share posterior fossa corridor anatomy with [[cerebellar-avm]] but demand fundamentally different operative philosophy due to brainstem eloquence.
