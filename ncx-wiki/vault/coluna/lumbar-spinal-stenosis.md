# Lumbar Spinal Stenosis

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is the syndrome of symptomatic narrowing of the lumbar spinal canal, lateral recesses, or neural foramina, producing compression of the cauda equina or exiting nerve roots. It is the single most common indication for spine surgery in adults over 65 and a defining workload of any general neurosurgical practice. Although the radiographic finding is ubiquitous in older patients, the clinical entity is defined by the combination of canal narrowing **and** characteristic symptoms — radiographic stenosis without symptoms is not a disease.

## Epidemiology

- Symptomatic LSS prevalence rises sharply with age: < 5 % under 50, > 20 % over 70.
- Most common at L4–L5 (60–70 % of single-level cases), followed by L3–L4 and L2–L3; L5–S1 is comparatively spared.
- Multilevel involvement is the rule rather than the exception in patients beyond 70.
- Female predominance modest in degenerative spondylolisthesis-associated stenosis (anteroposterior facet orientation).
- Comorbidity overlap: knee and hip osteoarthritis, peripheral vascular disease, diabetes — affects both differential diagnosis and surgical decision-making.

## Anatomy of the Lumbar Canal

- The **central canal** is bordered anteriorly by the disc and posterior longitudinal ligament, laterally by the pedicles, and posteriorly by the lamina and ligamentum flavum.
- The **lateral recess** lies between the medial pedicle wall, the disc anteriorly, and the superior articular facet posteriorly — the corridor traversed by the descending root before it exits at the next level.
- The **neural foramen** is bordered superiorly and inferiorly by the pedicles, anteriorly by the disc-vertebral body, and posteriorly by the pars interarticularis and superior articular facet.
- A descending root may be compressed in any of the three zones; symptoms map to whichever root is involved (L4 root in the L3–L4 lateral recess, L5 in the L4–L5 lateral recess, etc.).

## Pathophysiology

LSS reflects the cumulative effect of multilevel degenerative changes, often described as the **degenerative cascade**:

1. **Disc desiccation and height loss** narrow the foramen and shift load to the posterior elements.
2. **Reactive osteophytosis** at the vertebral end-plates and uncovertebral joints encroaches on the canal.
3. **Facet hypertrophy and capsular thickening** narrow the lateral recess and contribute to dynamic stenosis with extension.
4. **Ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and buckling** narrow the canal, especially in extension; calcification or ossification (rare in lumbar spine) compounds the effect.
5. **Degenerative spondylolisthesis** (most commonly L4–L5 grade I) shifts the cranial vertebra forward, narrowing the canal and lateral recess and adding a mechanical (instability) component to the compressive picture.

The compressed nerve roots suffer mechanical injury and venous congestion. Walking and standing increase lumbar lordosis, reduce canal area, and impair venous drainage; flexion (sitting, leaning, riding a bike) reverses these effects — the anatomic basis for **neurogenic claudication's flexion-relieved pattern**.

## Clinical Presentation

### Symptoms
- **Neurogenic claudication.** The cardinal complaint: buttock, posterior thigh, and calf pain or heaviness with standing and walking, relieved by sitting or forward flexion. Patients describe a characteristic shopping-cart sign (improvement with leaning forward).
- **Radiculopathy.** Dermatomal pain in the lower extremity, often coexistent with claudication; an L4–L5 lateral-recess stenosis classically irritates the L5 root.
- **Subjective leg weakness** during ambulation that resolves with rest, distinct from constant motor deficit.
- **Sensory disturbance** in the lower extremities, often poorly localized.
- **Bladder symptoms** are uncommon and warrant careful exclusion of cauda equina syndrome.
- **Axial low-back pain** is often present but is not the dominant or surgically responsive complaint.

### Examination
- Neurologic exam may be normal at rest, especially when symptoms are exertion-dependent.
- **Provocative tests**: prolonged standing reproduction, reverse straight-leg raise less reliable.
- **Stoop test**: pain with extension, relief with flexion.
- Reflexes may be diminished at compressed roots (knee for L3–L4, ankle for S1).
- Motor exam: subtle weakness in tibialis anterior (L4), extensor hallucis longus (L5), or gastrocnemius (S1) is common; gross paresis is uncommon outside acute or severe disease.
- Sensory exam: dermatomal hypoesthesia.
- Pulses, ankle-brachial index, and skin trophic changes evaluate the vascular differential.

### Differential — vascular vs neurogenic claudication

| Feature | Neurogenic | Vascular |
|---|---|---|
| Pain pattern | Buttock, posterior thigh, calf | Calf > thigh, buttock rare |
| Provocation | Standing + walking | Walking only |
| Relief | Flexion (sitting, leaning) | Standing still |
| Time to onset / relief | Minutes / minutes | Seconds / seconds |
| Pulses, ABI | Preserved | Diminished |
| Walking uphill / biking | Often comfortable (flexed) | Worse |
| Walking downhill | Often worse (extended) | Often comfortable |

Coexistence is common in the elderly; do not assume the obvious diagnosis explains everything.

## Imaging

### MRI (modality of choice)
- T2 sagittal: sequential canal narrowing with cauda equina crowding; "trefoil" axial canal at the worst level.
- T2 axial: facet hypertrophy, ligamentum flavum thickening, lateral recess narrowing, disc protrusion.
- **Sedimentation sign**: in supine MRI, lumbar nerve roots normally settle to the dependent (posterior) part of the thecal sac. Failure of sedimentation — roots packed tightly throughout the cross-section — is a sensitive sign of clinically meaningful stenosis.
- Quantitative thresholds: AP canal diameter < 10 mm (or cross-sectional area < 75 mm²) defines absolute stenosis; lateral recess height < 3 mm is abnormal.

### Standing / dynamic radiographs
- Flexion-extension lateral views detect **dynamic instability**: > 3–4 mm translation or > 10° angular change between flexion and extension.
- Standing radiographs (rather than supine) are critical for spondylolisthesis grading — lying down often reduces the slip.

### CT and CT myelography
- Useful when MRI is contraindicated (pacemaker, claustrophobia) or when bone detail is the question (foraminal osteophytes, pars defects, prior fusion).
- CT myelography can clarify dynamic compression in upright positions.

### EMG / NCS
- Useful in confounded cases (peripheral neuropathy from diabetes coexisting with LSS) and to map symptom-discordant imaging.

## Severity and Functional Assessment

- **Oswestry Disability Index (ODI)** and **Zürich Claudication Questionnaire** are the dominant patient-reported instruments.
- **Walking distance / time before symptom onset** is a practical bedside metric.
- **VAS for leg and back pain**, separately, helps distinguish surgically responsive (leg) from less responsive (axial back) components.

## Differential Diagnosis

- Vascular claudication (peripheral arterial disease).
- Hip osteoarthritis (groin and anterior thigh pain, internal rotation reproduces).
- Trochanteric bursitis.
- Diabetic peripheral neuropathy.
- Lumbar disc herniation (acute, dermatomal, often younger patient).
- Spondylodiscitis or epidural abscess (fever, night pain, elevated inflammatory markers).
- Tumor — primary spine, metastasis, intradural lesion (red flag: unrelenting, night-worse pain).
- Inflammatory arthritis (axial spondyloarthritis).
- Conus medullaris or cauda equina lesions of non-degenerative cause.

## Natural History

- Most untreated patients are **stable or slowly progressive** rather than rapidly deteriorating.
- A minority (~ 30 %) improve modestly with conservative care alone; another minority worsens substantially.
- Acute neurologic deterioration is uncommon outside cauda equina syndrome and warrants exclusion of competing diagnoses.

## Treatment

### Non-operative
- **Activity modification** that exploits flexion-dependent relief — bicycle ergometer over treadmill, walking with a cart.
- **Physiotherapy** focused on lumbar flexion, abdominal core, and aerobic conditioning.
- **NSAIDs** for symptom control with attention to renal and gastric risk in elderly patients.
- **Gabapentinoids** for radicular pain when NSAIDs fail.
- **Epidural corticosteroid injections** provide short-to-medium-term symptomatic relief in selected patients but have not been shown to alter long-term trajectory; the LESS (Lumbar Epidural Steroid Injections for Stenosis) randomized trial showed no advantage of steroid over lidocaine alone at 6 weeks.
- **Bracing** has limited evidence in LSS and is generally not recommended.

### Surgical indications
- Persistent symptoms (typically ≥ 3–6 months) refractory to a reasonable trial of conservative care, with imaging-confirmed stenosis matching the symptoms.
- Progressive neurologic deficit.
- **Cauda equina syndrome** — saddle anesthesia, urinary retention with overflow incontinence, bilateral leg weakness — is a surgical emergency requiring decompression within 24–48 hours.

### Surgical approaches

#### Decompression alone (laminectomy / laminotomy)
- The standard for stenosis **without instability**.
- Open midline laminectomy, bilateral laminotomy, or "over-the-top" unilateral approach with bilateral decompression — the choice is largely surgeon preference and patient anatomy.
- Minimally invasive variants (tubular retractors, microscope or endoscope) reduce paraspinal muscle injury and shorten hospitalization; long-term outcomes are equivalent to open decompression in most series.

#### Decompression + fusion
Indicated for:
- **Degenerative spondylolisthesis** with mechanical back pain or instability, especially grade ≥ I with > 3 mm dynamic motion.
- **Iatrogenic instability** anticipated with extensive facetectomy required for decompression.
- **Recurrent stenosis** after prior decompression with associated instability.
- **Symptomatic deformity** — significant scoliosis or sagittal imbalance.

The Försth (Swedish) and Ghogawala (United States) trials, both 2016, addressed the spondylolisthesis question with conflicting nuance. Försth showed no benefit of fusion over decompression alone; Ghogawala showed superiority of decompression + fusion for SF-36 physical component summary at 2 years. Modern practice synthesizes: **stable grade I spondylolisthesis without dynamic instability** is reasonable to decompress alone; **dynamic instability or significant axial back pain** favors adding fusion.

#### Interspinous distraction devices
- An older alternative whose role has receded; high reoperation rates and inconsistent outcomes have moved most surgeons away from these implants.

### SPORT trial outcomes
The Spine Patient Outcomes Research Trial enrolled patients with LSS and randomized to surgical or non-operative treatment. At 2, 4, and 8 years, surgery produced statistically and clinically meaningful improvements in pain (SF-36 bodily pain) and function (ODI) over non-operative care. The benefit was largest at 1–2 years and gradually narrowed over time as some non-operative patients improved and as some surgical patients suffered recurrent symptoms. The SPORT data underpin the modern recommendation that surgery is appropriate for moderate-to-severe LSS that has not responded to a reasonable conservative trial.

## Complications

- **Dural tear** (5–10 %): primary repair, occasional fibrin glue or fat graft, supine bed rest typically not required after watertight repair in lumbar laminectomy.
- **Postoperative hematoma**: rare but serious; sudden new neurologic deficit demands urgent imaging and often re-exploration.
- **Nerve root injury** (1–2 %).
- **Infection** (1–4 %, higher in diabetes, prior surgery, instrumented fusion).
- **Recurrent stenosis** at the index level (~ 10 % at 5 years) or **adjacent-segment disease** (especially after fusion).
- **Pseudarthrosis** in fusions: 5–25 % depending on number of levels, technique, and risk factors (smoking, NSAIDs, malnutrition, osteoporosis).
- **Medical complications** in this elderly population: cardiac, pulmonary, thromboembolic, urinary retention.

## Special Considerations

### Cauda equina syndrome
- Bilateral leg weakness, saddle anesthesia, sphincter dysfunction (retention with overflow > frank incontinence), severe back pain.
- Demands emergent MRI and surgical decompression, ideally within 24–48 hours of presentation, but delays in retention beyond 48 hours portend incomplete bladder recovery.
- Differential includes acute large central disc herniation, hematoma, tumor — not all "cauda equina" is degenerative stenosis.

### Spondylolisthesis grading (Meyerding)
| Grade | Slip |
|---|---|
| I | 1–25 % |
| II | 26–50 % |
| III | 51–75 % |
| IV | 76–100 % |
| V | > 100 % (spondyloptosis) |

Most degenerative slips at L4–L5 are grade I.

### Coexistence with cervical disease
Patients with LSS often have asymptomatic cervical stenosis. Tandem stenosis (concurrent cervical and lumbar canal stenosis) is found in 5–10 % of operative LSS series; if cervical signs (hyperreflexia, gait disturbance from upper-motor-neuron signs) are present, evaluate for [[cervical-spondylotic-myelopathy]] before lumbar surgery. Symptoms can otherwise be misattributed.

### Age, frailty, and shared decision
- Modern frailty indices outperform raw chronologic age in predicting perioperative complications.
- Pre-operative optimization of glycemic control, smoking cessation, nutrition, and bone health measurably reduces surgical morbidity.
- The decision to operate in an octogenarian with mild-moderate symptoms involves the patient's goals, walking targets, and tolerance of perioperative risk — not a simple imaging cutoff.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Operate on symptoms, not pictures.** Radiographic stenosis without claudication or progressive deficit does not warrant surgery.
2. **Decompress what hurts.** Identify the symptomatic level by pairing imaging with the clinical exam; do not blindly decompress every stenotic level — adjacent decompressions may be unnecessary and adjacent fusions definitely so.
3. **Preserve the pars and lateral facet.** A bilateral laminotomy with medial facetectomy decompresses the lateral recess while preserving structural integrity, sparing fusion in many cases.
4. **Watch for occult instability.** Standing flexion-extension films often reveal mobility that supine MRI missed; instability changes the operation from decompression-alone to decompression + fusion.
5. **Counsel realistically.** Surgery improves leg pain and walking distance more reliably than back pain; patients whose dominant complaint is axial back pain should understand the realistic upper limit of expected improvement.
6. **Do not under-resect cauda equina syndrome.** Time-to-decompression matters; mobilize the OR aggressively and consult colleagues if delay seems likely.
7. **Address [[cervical-spondylotic-myelopathy]] first** when tandem stenosis presents with myelopathic signs — operating the lumbar spine first risks delaying needed cervical decompression and confuses the post-operative picture.
