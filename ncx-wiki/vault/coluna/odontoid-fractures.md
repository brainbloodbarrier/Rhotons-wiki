# Odontoid Fractures

Fractures of the odontoid process (dens) of C2 are the most common upper-cervical injury in adults and a defining workload of any spine trauma practice. The clinical and radiographic picture is deceptively simple — pain in the upper neck after a fall, a fracture line on CT — but the management decision draws on patient age, fracture geometry, transverse-atlantal-ligament integrity, and a mature understanding of nonoperative-failure rates that have evolved substantially over the past three decades.

## Anatomy

The **odontoid process** (dens) is the cranial projection of the C2 (axis) vertebral body, articulating with the anterior arch of C1 (atlas) anteriorly and the **transverse atlantal ligament (TAL)** posteriorly. The TAL is the principal restraint to anterior translation of C1 on C2; its integrity defines whether atlantoaxial instability is concurrent with the bony injury.

- **Apical ligament**: from odontoid tip to basion (foramen magnum); minor stabilizer.
- **Alar ligaments**: bilateral, from odontoid tip to occipital condyles; primary axial-rotation restraint.
- **Cruciate ligament complex**: TAL is the horizontal arm; vertical bands extend to the basion and C2 body.

The dens is supplied by the **anterior and posterior ascending arteries** (branches of the vertebral artery) entering at the base — a tenuous blood supply that explains the high nonunion rate of waist-level fractures.

## Epidemiology and Mechanism

- 10–15 % of cervical spine fractures overall; highest among upper-cervical fractures (followed by [[atlas-fractures]] of C1 and Hangman's fractures of C2 pars interarticularis).
- **Bimodal age distribution**:
  - Young adults: high-energy trauma — motor vehicle collisions, sports, falls from height. Often associated with multilevel cervical or other system injuries.
  - Elderly: low-energy ground-level falls, often osteoporotic; isolated injury common but high mortality from medical comorbidities.
- Female predominance modest in the elderly cohort; male predominance in young adults.
- Mortality at 1 year in elderly type II fractures is substantial (15–30 % across modern series), driven by cardiopulmonary, infectious, and decompensation events rather than direct cord injury.

## Classification

### Anderson-D'Alonzo (1974) — original

| Type | Anatomy | Stability | Healing potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Avulsion of the odontoid tip (alar ligament insertion) | Usually stable | Heals; rare |
| **II** | Fracture through the base / waist of the odontoid (cancellous-cortical junction) | Often unstable | **Poor — 30–40 % nonunion with immobilization alone** |
| **III** | Fracture extending into cancellous body of C2 | Usually stable | Generally heals (80–90 %) with rigid immobilization |

### Grauer 2005 — modified, treatment-oriented for type II

| Subtype | Geometry | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **IIA** | Nondisplaced, no comminution | May respond to immobilization in young patients |
| **IIB** | Displaced, transverse / oblique-anterior, reducible | **Anterior odontoid screw** is geometrically favorable |
| **IIC** | Oblique-posterior (anterior-superior to posterior-inferior), comminuted, displaced > 5 mm | **Anterior screw biomechanically unfavorable; posterior C1–C2 fusion preferred** |

The Grauer subdivision guides operative-versus-nonoperative decisions and approach selection in modern practice.

### AOSpine subaxial classification
Has a parallel structure for axis-level trauma (A0–A4 morphology, B / C ligamentous + neurologic) but is more useful for sub-axial fractures than for the dens.

## Clinical Presentation

- Severe upper neck pain, restriction of head motion.
- Suboccipital headache; pain on attempting flexion / extension / rotation.
- Patients often **support their head with their hands**; this and the "atlas-fracture" reluctance to move the head are characteristic.
- Cranial nerve palsies (IX–XII) — Collet-Sicard syndrome — suggest vertebral artery involvement or extensive injury.
- Neurologic deficit from cord injury is uncommon but devastating when present (high cervical SCI; see [[spinal-cord-injury]]).
- Vertebral artery injury (dissection, occlusion) in 5–15 % — often clinically silent acutely.
- Concurrent injuries: 50 % of upper-cervical fractures have **associated cervical injuries** at other levels — image the entire cervical spine.

## Imaging

### CT (modality of choice)
- Whole cervical spine; sagittal and coronal multiplanar reconstructions.
- Defines fracture geometry: type, displacement, angulation, comminution.
- Detects associated [[atlas-fractures]] (40–50 % concurrence in some series), Hangman fractures, lateral-mass fractures.

### MRI
- TAL integrity (best on T2 / proton density), cord injury, ligamentous injury, vertebral artery patency (MR angiography).
- Mandatory before deciding operative vs nonoperative course in any borderline case.
- TAL disruption converts an isolated odontoid fracture into a complex C1–C2 instability requiring posterior fusion regardless of fracture type.

### Plain radiographs
- Open-mouth (odontoid) view and lateral cervical view; supplanted by CT for the unstable trauma patient.
- Standing flexion-extension lateral views — only after acute stability is established and only in cooperative awake patients — assess dynamic instability.

### Vertebral artery imaging
- CT angiography (or MRA) reasonable in displaced fractures, fractures with C2 transverse foramen involvement, or any patient with cranial nerve / brainstem signs.

## Management

### Type I
- Stable; rigid cervical collar for 6–12 weeks.
- Surgery reserved for late instability or neurologic compromise.

### Type II — the management debate

The principal decision is between **rigid external immobilization** (halo vest or, in modern practice with caveats, semi-rigid collar in selected elderly) and **internal fixation**.

#### Nonoperative
- **Halo vest** for 12 weeks: classical option. Nonunion rates with halo alone: 30–40 % for type II in adults; substantially worse in age > 50, displacement > 5 mm, angulation > 10°, posterior displacement, and comminution.
- **Hard collar**: increasingly accepted in elderly type II as the "ground truth" alternative — halo vests have substantial pulmonary, swallowing, and skin morbidity in older adults that may exceed the cost of asymptomatic nonunion.
- **Acceptance of asymptomatic nonunion**: a fibrous union of the odontoid in an elderly patient with stable alignment can be a clinically acceptable outcome — the clinically meaningful endpoint is functional, not radiographic.

#### Operative

**Anterior odontoid screw (Magerl / Böhler)**
- One or two cannulated screws across the fracture line via an anterior cervical approach.
- **Indications**: reducible, transverse-anterior or shallow-oblique fracture (Grauer IIB), intact TAL, anatomy permitting an acceptable cranial approach angle (no severe kyphosis, large chest, edentulous mandible).
- **Advantages**: preserves C1–C2 motion (50 % of head rotation occurs at C1–C2), single-level fixation.
- **Disadvantages**: technical difficulty in elderly with osteoporotic bone; pseudarthrosis if reduction is incomplete; not biomechanically suited for IIC oblique-posterior.

**Posterior C1–C2 fusion**
- **Modern Harms / Goel technique**: C1 lateral-mass screws + C2 pedicle (or pars / translaminar) screws connected by rods.
- **Older Magerl transarticular screws**: still used in selected anatomy; Magerl is a single screw across the C1–C2 facet.
- **Indications**: irreducible fracture, oblique-posterior (Grauer IIC), comminuted, TAL disrupted, prior nonoperative failure, pathologic anatomy precluding anterior screw.
- **Advantages**: high fusion rates (> 90 %), forgiving of fracture geometry.
- **Disadvantages**: sacrifices C1–C2 rotation (~ 50 % of head rotation), longer surgical exposure, posterior approach with patient prone (challenging in trauma).

#### Combined occipitocervical fusion
- Reserved for atlantooccipital instability, severe comminution, or extension into the occipital condyles.
- Functionally significant motion sacrifice; patients note loss of nodding motion.

### Type III
- **Rigid collar** or **halo vest** for 12 weeks.
- Healing rates 80–90 %.
- Surgery reserved for nonunion, displacement progression, or neurologic deterioration.

## Special Populations

### Elderly (age > 70 with type II)
- Nonunion rates with halo immobilization 50–80 %; medical morbidity of halo substantial.
- Surgical fixation (typically posterior C1–C2 fusion for technical reasons) reduces nonunion to 5–15 % but carries operative morbidity.
- **Modern shared decision**: posterior fusion if patient is fit and walking; rigid collar with planned acceptance of asymptomatic fibrous union if frail or non-ambulatory.
- One-year mortality irrespective of treatment is high; choose for quality of life rather than radiographic cure.

### Pediatric
- Synchondrosis at the C2 base persists into adolescence; radiographic distinction from acute fracture requires age-appropriate interpretation.
- Most pediatric odontoid fractures are nondisplaced and heal with rigid immobilization.

### Pathologic fractures
- Metastatic disease, multiple myeloma, osteoporotic insufficiency.
- Treatment integrates oncologic and orthopedic considerations; cement augmentation, posterior instrumentation, and radiation may be combined.

## Complications

### Nonoperative
- Nonunion (the dominant concern for type II in adults).
- Halo-related: pin-site infection, scalp ulceration, pulmonary complications, swallowing disturbance, falls in elderly.
- Late instability: fibrous union may decompensate years later with minor trauma.

### Operative

**Anterior odontoid screw**
- Pseudarthrosis (5–15 %), screw cutout, esophageal / vascular injury, RLN palsy, swallowing dysfunction.

**Posterior C1–C2 fusion**
- Vertebral artery injury (1–4 %, higher in anomalous V3 anatomy; preoperative CTA mandatory).
- C2 nerve / greater occipital nerve neuralgia.
- C1 lateral-mass screw misplacement.
- Loss of head rotation (expected, not a complication).
- Wound complications, dural tear (uncommon).
- Hardware failure.

## Prognosis

- Type I, type III: generally good with appropriate immobilization.
- Type II in young patients: good results with either anterior screw (in geometry-favorable cases) or posterior fusion.
- Type II in elderly: outcome dominated by overall medical condition; mortality at 1 year 15–30 % regardless of treatment.
- Late atlantoaxial instability after fibrous union: rare but recognized; surveillance with periodic imaging is reasonable in patients with known nonunion.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Image the whole cervical spine.** 40–50 % concurrent cervical injury rate; an isolated odontoid fracture is the exception, not the rule.
2. **Check the TAL on MRI.** TAL disruption converts a planning question (anterior screw vs posterior fusion) into a decision (posterior fusion mandatory).
3. **Vertebral artery anatomy before posterior fixation.** Anomalous V3 course (high-riding, hypoplastic) is common and can preclude conventional pedicle-screw trajectory; pre-operative CTA changes the operation.
4. **Anterior screw geometry first.** Reducibility, fracture angle, patient anatomy (chest, jaw, kyphosis) — if any of these is unfavorable, posterior fusion is the better default.
5. **In elderly type II, do not chase radiographic union at any cost.** The endpoint is function, not film. A symptomatic patient who fails the conservative trial is a surgical candidate; an asymptomatic fibrous union may be the right answer.
6. **Counsel about head rotation loss with posterior fusion.** Patients regain most function but notice the rotation deficit; pre-operative discussion preempts post-operative dissatisfaction.
7. **Distinguish from [[spinal-cord-injury]].** Most odontoid fractures spare the cord; when cord injury is present, the management cascade is dominated by SCI principles (MAP 85–90, decompression timing, ASIA) rather than the fracture itself.
8. **Consider [[atlas-fractures]] simultaneously.** C1 + C2 ("Jefferson + odontoid") combinations are common and may require occipitocervical instrumentation rather than C1–C2 alone.
