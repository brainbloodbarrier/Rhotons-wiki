# Spinal Cord Injury

Acute traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI) is among the most consequential acute presentations in neurosurgery: the difference between a life of full mobility and one of permanent paralysis often hinges on decisions made in the first hours. Modern care has moved from the universal high-dose-steroid protocol of the 1990s to a nuanced, level- and severity-specific approach centered on prompt anatomic diagnosis, hemodynamic optimization, and early surgical decompression for compressive incomplete injuries. The clinical and infrastructural demands are substantial — a system that delivers true 24-hour decompression for cervical SCI is the principal lever on long-term outcome.

## Epidemiology

- US incidence ~ 12 000–17 000 new cases per year; prevalence 250 000–300 000.
- Bimodal age distribution: peak in young men (16–30 years) from motor-vehicle collisions, sports, and assault; second peak in adults > 65 from low-energy falls superimposed on degenerative cervical stenosis.
- Male predominance ~ 4 : 1 overall, narrowing in the elderly cohort.
- Cervical injuries dominate (~ 60 %), thoracic ~ 20 %, thoracolumbar / lumbar ~ 20 %.
- Concurrent traumatic brain injury (see [[traumatic-brain-injury]]) in 25–60 % of SCI patients — affects examination, decision-making, and outcome.

## Mechanisms and Pathophysiology

### Primary injury
- **Mechanical disruption** at the moment of trauma — contusion, laceration, distraction, compression by retropulsed bone or disc.
- Largely irreversible by the time the patient reaches care.

### Secondary injury
A cascade evolving over hours to days:
- **Vascular**: ischemia from hypotension, vasospasm, microvascular thrombosis.
- **Excitotoxicity**: glutamate release, NMDA-receptor activation, calcium-mediated neuronal injury.
- **Inflammatory**: cytokine cascade, neutrophil and microglial recruitment, blood-spinal-cord barrier disruption.
- **Apoptotic cell death** of oligodendrocytes leading to demyelination of surviving axons.
- **Edema** with secondary compression in a non-distensible canal.

The therapeutic window for influencing secondary injury is the principal target of acute medical and surgical interventions.

### Mechanical injury patterns
- **Cervical hyperflexion**: facet dislocation, anterior wedge fracture; cord injury from disc-osteophyte and ligamentum flavum compression.
- **Cervical hyperextension**: in older patients with degenerative stenosis, often produces a **central cord syndrome** without bony injury — an "SCIWORA-like" picture in adults (originally an entity of children but the principle of cord injury without obvious fracture applies).
- **Axial compression / burst fracture**: at C1 ([[atlas-fractures]]), the thoracolumbar junction, or anywhere a high vertical force lands on a column-loaded spine.
- **Penetrating injury**: gunshot, knife — mechanism of injury distinct from blunt trauma; surgical and antibiotic decisions differ.

## Initial Assessment

### Pre-hospital
- Spinal precautions during extrication and transport.
- Airway and breathing prioritized; high cervical injury (C3–C5) impairs diaphragmatic function.
- Establish IV access and start crystalloid resuscitation.

### Emergency department
- ATLS primary survey.
- Document baseline neurologic exam **before** intubation or sedation when feasible.
- **ASIA International Standards** — motor (10 key muscles per side, MRC 0–5), sensory (28 dermatomes per side, light touch and pin-prick 0–2), and rectal exam (deep anal pressure, voluntary anal contraction).
- **AIS grade**:
  - **A** = complete: no motor or sensory function in S4–S5.
  - **B** = sensory incomplete: sensory but no motor below the neurologic level, including S4–S5.
  - **C** = motor incomplete: motor function below the neurologic level, more than half of key muscles below have grade < 3.
  - **D** = motor incomplete: half or more of key muscles below the neurologic level have grade ≥ 3.
  - **E** = normal.

### Specific syndromes (incomplete)
- **Central cord syndrome**: upper-extremity > lower-extremity weakness; bladder dysfunction; classically older patient with hyperextension on degenerative stenosis (overlap with [[cervical-spondylotic-myelopathy]]). Best prognosis for ambulation among incomplete syndromes.
- **Brown-Séquard**: hemicord injury — ipsilateral motor and dorsal-column loss, contralateral spinothalamic loss. Best prognosis overall.
- **Anterior cord syndrome**: motor and spinothalamic loss with preserved dorsal columns; worst prognosis.
- **Posterior cord syndrome**: rare; isolated dorsal-column loss.
- **Conus medullaris syndrome** vs **[[cauda-equina-syndrome]]**: T12–L1 lesions with mixed UMN / LMN features vs purely LMN.

## Imaging

### CT (modality of choice for trauma triage)
- Whole cervical spine CT in any high-risk trauma; modern triage uses **NEXUS** or **Canadian C-spine rule** for low-risk patients.
- Defines fractures, listhesis, alignment, retropulsed fragments.
- Faster, more available, sensitive for bony injury.

### MRI (modality of choice for cord parenchyma)
- T2 cord signal change (intraspinal hemorrhage, edema, contusion length).
- Ligamentous injury (interspinous, posterior longitudinal, anterior longitudinal — STIR best).
- Disc-osteophyte compression.
- Detects ligamentous and discal injuries that CT misses; mandatory before surgical planning.
- **Intramedullary hemorrhage** on MRI predicts the worst outcomes; long contusion segments predict worse than short.

### Plain radiographs
- Largely supplanted by CT for the unstable trauma patient.
- Standing radiographs valuable for the elderly faller without neurologic deficit to assess dynamic alignment.

## Acute Medical Management

### Hemodynamics
- **MAP 85–90 mmHg for 5–7 days** is recommended (AANS / CNS class III evidence). The rationale: cord perfusion in the injured segment is autoregulation-dysregulated and pressure-passive; sustained higher MAP supports residual perfusion.
- Distinguish **neurogenic shock** (sympathetic chain disruption above T6, producing hypotension AND bradycardia, warm extremities) from **hemorrhagic shock** (tachycardia, cool extremities). Neurogenic shock often requires vasopressors (norepinephrine, phenylephrine) rather than only volume.
- Bradycardia from unopposed vagal tone is treated with atropine or temporary pacing in severe cases.

### Respiratory
- High cervical (C3–C5) injuries impair diaphragmatic function — anticipate respiratory failure and intubate early rather than late.
- Mid- to lower-cervical and high-thoracic injuries impair intercostal function and cough — pulmonary complications dominate the early ICU course.

### DVT prophylaxis
- Mechanical prophylaxis from admission.
- Pharmacologic prophylaxis (LMWH) initiated within 72 hours barring bleeding contraindication.
- Consider IVC filter only when both pharmacologic and mechanical prophylaxis are contraindicated AND the patient cannot be early-mobilized.

### Steroids — current status
- **High-dose methylprednisolone (NASCIS II / III)** is no longer recommended as standard of care.
- AO Spine (2017) and AANS / CNS (2013) classify it as an option with insufficient evidence of benefit and a real signal of harm (gastrointestinal hemorrhage, infection, hyperglycemia).
- The original NASCIS trials' methodology has been extensively critiqued; subsequent meta-analyses show no consistent neurologic benefit.
- In specific scenarios (early presentation, patient counseling preference), some centers still administer the protocol — but it is no longer the default.

### Riluzole
- **Late-phase trials (RISCIS)** — sodium-channel blocker; AO Spine 2017 conditionally recommended for acute cervical SCI in selected patients; full trial results inform incremental updates.

### Other agents in trial
- Minocycline, hypothermia (selective spinal cooling), Cethrin (Rho-kinase inhibitor), stem-cell therapy — investigational, not standard of care.

## Surgical Management

### Indications and timing
- **STASCIS (Fehlings 2012)** — early decompression (< 24 hours) for cervical SCI was associated with greater odds of two-grade AIS improvement at 6 months versus delayed decompression.
- AO Spine 2017 guidelines: **decompression within 24 hours** is recommended for **incomplete cervical SCI with imaging-confirmed compressive lesion**.
- **Complete (AIS A) injury** — surgical decompression has uncertain benefit on neurologic recovery but may improve rehabilitation potential and prevent late deformity; the threshold for surgery is generally still met.
- **Without compressive lesion** (e.g., contusion alone) — surgery is not therapeutic for the cord, but may be indicated for instability.

### Approach selection
- **Cervical anterior** (ACDF, corpectomy) — for ventral disc-osteophyte or retropulsed body fragments at one or more levels; preserves motion segments but limited reach for posterior compression.
- **Cervical posterior** (laminectomy ± fusion, laminoplasty) — for posterior compression, multilevel disease, instability requiring posterior tension band restoration.
- **Combined anterior + posterior** — for severe deformity, multilevel ventral and dorsal compression, or late instability.
- **Thoracolumbar** — posterior instrumentation and decompression dominate; anterior approaches reserved for select indications (corpectomy for retropulsed body, kyphosis correction).

### Reduction of dislocation
- **Closed reduction** of cervical facet dislocation with traction (Gardner-Wells tongs; sequential weight increase) is appropriate in awake patients with intact cooperation; pre-reduction MRI is debated but increasingly recommended.
- Failed closed reduction → open reduction and instrumented fusion.

### Stabilization
- Pedicle screws, lateral-mass screws, anterior plating — instrumentation choice driven by injury level, fracture pattern, and surgeon preference.
- Goals: anatomic alignment, durable fixation, early mobilization.

## Critical Care Considerations

### Autonomic dysreflexia
- **Cord injury at or above T6** carries lifelong risk of autonomic dysreflexia: a noxious stimulus below the lesion (bladder distension, fecal impaction, pressure ulcer, ingrown nail) triggers unopposed sympathetic outflow → severe hypertension, headache, sweating above the lesion, flushing, nasal congestion.
- Treatment: identify and remove the trigger (catheterize, disimpact); short-acting antihypertensive (nifedipine, nitrate) if pressure remains > 150 mmHg.
- Patient and caregiver education is non-negotiable.

### Pulmonary
- Atelectasis, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism dominate early ICU complications.
- Aggressive pulmonary toilet, kinetic therapy, early tracheostomy in high-cervical injuries.

### Genitourinary
- Indwelling catheter early; transition to clean intermittent catheterization once hemodynamically stable.
- Long-term plans require urology expertise — bladder management is a defining quality-of-life factor.

### Gastrointestinal
- Stress-ulcer prophylaxis.
- Bowel program initiated early.
- Watch for ileus and cholelithiasis.

### Skin
- Pressure ulcer prevention from arrival — a complication that can derail rehabilitation.

## Prognosis

- **AIS grade at 72 hours** post-injury is the most useful single predictor; the initial exam (often confounded by spinal shock and concurrent injury) is less reliable.
- **AIS A** with no recovery at 1 month → > 95 % remain AIS A.
- **AIS B–D** at 72 hours have substantial recovery potential, particularly central-cord and Brown-Séquard syndromes.
- Older age, longer cord-contusion segment on MRI, intramedullary hemorrhage, and concurrent TBI predict worse outcomes.

## Long-term Care

- Rehabilitation should begin as soon as medically stable; specialized SCI rehabilitation units improve outcomes.
- Lifelong follow-up — pressure care, bladder / bowel management, autonomic dysreflexia education, neurogenic pain syndromes, late deformity, syringomyelia surveillance.
- Mental health support and peer support — depression and substance-use disorders have high prevalence in SCI populations.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Document the exam before sedation.** A clean baseline ASIA examination is the single most useful piece of data in subsequent decisions.
2. **MAP 85–90 for 5–7 days.** Vasopressor-supported MAP augmentation is feasible in any modern ICU; do not let goal MAP drift down because of nursing convenience.
3. **Distinguish neurogenic from hemorrhagic shock.** Volume alone fails in neurogenic shock; recognize the slow / warm hypotensive patient and use vasopressors early.
4. **Decompress within 24 hours when there is a compressive lesion.** STASCIS is the strongest evidence; the operating room should be available, not deferred to "morning list."
5. **Closed reduction of cervical facet dislocation in the awake patient is fast and effective.** Do not let MRI dependence delay reduction in a cooperative, examinable patient.
6. **Treat steroids as an option, not a reflex.** NASCIS protocols are not standard of care; if used, the indication and the patient's understanding of evidence should be documented.
7. **Anticipate autonomic dysreflexia from injury day one.** Education starts in the ICU; the family needs to recognize headache + flushed face + hypertension before it produces an intracranial hemorrhage at home.
8. **Coordinate with rehabilitation early.** The acute team's job is not done at hospital discharge — handoff to specialized SCI rehabilitation determines long-term function.
