# Hangman's Fracture

Hangman's fracture — formally **traumatic spondylolisthesis of the axis** — is a bilateral fracture through the pars interarticularis of C2, the second most common upper-cervical fracture after [[odontoid-fractures]]. The eponym derives from the lesion produced by judicial hanging with a submental knot, where the combined hyperextension and axial distraction snap the C2 pars bilaterally. In modern trauma populations the same fracture pattern arises from motor-vehicle collisions, diving accidents, and high-velocity sports; the clinical and surgical considerations are dominated by classification-driven decisions rather than the historical mechanism.

## Anatomy

The **pars interarticularis** of C2 is the narrow bony bridge between the superior and inferior articular processes — the structural connection between the C2 vertebral body / odontoid complex anteriorly and the C2 lamina / spinous process posteriorly. Bilateral pars fractures functionally **disconnect the C2 body from the C2 posterior arch**, producing a "floating" C2 body that translates anteriorly on C3 with disruption of the C2-C3 disc and posterior longitudinal ligament in unstable variants.

- **C2 vertebral artery** runs through the C2 transverse foramen; pars fractures with significant displacement can injure the artery.
- **Spinal canal at C2** is wide; the bilateral pars fracture decompresses rather than narrows the canal — explaining the relatively low rate of neurologic injury despite the dramatic-looking lesion.
- **C2-C3 disc** is the critical ligamentous structure for stability; type II and IIA fractures are defined by its disruption.

## Mechanism

- **Hyperextension + axial loading** (motor vehicle dashboard impact, fall onto chin) — the dominant modern mechanism.
- **Hyperextension + distraction** (judicial hanging) — the historical mechanism; produces type IIA pattern.
- **Hyperflexion** with secondary extension — less common; can produce type III with bilateral facet dislocation.

## Classification (Effendi-Levine-Edwards)

| Type | Pattern | Stability | Standard treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Nondisplaced, < 3 mm translation, < 11° angulation | Stable | Rigid cervical collar 6–12 weeks |
| **II** | Translation > 3 mm or angulation > 11°; C2-C3 disc disrupted | Unstable | Halo vest 12 weeks (with traction reduction first); surgical fixation if irreducible or fails |
| **IIA** | Severe angulation, minimal translation; disc disrupted but PLL intact | Unstable; **paradoxical response to traction** | Halo vest **without** traction; if traction is applied, fracture displaces further |
| **III** | Bilateral C2-C3 facet dislocation + bilateral pars fractures | Highly unstable | Open reduction + posterior C2-C3 fusion |

The **type IIA pitfall** is the single most clinically important subtlety: applying axial traction to a IIA fracture **worsens** the deformity by exploiting the intact PLL as a hinge. Recognition of the predominantly-angulated pattern on lateral radiograph or CT before initiating treatment is mandatory.

## Clinical Presentation

- High cervical pain, often with associated occipital or retro-orbital headache.
- Restricted neck motion; patient often supports the head with hands (similar to other upper-cervical injuries).
- **Neurologic deficit is uncommon** (~ 6–10 % overall, lower than in subaxial injuries), reflecting canal decompression by the bilateral pars fracture.
- Severe disability or death in the rare patient with associated cord injury or vertebral artery dissection.
- Concurrent injuries: closed head injury, facial trauma, [[atlas-fractures]], [[odontoid-fractures]] — image the entire cervical spine and head.

## Imaging

### CT (modality of choice)
- Whole cervical spine; sagittal and coronal multiplanar reconstructions.
- Defines fracture lines, displacement, angulation; distinguishes types I / II / IIA / III.
- Vertebral artery foramen involvement → consider CT angiography.

### MRI
- Indicated when classification is unclear, when neurologic deficit is present, or before surgery.
- Defines disc disruption (key for type II vs IIA distinction), PLL integrity, ligamentous injury, cord parenchymal change.
- Associated injuries — alar / apical ligaments, cruciate complex.

### Plain radiographs
- Lateral cervical view shows the classic spondylolisthesis of C2 on C3 with bilateral pars defects.
- Open-mouth view assesses concurrent C1 and dens injury.
- Modern triage favors CT for the unstable patient.

### Vertebral artery imaging
- CT angiography or MRA in displaced fractures or with cranial-nerve / brainstem signs; vertebral artery injury rate 5–15 % in displaced hangman's fractures.

## Management

### Type I
- **Rigid cervical collar** for 6–12 weeks.
- Repeat radiographs at 2–4 weeks and end-of-treatment to confirm union and stable alignment.
- Healing rate > 90 %.

### Type II
- **Initial reduction with halo traction** (5–10 lb), followed by **halo vest immobilization** for 12 weeks.
- Reduction is monitored radiographically; persistent translation > 5 mm despite reduction is a relative indication for surgery.
- Surgical fixation (anterior C2-C3 plating with cage, or posterior C2 pedicle / pars screws) for irreducible fractures, failed nonoperative trial, or anatomically unfavorable patients (elderly, osteoporotic, cardiopulmonary contraindication to halo).
- Healing rate with halo > 80 %; surgical fixation > 95 % fusion rate.

### Type IIA — the critical exception
- **No traction.** Traction worsens angulation by exploiting the intact PLL.
- **Halo vest without traction**, applying gentle extension to reduce angulation.
- 12 weeks of immobilization.
- Failure of reduction or progression → surgical fixation.

### Type III
- **Surgical management is required.** Open reduction of the bilateral C2-C3 facet dislocation followed by posterior C2-C3 fusion (Magerl-style transarticular or modern C2-C3 pedicle / pars screws).
- Often associated with cord injury; ASIA documentation and SCI-protocol management apply (see [[spinal-cord-injury]]).
- Halo alone is insufficient — the facet dislocation does not reduce predictably with closed methods.

## Surgical Approaches

### Anterior C2-C3 plate and cage
- For type II requiring fixation; an anterior cervical approach with discectomy / corpectomy and plating across C2-C3.
- Advantages: direct disc removal, single-level fixation, preserves more upper-cervical motion.
- Disadvantages: technical difficulty in the high-anterior-cervical zone, dysphagia, need for pre-pharyngeal mobilization.

### Posterior C2 pedicle / pars screw fixation
- Bilateral C2 pedicle (or pars) screws — increasingly the modern default for type II / III.
- Allows direct fracture reduction and stable fixation.
- Vertebral artery anatomy (anomalous V3) may preclude pedicle screw on one or both sides; preoperative CTA mandatory.

### Posterior C1-C2-C3 fusion
- Reserved for severe instability with concurrent C1 injury, anomalous vertebral artery anatomy precluding C2 pedicle fixation, or revision surgery.
- Sacrifices C1-C2 rotation in addition to C2-C3 motion.

## Complications

- **Nonunion** in type II: 5–15 % with halo alone; lower with surgical fixation.
- **Vertebral artery injury** (5–15 %): consider CTA in displaced fractures; manage according to grade (medical antithrombotic therapy in most; endovascular if active extravasation or symptomatic dissection).
- **Halo-related morbidity** in elderly: pin-site infection, swallowing dysfunction, pulmonary complications, falls.
- **Iatrogenic worsening of type IIA** by misapplied traction — the cardinal preventable complication.
- **Adjacent-segment disease** late after fusion.

## Special Populations

### Elderly
- Halo vest morbidity high; surgical fixation often preferred for type II despite higher upfront cost.
- One-year mortality elevated similar to other upper-cervical injuries in elderly.

### Pediatric
- Synchondrosis of C2 persists into adolescence; fracture distinction from synchondrosis requires age-appropriate interpretation.
- Most pediatric pars fractures are stable type I and heal with rigid collar.

### Combined upper-cervical injuries
- Concurrent [[atlas-fractures]] or [[odontoid-fractures]] are common (15–25 % concurrence in some series).
- Combined injuries often require occipitocervical or extended posterior fusion rather than C2-C3 alone.
- The classic "Jefferson + Hangman" combination is unstable and surgical from the outset.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Recognize type IIA before applying traction.** Predominantly angulated pars fracture with intact PLL on imaging — apply gentle extension in halo, not traction. Misapplied traction in type IIA is a recurring teaching-set complication.
2. **Image the vertebral artery in any displaced fracture.** CTA is fast, low-risk, and changes management when injury is found.
3. **Concurrent C1 / odontoid injuries change the operation.** A C1-C2 + Hangman combination is occipitocervical or C0-C2-C3, not C2-C3 alone.
4. **Surgical fixation in elderly type II is reasonable.** The morbidity of halo in older patients often exceeds the operative cost; modern posterior pedicle-screw fixation is well-tolerated.
5. **Type III is operative.** Bilateral facet dislocation does not reliably reduce with closed methods; commit to OR early.
6. **Counsel about head rotation loss with C2-C3 fusion.** Less than after C1-C2 fusion (which sacrifices ~ 50 % of head rotation), but patients note the upper-cervical stiffness.
7. **Document the neurologic exam.** Although deficit is uncommon, when it occurs it dominates the management; ASIA examination at presentation and post-treatment is the standard.
8. **Distinguish from [[odontoid-fractures]] geometrically.** Both are C2 injuries but the structural lesion (pars vs dens) drives a completely different operative plan.
