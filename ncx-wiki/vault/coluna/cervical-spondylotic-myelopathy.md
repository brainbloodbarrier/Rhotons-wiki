# Cervical Spondylotic Myelopathy

Cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM), part of the broader degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) spectrum, is the most common cause of non-traumatic spinal cord dysfunction in adults beyond the fifth decade. It results from the cumulative effect of age-related degenerative changes that narrow the cervical spinal canal and chronically compress the cord. The clinical course is typically insidious; many patients are diagnosed only after years of subtle gait and dexterity decline, by which time irreversible cord injury has occurred.

## Epidemiology

- Prevalence rises sharply after age 55; in selected MRI screening cohorts more than half of asymptomatic adults > 60 show spondylotic compression, though only a fraction develop overt myelopathy.
- Male predominance (≈ 2.7 : 1 in surgical series).
- Higher risk in occupations and ethnicities with elevated OPLL prevalence (East Asian populations).
- Major source of disability, falls, and loss of independence in the elderly.

## Pathophysiology

CSM is the end-state of a **degenerative cascade** that operates through both **static** and **dynamic** mechanisms.

### Static compression
- **Disc-osteophyte complex.** Loss of disc height triggers reactive osteophytosis at the vertebral end-plates and uncovertebral joints. Ventral spurs encroach on the canal and intervertebral foramina.
- **Ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and buckling.** Loss of disc height shortens the segmental column; the posterior ligamentum buckles into the canal during extension. Chronic micro-trauma drives fibrosis and calcification.
- **Posterior longitudinal ligament hypertrophy / OPLL.** Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (continuous, segmental, mixed, or localized) is an independent cause of canal stenosis, especially in East Asian populations.
- **Congenital narrow canal.** Pavlov ratio < 0.8 (sagittal canal diameter / vertebral body diameter on lateral radiograph) marks the substrate on which any acquired compression becomes symptomatic earlier.

### Dynamic compression
- **Flexion** stretches the cord over ventral spurs.
- **Extension** narrows the canal further as the ligamentum flavum buckles posteriorly and the disc-osteophyte complex bulges anteriorly.
- **Cervical kyphosis** creates a permanent draping of the cord over ventral pathology, increasing tension and reducing perfusion.
- **Subluxation / segmental instability** delivers repetitive shear injury.

### Cord injury mechanisms
- Direct mechanical compression of grey matter and the lateral corticospinal tracts.
- Ischemia from compromise of anterior spinal artery branches and intramedullary microvasculature.
- Repeated dynamic injury → demyelination, gliosis, neuronal loss → confluent T2 hyperintensity (myelomalacia) on MRI.
- Early changes are reversible; chronic myelomalacia with cavitation is not.

## Clinical Presentation

The presentation is heterogeneous and frequently mistaken for normal aging or peripheral neuropathy.

### Symptoms
- **Gait disturbance.** Wide-based, slow, "spastic-ataxic" gait. Patients describe imbalance and frequent stumbling. Often the first complaint.
- **Hand clumsiness.** Loss of fine motor control — buttoning shirts, writing, picking up coins. The "myelopathy hand" — wasting of intrinsics with weakness in finger abduction.
- **Numbness and paresthesias** in hands and arms; less commonly trunk and legs.
- **Lhermitte phenomenon.** Electric-shock sensation down the spine on neck flexion; reflects dorsal column irritation.
- **Bladder urgency and frequency.** Late finding; frank incontinence is rare and warrants exclusion of other pathology.
- **Neck pain and stiffness** are inconsistent — many patients have minimal axial pain.
- **Radicular pain** from coexistent foraminal stenosis is frequent and often dominates the early picture.

### Signs (upper-motor-neuron pattern below the level of compression)
- **Hyperreflexia** at lower extremity reflexes; biceps and triceps may be hyperreflexic if the lesion is above C5–C6.
- **Hoffmann sign.** Reflex finger flexion on flicking the distal phalanx of the middle finger.
- **Inverted radial reflex.** Tapping the brachioradialis tendon elicits finger flexion instead of forearm pronation; localizes compression to C5–C6.
- **Clonus** at the ankle.
- **Babinski sign** (extensor plantar response).
- **Spastic gait;** Romberg may be positive from dorsal column involvement.
- **Lower-motor-neuron signs at the level** of the lesion — wasting, hyporeflexia in the involved root distribution — coexist with UMN signs below it, the classic "mixed picture."

## Imaging

### MRI (study of choice)
- T2-weighted sagittal and axial sequences quantify canal narrowing, cord deformation, and intramedullary signal change.
- **T2 hyperintensity** within the cord (myelomalacia) is associated with worse baseline severity and less complete recovery.
- **T1 hypointensity** in the same region indicates established gliosis and predicts incomplete reversibility.
- Diffusion tensor imaging and magnetization transfer ratio are emerging as quantitative biomarkers but are not yet routine.

### Plain radiographs
- Lateral upright views detect **kyphosis, listhesis, and dynamic instability** (flexion-extension series).
- **Pavlov ratio** screens for congenital narrowing.
- Disc-space narrowing, anterior osteophytes, and uncovertebral spurring on AP view are typical.

### CT
- Best for OPLL morphology, calcified disc-osteophyte complex, and bony canal dimensions.
- Mandatory before posterior approaches in OPLL to plan whether the posterior ligament must also be addressed.

### Adjuncts
- **Electrodiagnostics** (NCS / EMG) help separate myelopathy from peripheral neuropathy or motor neuron disease, particularly when the picture is mixed.
- **Somatosensory and motor evoked potentials** are useful intraoperatively and in selected ambiguous cases.

## Severity Classification

### Nurick grade (1972)
| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 0 | Root signs / symptoms; no cord involvement |
| 1 | Cord signs; gait normal |
| 2 | Mild gait abnormality; employed |
| 3 | Gait abnormality limits employment |
| 4 | Walks only with assistance |
| 5 | Wheelchair / bedridden |

### Modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score
Eighteen-point scale spanning four domains (upper extremity motor, lower extremity motor, sensory, sphincter). Lower scores = worse function.

| Severity | mJOA |
|----------|------|
| **Mild** | 15–17 |
| **Moderate** | 12–14 |
| **Severe** | < 12 |

The mJOA is the dominant outcome metric in modern trials and the basis for the AOSpine 2017 treatment thresholds.

### AOSpine staging
A more recent ordinal scheme combining mJOA, neurologic exam, MRI signal change, and radiographic compression grade; stratifies surgical urgency and predicts response.

## Differential Diagnosis

- **Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis** — pure motor, fasciculations, no sensory, no sphincter; bulbar features.
- **Multiple sclerosis** — multifocal, optic neuritis, brainstem signs, oligoclonal bands.
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency / subacute combined degeneration** — dorsal columns + corticospinal tracts; macrocytosis; check methylmalonic acid.
- **Normal-pressure hydrocephalus** — gait + cognition + continence; brain MRI clarifies.
- **Tropical spastic paraparesis (HTLV-1)** — chronic progressive paraparesis in endemic populations.
- **Infectious / inflammatory transverse myelitis** — subacute, often with sensory level.
- **Intramedullary tumor or syrinx** — MRI distinguishes; consider in atypical age or rapid progression. See [[chiari-malformation]] for craniocervical-junction differentials that may mimic or coexist.
- **Cervical traumatic injury** — acute, often with trauma history; see [[atlas-fractures]] for the classical upper-cervical bony pattern.

## Natural History

- A subset remains stable for years; another stepwise-deteriorates with discrete worsening events; a smaller group shows steady progression.
- Predictors of progression include severity at presentation, T2 signal change, and male sex.
- Once moderate-to-severe deficit is established, spontaneous improvement is uncommon.
- Asymptomatic radiographic compression has a low but real annual conversion rate to symptomatic myelopathy; surveillance with reassessment every 6–12 months is reasonable.

## Treatment

### Non-operative
- Reasonable for **mild** disease (mJOA 15–17) without evidence of progression and with shared decision-making.
- Includes activity modification (avoiding contact sports and high-risk falls), physiotherapy aimed at posture and balance, and treatment of comorbidities.
- Cervical collars, traction, and manipulation lack convincing evidence and may be harmful.
- Strict surveillance: re-examination and repeat mJOA every 6 months, with low threshold to convert to surgery on progression.

### Surgical indications (AOSpine 2017)
- **Recommend** surgical decompression for moderate or severe disease (mJOA ≤ 14).
- **Offer** surgery to mild disease (mJOA 15–17) with progression or impairment of quality of life; non-operative trial is a reasonable alternative.
- Asymptomatic compression with electrophysiologic evidence of dysfunction or clinical evidence of radiculopathy is a relative indication.

### Surgical approaches

| Approach | Best for | Trade-offs |
|----------|----------|------------|
| **ACDF** (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion) | One- to three-level disc-osteophyte ventral compression with neutral or lordotic alignment | Direct removal of compressive lesion; restores lordosis; pseudarthrosis risk rises with multilevel constructs |
| **Anterior cervical corpectomy and fusion (ACCF)** | Retrovertebral compression spanning a body (e.g., OPLL behind a vertebral body) | More extensive ventral decompression; higher graft-related complications; loss of one extra motion segment |
| **Laminoplasty** | Multilevel (≥ 3) compression with preserved lordosis; favored in OPLL | Preserves motion; lower fusion-related morbidity; depends on cord drifting backward, which fails in kyphosis |
| **Laminectomy + fusion** | Multilevel compression with kyphosis, instability, or facet arthropathy | Decompression plus alignment correction; loss of motion; adjacent-segment disease over time |
| **Combined anterior + posterior** | Severe multilevel disease with kyphosis and ventral plus dorsal compression | Most powerful reconstruction; highest morbidity; reserved for severe deformity or salvage |

The choice is governed by **(1) levels involved, (2) sagittal alignment, (3) primary site of compression (ventral, dorsal, or both), (4) presence of OPLL, (5) facet integrity and instability,** and **(6) patient factors** (age, comorbidity, bone quality).

### Outcomes
- AOSpine cohorts show mean mJOA improvement of 2.5–3 points and significant Nurick grade improvement at 12–24 months across approaches.
- Predictors of poorer recovery: longer symptom duration (especially > 12 months), severe baseline deficit, kyphosis, T1 hypointensity, multifocal T2 signal change, advanced age, smoking, diabetes.
- Overall complication rate of surgery is ≈ 15 %; serious complications (C5 palsy, dysphagia, hardware failure, pseudarthrosis, CSF leak) occur but most are manageable.

### C5 palsy
- Postoperative deltoid / biceps weakness occurring within hours to days of decompression at any level (most common after multilevel posterior decompression).
- Probably reflects nerve-root tethering as the cord drifts dorsally; usually self-limited, recovers over weeks to months.
- Not an indication for re-operation in isolation.

## Complications and pitfalls
- **Inadequate decompression** — failure to address the principal compressive element; relevant in choosing anterior vs posterior in mixed disease.
- **Loss of lordosis after laminoplasty / laminectomy** in patients with marginal preoperative alignment; combined or anterior approach may be preferable.
- **Adjacent-segment disease** at fused levels, especially after long anterior constructs; total disc replacement is an option in selected single-level cases without instability.
- **Pseudarthrosis** in multilevel ACDF — increases with the number of levels and with smoking; rigid plating and graft choice (allograft vs autograft vs cage with BMP-considered judiciously) modify the risk.
- **Dural injury** is more frequent in OPLL and in revision surgery; careful preoperative CT planning is mandatory.

## Surveillance and follow-up
- Postoperative MRI at 3 and 12 months in symptomatic patients to confirm decompression and detect recurrent stenosis.
- Standing radiographs at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months for fusion assessment and alignment.
- mJOA at 6 and 12 months as the principal functional metric.
- Patients with persistent T2 signal change at 12 months warrant continued surveillance for late deterioration.

## Key Points for the Surgical Decision

1. **Do not under-treat moderate-to-severe disease.** The strongest predictor of poor recovery is delay; once the cord has been chronically compressed, the window of reversibility narrows.
2. **Match the approach to the pattern of compression and to alignment**, not to surgeon preference. Mixed disease and kyphosis demand more than a single-modality solution.
3. **Counsel realistically.** The goal of surgery is to halt progression and improve function modestly; complete reversal of established myelopathy is uncommon.
4. **Address comorbidities preoperatively** — smoking cessation, glycemic control, and bone-health optimization measurably reduce pseudarthrosis and complication rates.
5. **Recognize the spectrum.** Spinal cord injury from CSM is a chronic, dynamic disease; ongoing surveillance after surgery — and during conservative management — is part of the standard of care, not optional. A working understanding of [[dura]] anatomy and craniocervical-junction physiology informs surgical planning.
