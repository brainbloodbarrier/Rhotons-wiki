---Operative Techniques and Instrumentation

The introduction of the operating microscope converted nearly all of neurosurgery into a microsurgical specialty. Rhoton's systematic approach to instrumentation and technique forms the foundation upon which all [[surgical-approaches-overview|surgical approaches]] are built.

## Core Principles

### The Operating Microscope
The microscope provides magnified stereoscopic vision and intense illumination, enabling:
- Neural and vascular structures to be delineated with greater visual accuracy
- Deep areas to be reached with less brain retraction and smaller cortical incisions
- Bleeding points to be coagulated with less damage to adjacent structures
- Nerves distorted by tumor to be preserved with greater frequency
- Anastomosis of small vessels and nerves previously impossible

### Pencil Grip Over Pistol Grip
Instruments should be held in a **pencil grip** between the thumb and index finger — not a pistol grip with the whole hand. The pencil grip permits delicate finger movements to position the instrument tip, while the hand rests on the wound margin for stability. The pistol grip requires coarser wrist, elbow, and shoulder movements.

### Round-Handle Instruments
Round-handle forceps, scissors, and needle-holders allow rotation between thumb and forefinger, avoiding the need to rotate the entire wrist. First developed for [[superficial-temporal-artery]]-[[middle-cerebral-artery]] bypass, this design improves accuracy across all microsurgical tasks.

### Macrosurgery-Microsurgery Continuum
Rather than separate instrument sets for macro and micro work, select instruments with handles and tactile characteristics suitable for both — changing only the tip size:
- **Macrosurgical forceps**: grasping tips 2-3 mm
- **Microsurgical forceps**: tips 0.3-1.0 mm
- **Microsurgical tissue forceps**: tip separation ≤8 mm
- **Microneedle-holders**: tips open ≤3 mm
- **Microscissors**: tips open 2-5 mm

### Dull Finish
Instruments must have a dull finish — brilliant light reflected from polished surfaces through the microscope interferes with vision and photograph quality.

## Patient Positioning

| Position | Indication | Key details |
|---|---|---|
| Supine | Frontal, temporal, anterior parietal, most [[cranial-base|cranial base]] approaches | Head turning + shoulder elevation to rotate torso |
| Three-quarter prone (park-bench) | Posterior parietal, occipital, [[foramen-magnum|suboccipital]] | Table tilted to elevate head; reduces venous engorgement |
| Semi-sitting | [[posterior-fossa|Posterior fossa]], cervical (selected surgeons) | Best venous drainage; air embolism risk requires Doppler + right atrial catheter |
| Prone | Midline posterior approaches | Surgeon seated at head |

**Positioning principles:**
- Avoid extreme head/neck rotation that obstructs venous drainage
- Sagittal suture parallel to floor (for CP angle work) broadens operative access
- Gentle head flexion opens the angle between upper chest and operative site
- Tilting the table to elevate the head reduces venous distension significantly
- Elastic/pneumatic stockings prevent DVT for all cases

## Head Fixation

The pinion headholder with three pins provides rigid fixation while allowing intraoperative repositioning:

**Pin placement rules:**
- Avoid CSF shunts
- Avoid thin bone (frontal sinus, mastoid air cells)
- Avoid thick temporalis muscle (unstable grip)
- Position away from incision and [[orbit|orbit]]
- Use shorter pediatric pins for thin crania
- Use radiolucent headholders when intraoperative fluoroscopy is planned

The headholder serves as the attachment point for brain retractor systems. Three sunburst sites (rather than one) provide flexibility for retractor and image guidance attachment.

## Key Instrument Categories

### Bayonet Forceps
The standard neurosurgical instrument. Must be properly balanced — when resting on the thumb-index web, the instrument should not fall forward when released. Available in three lengths for superficial, deep, and extra-deep sites. Test tension in the gloved hand (resistance perceived as adequate bare-handed becomes imperceptible gloved).

### Bipolar Coagulation
Routine for bleeding control from scalp, dura, and intracranial sites. Around sensitive structures ([[facial-nerve]], [[optic-nerve]]), substitute hemostatic gelatin sponge (Gelfoam) rather than even gentle bipolar coagulation.

### Suction Tubes
Held in pencil grip. Function as both aspiration and second-hand dissection tools.

### Brain Retractors
Tapered spatulas attached to self-retaining systems mounted on the pinion headholder. The system must allow repositioning without losing the exposure.

### High-Speed Drills
Replaced manual trephines and Gigli saws for craniotomy. Cutting burrs with foot plates protect the dura. Key principles:
- Avoid extremely long bone cuts, especially across bony prominences ([[sphenoid-bone|pterion]]) or [[dural-venous-sinuses|venous sinuses]]
- Place holes on each side of a sinus, strip dura, then cut — never cut across a sinus in a long pass
- Bone wax controls bone edge bleeding

### Cup Forceps and Bone Curettes
For tumor removal and bone reshaping within the skull base.

## Craniotomy Principles

1. Scalp flaps: broad base, adequate blood supply — pedicle never narrower than flap width
2. Preserve the [[superficial-temporal-artery]] if EC-IC bypass is possible
3. Avoid the [[facial-nerve]] frontal branch: incisions reaching the zygoma >1.5 cm anterior to the ear risk this nerve
4. **Temporalis muscle preservation**: use periosteal elevator (not cutting Bovie) for subperiosteal dissection — neurovascular supply courses in periosteal attachments
5. Free bone flap with plates and screws (not silk sutures, which allow inward settling)
6. Avoid metallic clips near imaging zones (use nonmagnetic alloys or titanium if needed)

## Scalp Landmarks

Before draping for supratentorial procedures, mark:
- Coronal, sagittal, and lambdoid sutures
- Rolandic and [[sylvian-fissure|sylvian fissures]]
- Pterion, inion, asterion, and keyhole
