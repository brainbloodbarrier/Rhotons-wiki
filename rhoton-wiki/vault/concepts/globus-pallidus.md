# Globus Pallidus

The globus pallidus (GP) is the medial component of the lentiform nucleus and the principal output station of the basal ganglia motor circuit. Despite the visual gestalt of a single triangular gray-matter mass on coronal section, the GP is functionally and surgically two distinct nuclei separated by the medial medullary lamina: an external segment (GPe) embedded in the cortico-striato-pallido-thalamic indirect pathway, and an internal segment (GPi) whose tonic GABAergic firing inhibits motor thalamus. Almost every clinically important property of the pallidum — its role in Parkinson's disease, in dystonia, in the modern DBS algorithm — is rooted in this two-segment architecture.

## Location and Boundaries

The GP occupies the medial portion of the lentiform nucleus, deep to the insula and lateral to the internal capsule.

- **Lateral**: the putamen, separated from the GP by the **lateral medullary lamina**. The putamen and GP together form the lentiform nucleus.
- **Medial**: the posterior limb of the [[internal-capsule]] separates the GPi from the [[thalamus]]. The corticospinal fibers descend through the posterior limb adjacent to the GPi; current spread medially during DBS produces tonic muscle contraction on contralateral limbs at low amplitudes.
- **Inferior**: the **optic tract** runs inferolateral to the GPi as it sweeps from the chiasm to the lateral geniculate; stimulation that captures the optic tract produces phosphenes — a useful intraoperative landmark and a side-effect indicator.
- **Superior**: the corona radiata and the body of the [[caudate-nucleus]] (head and body of caudate are superomedial across the internal capsule).
- **Anterior**: the GP tapers anteriorly toward the anterior commissure region.
- **Posterior**: the GPi extends posteriorly toward the upper substantia innominata; the [[substantia-nigra]] lies inferior and posterior in the rostral [[midbrain]].

The GPi is divided by an **accessory medullary lamina** into anterior (external) and posterior (internal) parts; the **postero-ventro-lateral GPi** is the canonical sensorimotor zone targeted by DBS.

## Internal Architecture

### External segment (GPe)
- Larger, more lateral, lower iron content (slightly less hypointense on T2 / SWI).
- Tonically active GABAergic neurons firing 50–80 Hz at baseline.
- Receives:
  - **Striatum** (caudate, putamen) — the canonical indirect-pathway input, GABAergic.
  - **Subthalamic nucleus** ([[subthalamic-nucleus]]) — glutamatergic feedback.
- Projects to:
  - STN — closing the indirect-pathway loop.
  - GPi — providing direct disinhibitory regulation of the output nucleus.
  - Thalamic reticular nucleus — modulating thalamic output.

### Internal segment (GPi)
- Smaller, more medial, higher iron content (more hypointense on T2 / SWI).
- Tonically active GABAergic neurons firing 60–90 Hz at baseline.
- Receives:
  - Striatum (direct pathway input) — GABAergic.
  - GPe — GABAergic.
  - STN — glutamatergic (the only excitatory drive on the output nucleus).
  - Substantia nigra pars compacta — dopaminergic modulation.
- Projects to:
  - Ventral anterior (VA) and ventral lateral (VL) thalamic nuclei — GABAergic. These thalamic nuclei project excitatorily to primary motor cortex and supplementary motor area; the GPi tonically restrains motor thalamus, and reduction of GPi output (during voluntary movement) disinhibits thalamocortical drive.
  - Centromedian / parafascicular thalamic nuclei — modulating thalamostriatal feedback.
  - Pedunculopontine nucleus — gait and arousal modulation.
  - Lateral habenula (small projection).

## Connections in the Direct / Indirect / Hyperdirect Framework

- **Direct pathway**: cortex → putamen → GPi (inhibitory) → motor thalamus → cortex (excitatory). Net effect: facilitation of voluntary movement.
- **Indirect pathway**: cortex → putamen → GPe (inhibitory) → STN (disinhibitory glutamatergic drive) → GPi → motor thalamus. Net effect: suppression of competing or unwanted movement.
- **Hyperdirect pathway**: cortex → STN → GPi → motor thalamus, bypassing the striatum. Mediates rapid stop-signal control.

In Parkinson's disease, dopamine depletion biases the striatum toward indirect-pathway dominance, GPi hyperactivity, and excessive thalamic inhibition — producing bradykinesia and rigidity. In dystonia, GPi output is paradoxically lower than normal but **maladaptively patterned**, producing co-contractions; high-frequency GPi DBS desynchronizes this pattern and reduces pathologic movement.

## Vascular Supply

Predominantly the **lenticulostriate arteries**:
- **Lateral lenticulostriates**: branches of the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery — supply most of the lateral GP and putamen.
- **Medial lenticulostriates**: branches of the A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery — supply the medial GP and the head of the caudate.
- **Anterior choroidal artery**: supplies the medial GPi, the posterior limb of the internal capsule, and adjacent lateral geniculate / hippocampus.

The GP is therefore vulnerable to lacunar infarcts in the lenticulostriate territory and to anterior choroidal infarcts (the AChA syndrome combines contralateral hemiparesis from internal-capsule injury, hemisensory loss, and homonymous hemianopia).

## Imaging

### MRI
- **T2 / T2\***: GP is hypointense relative to surrounding white matter due to high iron content; GPi is more hypointense than GPe.
- **SWI / QSM**: striking iron-related hypointensity provides reliable direct visualization of the GP segments and the medial medullary lamina at 3 T and especially 7 T.
- **T1**: relatively isointense to gray matter.
- **Tractography**: pallido-thalamic and pallido-pedunculopontine bundles can be reconstructed and increasingly inform DBS lead orientation in advanced centers.

### Targeting (indirect, AC-PC reference for postero-ventro-lateral GPi)
- **Lateral**: 19–22 mm from midline.
- **Anterior**: 2–3 mm anterior to mid-commissural point.
- **Inferior**: 4–6 mm below the AC-PC line.

These coordinates are starting points; modern DBS practice combines direct visualization (T2 / SWI / QSM) with intraoperative microelectrode mapping.

## Surgical Relevance

### Deep brain stimulation

#### Generalized and segmental dystonia (Vidailhet 2005)
The **postero-ventro-lateral GPi** is the standard-of-care DBS target for primary generalized and segmental dystonia, with substantial improvement in disability and pain scores at 6 and 36 months in randomized data. Response is delayed: motor benefit accrues over weeks to months, not hours, distinguishing it from STN-DBS in Parkinson's disease (where benefit is often immediate).

#### Parkinson's disease
GPi-DBS is an alternative to [[subthalamic-nucleus]] (STN) targeting, with comparable motor benefit and:
- **Less levodopa reduction** post-operatively (~ 10–30 % vs 30–60 % for STN).
- **Better cognitive and mood profile** — favored in patients with mild cognitive decline, depression, or impulsivity vulnerability.
- **More direct dyskinesia suppression** — particularly useful when peak-dose dyskinesias dominate.
- **Higher battery drain** — pulse-width and amplitude requirements typically exceed STN.

The COMPARE and CSP-468 trials inform shared decision-making between STN and GPi targeting; modern practice individualizes the choice rather than adopting a single default.

#### Tourette syndrome and OCD
Refractory severe Tourette syndrome and OCD are emerging GPi indications (anteromedial / limbic GPi), though the evidence base remains less mature than for movement disorders.

### Pallidotomy
Stereotactic radiofrequency lesioning of the GPi (Laitinen-style postero-ventral pallidotomy) was the precursor to modern DBS and remains a valid option for unilateral disease in patients who cannot tolerate hardware (poor wound healing, bleeding diathesis, geographic constraints on follow-up). MRI-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) pallidotomy is an emerging non-invasive alternative.

### Trajectory considerations
- **Entry**: pre-coronal, paramedian, with care to avoid sulci and visible cortical veins; typical lateral entry 25–35 mm from midline.
- **Trajectory**: angled in the sagittal plane to traverse the postero-ventro-lateral GPi for 4–6 mm of nucleus.
- **Microelectrode recording**: characteristic GPe firing is irregular, with high-frequency pauses ("pausers" and "bursters"); GPi shows tonic high-frequency firing without pauses ("tonic units"); below the GPi, optic tract crossings produce visual-evoked responses to light flashes.
- **Macrostimulation testing**:
  - **Phosphenes** at low amplitude — current spread to optic tract; lead too inferior or too lateral.
  - **Tonic muscle contraction** at low amplitude — internal-capsule activation; lead too medial or too posterior.
  - **Therapeutic window** of at least ~ 1.5–2 V between desired effect and side effects defines an acceptable contact.

## Functional Implications of Lesions

- **Pallidal lesions** (e.g., carbon-monoxide poisoning, cyanide, bilateral basal-ganglia infarcts): produce parkinsonism, dystonia, akinetic mutism, or cognitive blunting depending on extent and laterality.
- **Hemiballism** is more typical of [[subthalamic-nucleus]] than pallidal lesions, but bilateral pallidal lesions can produce profound apathy, abulia, and frontal disinhibition through interruption of associative and limbic loops.
- **Pantothenate kinase-associated neurodegeneration (PKAN)** produces the classic "**eye of the tiger**" sign on T2 — central hyperintensity within a hypointense GPi from iron accumulation.

## Anatomic Pitfalls in DBS

- **Medial deviation** → corticospinal capture in the [[internal-capsule]]; tonic contraction at low amplitudes; reposition laterally.
- **Inferior deviation** → optic tract phosphenes; reposition superiorly.
- **Posterior deviation** → ventral postero-lateral thalamus capture; paresthesias.
- **Anterior or superior deviation** → suboptimal therapeutic window; reposition into the postero-ventro-lateral target.
- **Excessive lateral deviation** → reaches the putamen, which is GABAergic but with fundamentally different physiology; ineffective stimulation.

## Surgical Pearls

1. **Direct visualization beats indirect coordinates.** On 3 T T2 / SWI, the medial medullary lamina between GPe and GPi is reliably identifiable; the postero-ventro-lateral GPi target is best chosen on imaging rather than from the AC-PC formula.
2. **The optic tract is the floor of the GPi target.** Macrostimulation phosphenes are not a complication — they are a confirmatory landmark indicating you are at the inferior border of the GPi.
3. **Counsel realistically for dystonia.** Benefit is delayed; families need to understand that the absence of immediate improvement after lead placement is expected and not a failure.
4. **Choose GPi over STN when cognition or mood is fragile.** STN-DBS confers larger levodopa reduction but a higher rate of cognitive and behavioral side effects; in patients on the edge, GPi is the safer choice.
5. **Anticipate higher battery drain.** GPi-DBS amplitude and pulse-width requirements outpace STN; advise patients of more frequent battery changes (or recommend rechargeable systems).
6. **Beware lenticulostriate territory in trajectory planning.** A trajectory that crosses a sulcus or visible cortical vein invites microhemorrhage; pallidal targeting is no more forgiving than subthalamic.
